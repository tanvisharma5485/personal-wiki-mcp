# Exoplanets

## Key Points

* Exoplanets are planets orbiting stars beyond the Solar System and reveal planetary systems with sizes, compositions, and architectures unlike those found locally.
* Major detection methods include transits, radial velocity, direct imaging, gravitational microlensing, astrometry, and timing techniques, each with different biases.
* Atmospheric characterization, mass-radius measurements, host-star properties, and orbital context are essential when assessing composition and habitability.

## Overview

An exoplanet is a planet that orbits a star outside our Solar System.

The word comes from:

Extra + solar + planet

meaning a planet beyond the Solar System.

Thousands of exoplanets have been confirmed, and many more candidates have been identified.

Exoplanet research helps scientists understand:

* How planets form

* How planetary systems evolve

* How common Earth-like worlds may be

* How diverse planets can become

* Whether habitable environments may exist elsewhere

* Whether life could exist beyond Earth

## Basic Definition

An exoplanet is a planetary-mass object orbiting a star other than the Sun.

Most known exoplanets orbit stars within the Milky Way Galaxy.

Some planets may not orbit any star and are called:

* Rogue planets

* Free-floating planets

These objects are related to planetary science but are not always included under the strictest definition of exoplanet.

## First Exoplanet Discoveries

The first confirmed planets outside the Solar System were discovered around a pulsar.

In 1992, planets were confirmed around:

PSR B1257+12

This demonstrated that planetary systems could exist outside the Solar System.

## First Exoplanet Around a Sun-Like Star

In 1995, astronomers announced the discovery of:

51 Pegasi b

It was the first confirmed exoplanet orbiting a normal Sun-like main-sequence star.

Its discovery transformed modern planetary astronomy.

## 51 Pegasi b

51 Pegasi b is a gas giant orbiting extremely close to its star.

It completes an orbit in only a few days.

It became the classic example of a:

Hot Jupiter

Its existence surprised scientists because giant planets in the Solar System orbit far from the Sun.

## Why Exoplanets Are Difficult to Detect

Stars are:

* Very bright

* Much larger than planets

Planets are:

* Much smaller

* Much dimmer

* Extremely close to their stars in apparent angular separation

For this reason, most exoplanets are not discovered by directly photographing them.

Instead, astronomers detect their effects on their host stars.

## Major Detection Methods

Important exoplanet detection methods include:

* Transit method

* Radial velocity method

* Direct imaging

* Gravitational microlensing

* Astrometry

* Transit timing variations

* Pulsar timing

## Transit Method

The transit method detects a planet when it passes in front of its star from Earth's point of view.

During a transit, the planet blocks a small amount of starlight.

This causes a temporary decrease in the star's observed brightness.

## Transit Light Curve

A graph of stellar brightness over time is called a:

Light curve

When a planet transits:

Brightness decreases

Then:

Brightness returns to normal

Repeated periodic dips can indicate an orbiting planet.

## Transit Depth

Transit depth depends approximately on the ratio between the planet's radius and the star's radius.

A simplified relationship is:

Transit depth ≈ (Planet radius / Star radius)²

Therefore, larger planets block more light.

## What Transit Observations Can Reveal

Transit observations can provide information about:

* Planet radius

* Orbital period

* Orbital inclination

* Atmospheric composition in some cases

* Presence of additional planets

## Limitations of Transit Method

A transit is observable only if the planetary orbit is aligned appropriately with Earth.

Most planetary systems are not oriented so that every planet transits from our viewpoint.

Therefore, the transit method detects only a subset of all planets.

## Kepler Space Telescope

NASA's Kepler mission revolutionized exoplanet astronomy.

It monitored the brightness of large numbers of stars to search for planetary transits.

Kepler demonstrated that planets are extremely common in the Milky Way.

## Kepler's Importance

Kepler discovered:

* Thousands of exoplanet candidates

* Multi-planet systems

* Small rocky planets

* Planets near habitable zones

Its results showed that planetary systems are far more diverse than the Solar System alone suggested.

## K2 Mission

After problems with its reaction wheels, Kepler continued operating in a modified mission called:

K2

K2 observed different regions along the ecliptic and continued discovering planets and studying stars.

## TESS

TESS stands for:

Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite

It searches for exoplanets around relatively nearby and bright stars.

TESS is especially useful for finding planets suitable for follow-up observations.

## TESS Detection Strategy

TESS monitors large regions of the sky for periodic decreases in stellar brightness.

Its targets include:

* Small planets

* Super-Earths

* Sub-Neptunes

* Hot Jupiters

* Multi-planet systems

## Radial Velocity Method

The radial velocity method detects the motion of a star caused by the gravity of an orbiting planet.

A planet and star both orbit their common center of mass.

The star therefore moves slightly toward and away from Earth.

## Doppler Effect

Radial velocity measurements use the Doppler effect.

When the star moves toward Earth:

Spectral lines shift toward shorter wavelengths.

This is called:

Blueshift

When the star moves away:

Spectral lines shift toward longer wavelengths.

This is called:

Redshift

## Stellar Wobble

The repeated redshift and blueshift of a star reveals its gravitational wobble.

This can indicate the presence of an orbiting planet.

## What Radial Velocity Reveals

Radial velocity measurements can estimate:

* Orbital period

* Orbital eccentricity

* Minimum planet mass

When combined with transit measurements, scientists can often determine the planet's true mass and density.

## Minimum Mass

Radial velocity usually measures:

M sin i

where:

M = planet mass

i = orbital inclination

If the inclination is unknown, the derived value is a minimum mass.

## Transit Plus Radial Velocity

Transit provides:

Planet radius

Radial velocity provides:

Planet mass

Combining these allows calculation of:

Density

Density is extremely important for determining whether a planet is:

* Rocky

* Water-rich

* Gas-rich

## Planet Density

Density is calculated approximately as:

Density = Mass / Volume

A high density may indicate a rocky or metal-rich planet.

A low density may indicate:

* Thick atmosphere

* Hydrogen-helium envelope

* Ice-rich composition

## Direct Imaging

Direct imaging attempts to capture actual light from an exoplanet.

This is difficult because the planet lies close to a much brighter star.

Direct imaging is generally easiest for:

* Young planets

* Massive planets

* Planets far from their stars

## Coronagraph

A coronagraph blocks much of a star's light.

This can make nearby faint planets easier to detect.

## Starshade

A starshade is a proposed or developing external spacecraft concept designed to block starlight before it enters a telescope.

It could potentially improve direct imaging of faint planets.

## Adaptive Optics

Ground-based telescopes use adaptive optics to correct atmospheric distortion.

This greatly improves high-resolution imaging.

It is important for direct imaging of exoplanets.

## Directly Imaged Planets

Direct imaging provides information about:

* Planet brightness

* Temperature

* Atmospheric composition

* Orbital motion

Young giant planets are easier to image because they still radiate substantial heat.

## Gravitational Microlensing

Gravitational microlensing occurs when gravity from a foreground star bends and magnifies light from a background star.

If the foreground star has a planet, the planet can produce an additional temporary magnification signal.

## Einstein's Gravity and Microlensing

General relativity predicts that mass bends spacetime.

Light passing near a massive object follows the curved spacetime geometry.

This can produce gravitational lensing.

## Advantages of Microlensing

Microlensing can detect:

* Low-mass planets

* Planets far from their stars

* Distant planetary systems

* Some free-floating planets

## Limitations of Microlensing

Microlensing events are usually:

* Temporary

* Non-repeating

This makes follow-up observations more difficult.

## Astrometry

Astrometry measures the precise position of a star in the sky.

An orbiting planet can cause a tiny periodic positional movement of its host star.

This method is called:

Astrometric detection

## Astrometry Advantages

Astrometry can be particularly useful for:

* Massive planets

* Wide orbits

It can also help determine orbital inclination and true planetary mass.

## Gaia and Exoplanets

ESA's Gaia mission makes extremely precise measurements of stellar positions and motions.

These measurements can reveal astrometric signatures of planetary companions.

## Pulsar Timing

Pulsars are rapidly rotating neutron stars.

They emit extremely regular pulses.

An orbiting planet causes tiny changes in the arrival times of these pulses.

These timing variations can reveal planets.

## Pulsar Planets

The first confirmed exoplanets were discovered using pulsar timing.

Their existence demonstrated that planets can occur around very different types of stellar remnants.

## Transit Timing Variations

In multi-planet systems, planets gravitationally influence each other.

This can cause transits to occur slightly earlier or later than expected.

These variations are called:

Transit Timing Variations

or:

TTVs

## Why TTVs Matter

TTVs can reveal:

* Additional planets

* Planet masses

* Orbital interactions

* Resonances

They are particularly useful in compact multi-planet systems.

## Exoplanet Naming

Exoplanets are usually named after their host stars.

The first discovered planet receives the lowercase letter:

b

The next usually receives:

c

Then:

d

and so on.

Example:

Kepler-90 b

Kepler-90 c

Kepler-90 d

The star itself is conceptually associated with the letter a, though it is usually not written that way.

## Types of Exoplanets

Exoplanets show enormous diversity.

Common categories include:

* Rocky planets

* Super-Earths

* Sub-Neptunes

* Mini-Neptunes

* Gas giants

* Hot Jupiters

* Hot Neptunes

* Ocean-world candidates

* Lava planets

* Ultra-short-period planets

* Circumbinary planets

## Rocky Planets

Rocky exoplanets are dominated by:

* Silicate rock

* Metal

They are broadly analogous to terrestrial planets such as:

* Mercury

* Venus

* Earth

* Mars

## Terrestrial Exoplanets

A terrestrial exoplanet is generally a rocky planet.

Its surface may consist largely of:

* Silicate minerals

* Metal

* Volcanic materials

However, having an Earth-like size does not automatically mean the planet is Earth-like in environment.

## Super-Earth

A super-Earth is a planet more massive or larger than Earth but significantly smaller than giant planets such as Uranus and Neptune.

The term refers primarily to size or mass.

It does not mean:

More habitable than Earth

## Super-Earth Size Range

There is no single universal boundary.

Many studies use values roughly between:

Earth-sized

and:

Several Earth masses or approximately 1–2 Earth radii

depending on classification method.

## Sub-Neptune

Sub-Neptunes are planets smaller than Neptune but often larger than typical rocky planets.

Many possess substantial:

Hydrogen-helium atmospheres

They are among the most common exoplanet types detected.

## Mini-Neptune

Mini-Neptune is another commonly used term for planets smaller than Neptune but with significant gaseous envelopes.

Terminology overlaps with:

Sub-Neptune

## Radius Gap

Observations reveal a relative shortage of planets at certain sizes between many super-Earths and sub-Neptunes.

This is often called the:

Radius valley

or:

Fulton gap

It may result from atmospheric loss and planetary evolution.

## Photoevaporation

High-energy radiation from a star can heat a planet's upper atmosphere.

Gas may then escape into space.

This process is called:

Photoevaporation

It may strip some planets of hydrogen-helium envelopes.

## Core-Powered Mass Loss

Another proposed mechanism for atmospheric loss is:

Core-powered mass loss

Heat remaining from planetary formation can contribute to atmospheric escape.

Both this mechanism and photoevaporation may help explain the radius gap.

## Gas Giant

Gas giants are large planets dominated by hydrogen and helium.

Examples in the Solar System are:

* Jupiter

* Saturn

Exoplanetary gas giants can orbit at many different distances from their stars.

## Hot Jupiter

A hot Jupiter is a Jupiter-sized gas giant orbiting extremely close to its host star.

Typical characteristics include:

* High temperatures

* Short orbital periods

* Strong stellar irradiation

## Why Hot Jupiters Were Surprising

Classical Solar System-based ideas suggested giant planets should form far from their stars where volatile materials are abundant.

Hot Jupiters demonstrated that planets can migrate substantially after formation.

## Planetary Migration

Planetary migration is the change in a planet's orbital distance after formation.

Possible mechanisms include interaction with:

* Protoplanetary disk

* Other planets

* Gravitational scattering

Migration explains many unusual exoplanet architectures.

## Hot Neptune

A hot Neptune is a Neptune-sized planet orbiting close to its star.

These worlds experience intense stellar radiation.

## Hot Neptune Desert

Astronomers observe relatively few Neptune-sized planets at extremely short orbital periods.

This region is called the:

Hot Neptune desert

Atmospheric escape and migration may contribute to it.

## Ultra-Short-Period Planets

Ultra-short-period planets have orbital periods shorter than approximately:

1 day

Many are rocky.

Their surfaces can reach extremely high temperatures.

## Lava Planets

Some rocky exoplanets orbit so close to their stars that surface temperatures can melt rock.

These are sometimes called:

Lava planets

Possible features include:

* Molten rock oceans

* Rock vapor atmospheres

* Extreme temperature differences

## Ocean Worlds

Some exoplanets may contain large quantities of water.

Hypothetical or candidate ocean planets could have:

* Deep global oceans

* High-pressure ice beneath oceans

* Water-rich interiors

Direct confirmation is difficult.

## Water Worlds

Water worlds are planets thought to contain much more water than Earth.

They need not necessarily have exposed liquid oceans at their surfaces.

## Carbon Planets

Some theoretical planetary compositions may contain unusually high fractions of carbon-rich material.

These hypothetical bodies are sometimes called:

Carbon planets

Their actual abundance remains uncertain.

## Iron-Rich Planets

Some exoplanets may have unusually large metallic cores.

Possible causes include:

* Formation conditions

* Giant impacts

* Mantle stripping

Mercury provides a Solar System example of a planet with a relatively large iron core.

## Puffy Planets

Some low-density exoplanets have very large radii compared with their masses.

These are sometimes called:

Puffy planets

or:

Super-puffs

They can possess extended atmospheres.

## Super-Puffs

Super-puffs have exceptionally low densities.

Some may have:

* Large hydrogen-helium atmospheres

* High-altitude hazes

* Dusty atmospheric structures

## Circumbinary Planet

A circumbinary planet orbits two stars rather than one.

The stars themselves orbit each other.

The planet orbits the binary pair.

## Tatooine Planets

Circumbinary planets are sometimes informally called:

Tatooine planets

after the fictional planet orbiting two suns in Star Wars.

This is not a formal scientific term.

## Multi-Planet Systems

Many stars host multiple planets.

These systems can contain:

* Compact planet chains

* Orbital resonances

* Rocky inner planets

* Outer giant planets

## Compact Systems

Some planetary systems fit several planets inside an orbital region smaller than Mercury's orbit around the Sun.

Such tightly packed systems have no close equivalent among the major planets of our Solar System.

## TRAPPIST-1

TRAPPIST-1 is an ultracool red dwarf star hosting seven known roughly Earth-sized planets.

Several orbit in or near the star's habitable-zone region.

The system is famous because:

* Planets are approximately Earth-sized

* Orbits are compact

* Several planets are dynamically resonant

## TRAPPIST-1 Resonances

The planets form a chain of orbital relationships.

These resonances provide evidence about:

* Planet formation

* Migration

* Long-term system stability

## Kepler-90

Kepler-90 is a planetary system known for hosting numerous planets.

Its discovery demonstrated that stars can possess planetary systems with many members.

## Kepler-11

Kepler-11 contains several planets orbiting very close to their star.

Its compact architecture provided important information about multi-planet dynamics.

## Proxima Centauri

Proxima Centauri is the closest known star to the Sun.

It hosts at least one confirmed exoplanet:

Proxima Centauri b

## Proxima Centauri b

Proxima Centauri b has a minimum mass broadly comparable to Earth.

It orbits within the nominal habitable-zone region of its red dwarf host.

However, habitability is uncertain because Proxima Centauri is magnetically active.

## Alpha Centauri System

Proxima Centauri is part of the wider Alpha Centauri stellar system.

Because of its proximity, planets in this system are important targets for future research.

## Habitable Zone

The habitable zone is the range of distances around a star where a rocky planet with suitable atmospheric conditions could potentially maintain liquid water on its surface.

It is sometimes called:

Goldilocks zone

## Habitable Does Not Mean Inhabited

A planet in the habitable zone is not necessarily:

* Habitable

* Earth-like

* Inhabited

It only satisfies one important orbital condition related to possible surface liquid water.

## Factors Affecting Habitability

Habitability may depend on:

* Stellar radiation

* Planet mass

* Atmosphere

* Atmospheric pressure

* Greenhouse effect

* Magnetic environment

* Water availability

* Surface temperature

* Geological activity

* Orbital stability

* Stellar activity

## Inner Edge of Habitable Zone

Too close to the star, a planet may become too warm.

Possible processes include:

* Strong greenhouse warming

* Water evaporation

* Moist greenhouse

* Runaway greenhouse

## Outer Edge of Habitable Zone

Farther from the star, temperatures may become too low for stable surface liquid water.

Greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide can extend the habitable region outward.

## Conservative and Optimistic Habitable Zones

Scientists use different models.

A conservative habitable zone uses stricter assumptions.

An optimistic habitable zone can include wider limits based partly on possible past conditions on Venus and Mars.

## Runaway Greenhouse

A runaway greenhouse can occur when warming causes increased atmospheric water vapor, which strengthens greenhouse heating.

Under extreme conditions, surface oceans may evaporate.

Venus is often studied in relation to runaway greenhouse evolution.

## Red Dwarf Habitable Zones

Red dwarfs are much cooler and dimmer than the Sun.

Their habitable zones lie close to the star.

Planets there can experience:

* Strong stellar flares

* Intense ultraviolet and X-ray radiation

* Powerful stellar winds

* Tidal effects

## Red Dwarf Stars

Red dwarfs are also called:

M dwarfs

They are:

* Small

* Cool

* Very long-lived

* Extremely common

Their abundance makes them major targets in exoplanet searches.

## Tidal Locking

A planet orbiting close to its star may become tidally locked.

One side then remains approximately facing the star.

The opposite side remains approximately facing away.

## Is a Tidally Locked Planet Uninhabitable?

Not necessarily.

Atmospheric and ocean circulation could transport heat from the day side to the night side.

Habitability depends on many factors.

## Terminator Zone

The boundary between the permanent day side and night side of a tidally locked planet is called the:

Terminator

Some studies investigate whether temperate conditions might exist near this region.

## Stellar Flares

Flares release intense bursts of radiation.

Frequent strong flares can affect exoplanet:

* Atmospheres

* Surface radiation

* Chemistry

* Habitability

## Stellar Wind

Stars produce streams of charged particles called:

Stellar winds

The solar wind is the Sun's stellar wind.

Strong stellar winds can influence exoplanet atmospheres.

## Atmospheric Escape

Exoplanets can lose atmospheric gases through:

* Thermal escape

* Photoevaporation

* Stellar-wind interaction

* Ion pickup

* Hydrodynamic escape

Atmospheric evolution strongly affects long-term habitability.

## Hydrodynamic Escape

When intense high-energy radiation heats an upper atmosphere, gas can flow outward in bulk.

This process is called:

Hydrodynamic escape

It was probably important for many young planets.

## Exoplanet Atmospheres

Scientists can study some exoplanet atmospheres even though the planets cannot be spatially resolved.

Important techniques include:

* Transmission spectroscopy

* Emission spectroscopy

* Phase curves

* Direct spectroscopy

## Transmission Spectroscopy

During a transit, a small amount of starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere.

Atmospheric molecules absorb specific wavelengths.

By examining these absorption features, scientists can infer atmospheric composition.

## Possible Atmospheric Molecules

Exoplanet spectroscopy can search for molecules such as:

* Water vapor

* Carbon dioxide

* Carbon monoxide

* Methane

* Sodium

* Potassium

The detectable molecules depend on atmospheric conditions and instrument sensitivity.

## Emission Spectroscopy

When a planet passes behind its star, scientists can compare the system's brightness before and during the event.

The difference can reveal light emitted or reflected by the planet.

This event is called a:

Secondary eclipse

## Secondary Eclipse

A secondary eclipse occurs when an exoplanet passes behind its star.

For hot planets, this helps determine:

* Temperature

* Atmospheric emission

* Day-side spectrum

## Phase Curve

As a planet orbits its star, different portions of its illuminated hemisphere become visible.

Changes in total observed brightness create a:

Phase curve

Phase curves can reveal:

* Day-night temperature contrast

* Atmospheric circulation

* Cloud distribution

* Reflectivity

## Hot Jupiter Atmospheres

Hot Jupiters can have atmospheric temperatures exceeding:

1,000 K

Some are much hotter.

Their atmospheres may contain:

* Water vapor

* Carbon monoxide

* Metals

* Clouds

* Hazes

## Ultra-Hot Jupiters

Ultra-hot Jupiters receive extreme stellar radiation.

Their day-side temperatures can become high enough to:

* Dissociate molecules

* Vaporize metals

* Ionize atmospheric species

## Temperature Inversion

Some exoplanet atmospheres can become warmer at higher altitude.

This structure is called a:

Temperature inversion

The phenomenon depends on atmospheric absorbers and stellar irradiation.

## Exoplanet Clouds

Clouds and hazes can strongly influence exoplanet spectra.

Possible cloud materials include:

* Water

* Silicates

* Metal compounds

* Hydrocarbons

depending on temperature.

## Silicate Clouds

On very hot planets, minerals that form rocks on Earth can vaporize and later condense as cloud particles.

Some giant exoplanets may therefore contain:

Silicate clouds

## Atmospheric Hazes

Photochemical reactions can create high-altitude hazes.

These can obscure molecular absorption features.

Titan in our Solar System provides a useful example of a haze-rich atmosphere.

## Biosignature

A biosignature is an observable feature that could potentially indicate biological activity.

Possible atmospheric biosignatures include particular combinations of gases.

However, no single molecule automatically proves the existence of life.

## Oxygen as Biosignature

Oxygen can be associated with life on Earth.

However, oxygen may also be generated through non-biological processes.

Therefore, detecting oxygen alone would not prove life.

## Methane as Biosignature

Methane may be produced biologically or geologically.

Its interpretation depends on:

* Atmospheric composition

* Stellar environment

* Geological context

## Disequilibrium Biosignatures

Life can maintain combinations of gases that would otherwise chemically react and disappear.

Atmospheric chemical disequilibrium may therefore provide stronger evidence than a single gas.

## False Positives

A false positive occurs when a non-biological process creates an apparent biosignature.

Scientists must distinguish biological explanations from:

* Photochemistry

* Volcanism

* Atmospheric escape

* Water loss

* Surface chemistry

## Technosignature

A technosignature is possible evidence of advanced technology.

Proposed examples include:

* Artificial radio signals

* Laser signals

* Industrial atmospheric chemicals

* Unusual artificial illumination

No confirmed extraterrestrial technosignature has been discovered.

## James Webb Space Telescope

JWST is a major tool for exoplanet atmospheric research.

It can observe:

* Transits

* Secondary eclipses

* Atmospheric spectra

particularly in infrared wavelengths.

## What JWST Can Study

JWST can investigate:

* Atmospheric composition

* Temperature

* Clouds

* Hazes

* Molecular absorption

for selected exoplanets.

## JWST Limitations

JWST cannot simply take detailed surface photographs of Earth-like exoplanets.

Most exoplanet information remains indirect.

Interpretation requires sophisticated models.

## Ground-Based Telescopes

Large ground-based telescopes contribute to exoplanet research through:

* Radial velocity

* Direct imaging

* High-resolution spectroscopy

* Transit observations

## Extremely Large Telescopes

Next-generation extremely large ground-based telescopes are designed to improve:

* Direct imaging

* Atmospheric spectroscopy

* Planet characterization

## Orbital Period

The orbital period is the time required for a planet to complete one orbit around its star.

Close planets may orbit in:

Hours or days

Distant planets may require:

Years or decades

## Kepler's Third Law

Planetary orbital period and distance are related through Kepler's third law.

For planets orbiting the same star:

P² ∝ a³

where:

P = orbital period

a = semimajor axis

## Orbital Eccentricity

Many exoplanets have eccentric orbits.

Some are much more elongated than most major Solar System planets.

High eccentricity produces large changes in stellar heating during an orbit.

## Orbital Inclination

Inclination describes the orientation of the orbital plane.

Transit detections favor systems viewed nearly edge-on.

This creates observational bias.

## Orbital Resonance

Two planets are in orbital resonance when their orbital periods form a simple numerical relationship.

Examples include:

2:1

3:2

Resonances can influence long-term stability.

## Resonant Chains

Some compact systems contain several planets linked through resonances.

TRAPPIST-1 is a famous example.

## Planetary System Architecture

Planetary architecture describes how planets are arranged around a star.

It includes:

* Planet sizes

* Orbital distances

* Eccentricities

* Inclinations

* Resonances

Exoplanet systems show enormous architectural diversity.

## Hot Jupiter Migration

Hot Jupiters probably formed farther from their stars.

Possible migration mechanisms include:

* Disk migration

* Planet-planet scattering

* High-eccentricity migration

## Disk Migration

A young planet gravitationally interacts with the surrounding protoplanetary disk.

These interactions can change its orbit.

## Planet-Planet Scattering

Strong gravitational encounters between young planets can drastically change their orbits.

Possible outcomes include:

* Eccentric orbits

* Ejections

* Collisions

* Inward or outward migration

## High-Eccentricity Migration

A giant planet may first enter a highly eccentric orbit.

Tidal interactions near the star can then circularize and shrink its orbit.

This may produce some hot Jupiters.

## Spin-Orbit Misalignment

Some exoplanets orbit at large angles relative to the rotation axis of their stars.

Some even follow nearly retrograde orbits.

These configurations provide clues about planetary migration.

## Rossiter-McLaughlin Effect

During a transit, a planet blocks different rotating regions of the stellar surface.

This produces a temporary distortion in the star's measured radial velocity.

The effect can reveal the alignment between:

* Stellar rotation

* Planet orbit

## Planet Formation

Planets form in disks of gas and dust surrounding young stars.

These are called:

Protoplanetary disks

## Protoplanetary Disk

A protoplanetary disk contains:

* Gas

* Dust

* Ice

* Organic compounds

Dust particles collide and grow into larger bodies.

## Planet Formation Sequence

A simplified sequence is:

Dust grains

→

Pebbles

→

Planetesimals

→

Planetary embryos

→

Planets

## Core Accretion

Core accretion is the leading model for formation of many planets.

A solid core forms first.

If it becomes sufficiently massive before the gas disk disappears, it can capture large quantities of hydrogen and helium.

This can create a giant planet.

## Disk Instability

Another proposed giant-planet formation mechanism is:

Disk instability

A massive region of the protoplanetary disk may gravitationally collapse directly.

This process may produce giant planets under particular conditions.

## Snow Line

The snow line is the distance from a young star beyond which temperatures are low enough for volatile compounds such as water to freeze.

Beyond the snow line, more solid material may be available for planet formation.

## Metallicity

In astronomy, metallicity refers to the abundance of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium.

Stars with higher metallicity have historically been found to be more likely to host giant planets.

The relationship for small planets is different and less strongly dependent on high metallicity.

## Host Star Types

Exoplanets have been discovered around many types of stars, including:

* Sun-like stars

* Red dwarfs

* Giant stars

* Binary stars

* Pulsars

## Binary Star Planets

Planets in binary systems can have different orbital configurations.

A planet can orbit:

One star in the pair

or:

Both stars

## S-Type Orbit

In a binary system, an S-type planet orbits one star while the second star remains farther away.

## P-Type Orbit

A P-type planet orbits both stars.

This is a:

Circumbinary orbit

## Planets Around White Dwarfs

Planetary debris and some planetary-mass companions have been observed around white dwarfs.

These systems reveal what may happen to planetary systems after Sun-like stars evolve.

## Pulsar Planets

Pulsar planets demonstrate that planetary bodies can orbit neutron stars.

Some may form after the supernova event or survive complex stellar evolution.

## Rogue Planets

Rogue planets travel through space without being gravitationally bound to a star.

Possible origins include:

* Ejection from planetary systems

* Direct formation from collapsing material

Their total population remains uncertain.

## How Rogue Planets Are Detected

Gravitational microlensing is an important method for detecting free-floating planetary-mass objects.

Because they have no bright host star, ordinary transit detection cannot be used.

## Planet Radius

Planet radius is commonly measured in:

Earth radii

or:

Jupiter radii

Symbol examples include:

R?

R\_J

## Planet Mass

Planet mass is commonly measured in:

Earth masses

or:

Jupiter masses

Examples:

M?

M\_J

## Equilibrium Temperature

A planet's equilibrium temperature estimates its temperature based on:

* Stellar luminosity

* Orbital distance

* Reflectivity

It does not fully account for atmospheric greenhouse effects.

## Albedo

Albedo is the fraction of incoming light reflected by a planet.

High-albedo planets reflect more stellar energy.

Low-albedo planets absorb more.

## Bond Albedo

Bond albedo measures the fraction of total incident electromagnetic energy reflected in all directions and wavelengths.

It is useful in planetary energy-balance calculations.

## Greenhouse Effect

Atmospheric gases can absorb outgoing infrared radiation and warm a planet's surface.

The greenhouse effect is essential for Earth's climate.

Too much greenhouse warming can produce extreme conditions.

## Exoplanet Weather

Some exoplanets may experience extremely intense weather.

Possible phenomena include:

* Supersonic winds

* Silicate clouds

* Metal rain

* Extreme storms

* Huge day-night temperature differences

## Hot Jupiter Winds

Hot Jupiters are expected to develop strong atmospheric circulation because one hemisphere receives intense stellar heating.

Wind speeds can reach kilometers per second in some atmospheric models and measurements.

## Day-Night Heat Redistribution

Atmospheres can transport heat from a planet's day side to night side.

The efficiency depends on:

* Atmospheric composition

* Pressure

* Wind speed

* Rotation

* Stellar heating

## Exoplanet Magnetic Fields

Detecting exoplanet magnetic fields is difficult.

Possible methods include searching for:

* Radio emission

* Star-planet interactions

* Atmospheric escape signatures

Magnetic fields may influence atmospheric evolution.

## Star-Planet Interaction

Close-in planets can interact with their stars through:

* Gravity

* Magnetic fields

* Stellar wind

These interactions may affect both planet and star.

## Tidal Forces

Strong tidal forces act on planets orbiting close to their stars.

They can cause:

* Tidal locking

* Internal heating

* Orbital circularization

* Orbital decay

## Tidal Heating

Changing gravitational stresses can generate internal heat.

The Solar System moon Io provides a dramatic example.

Some exoplanets may also experience intense tidal heating.

## Exomoons

An exomoon is a moon orbiting an exoplanet.

Scientists have searched for exomoons using:

* Transit timing

* Transit shape

* Direct observations

No universally accepted large population of confirmed exomoons has yet been established.

## Why Exomoons Matter

Exomoons may:

* Reveal planetary formation

* Influence planet dynamics

* Potentially provide habitable environments

Large moons around giant planets are especially interesting.

## Exorings

Some exoplanets may possess ring systems.

Very large ring structures around young objects have been proposed from unusual transit signals.

Confirmed detailed exoring systems remain difficult to establish.

## Exocomets

Comets around other stars are called:

Exocomets

They can sometimes be detected when gas or dust passes in front of a star.

## Debris Disks

Debris disks contain dust produced by collisions among:

* Asteroids

* Comets

* Planetesimals

They are analogous in some ways to:

* Asteroid Belt

* Kuiper Belt

Observations of debris disks provide information about planetary-system architecture.

## Protoplanetary Disks Versus Debris Disks

Protoplanetary disk:

Young, gas-rich planet-forming disk.

Debris disk:

Older disk dominated by dust produced by collisions among solid bodies.

## Direct Observation of Planet Formation

Modern telescopes can image protoplanetary disks containing:

* Rings

* Gaps

* Spirals

Some of these structures may be produced by forming planets.

## Migration Evidence

Exoplanet populations provide strong evidence that planetary migration is common.

Examples include:

* Hot Jupiters

* Resonant planet chains

* Compact planetary systems

## Solar System Comparison

The Solar System contains:

* Four terrestrial planets

* Four giant planets

* Large empty orbital gaps by comparison with some compact systems

Many exoplanet systems look very different.

## No Super-Earth in Solar System

The Solar System has no planet between Earth and Neptune in size.

However, super-Earths and sub-Neptunes are very common among known exoplanets.

This is one of the major contrasts between our Solar System and many other systems.

## Hot Jupiter Absence in Solar System

The Solar System contains no gas giant extremely close to the Sun.

Jupiter orbits at approximately:

5.2 AU

Hot Jupiters often orbit in only a few days.

## Detection Bias

Known exoplanet populations do not perfectly represent the true planetary population.

Detection methods favor certain planets.

Transit surveys favor:

* Large planets

* Close-in planets

* Short-period planets

Radial velocity also favors:

* Massive planets

* Close-in planets

Scientists correct statistically for these biases.

## Why Short-Period Planets Are Easier to Find

Short-period planets transit more frequently.

This allows repeated observations over a shorter time.

They also often produce stronger radial-velocity signals.

## Why Large Planets Are Easier to Find

Large planets:

* Block more starlight

* Produce stronger gravitational effects

* Are easier to image when young and bright

Therefore, early exoplanet discoveries were dominated by giant planets.

## Earth Analog

An Earth analog would broadly resemble Earth in important properties such as:

* Size

* Mass

* Rocky composition

* Stellar irradiation

* Orbital environment

Finding a true Earth analog is much more difficult than finding a close-in giant planet.

## Earth Twin

An Earth twin is an even stricter concept.

It would potentially have:

* Similar size

* Similar mass

* Similar atmosphere

* Similar temperature

* Similar star

* Similar orbital distance

No confirmed exact Earth twin is known.

## Earth-Like Does Not Mean Habitable

The phrase Earth-like may refer only to:

* Radius

* Mass

* Equilibrium temperature

It should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of oceans, atmosphere, or life.

## Goldilocks Planet

The term Goldilocks planet is an informal description for a planet with conditions considered neither too hot nor too cold for liquid water.

The scientific concept is generally:

Habitable-zone planet

## Habitable Zone Around Different Stars

The habitable zone is farther from:

Hot luminous stars

and closer to:

Cool faint stars

Therefore, there is no universal habitable-zone distance.

## Main Sequence Stars

Main-sequence stars generate energy primarily through hydrogen fusion in their cores.

Most exoplanet habitability studies focus strongly on main-sequence hosts.

## Stellar Lifetime

Long-lived stars provide more time for planetary environments and potentially biological evolution.

Very massive stars live much shorter lives than stars like the Sun.

## Red Dwarf Longevity

Red dwarfs can remain on the main sequence for:

Trillions of years

in theory.

No red dwarf has yet reached the end of its main-sequence lifetime because the Universe is only about 13.8 billion years old.

## Stellar Activity and Habitability

Long lifetime alone does not guarantee favorable conditions.

Young red dwarfs can produce strong:

* Flares

* X-rays

* Ultraviolet radiation

* Stellar winds

## Planetary Protection

Potential protection mechanisms include:

* Thick atmosphere

* Magnetic field

* Ocean

* Surface shielding

* Atmospheric chemistry

No single factor determines habitability.

## Water Detection

Astronomers can search for water vapor in exoplanet atmospheres.

Detection of water vapor does not necessarily mean liquid surface oceans exist.

## Liquid Water

Liquid surface water depends on:

* Pressure

* Temperature

* Atmospheric composition

A planet can contain water vapor or ice without having surface oceans.

## Carbon Dioxide

Carbon dioxide is an important greenhouse gas.

Its presence can strongly affect planetary climate.

It is also produced by both geological and biological processes.

## Methane

Methane is an important atmospheric molecule.

It can be produced by:

* Biology

* Volcanism

* Hydrothermal chemistry

* Photochemical processes

Context is essential when interpreting it.

## Oxygen and Ozone

Oxygen and ozone are important biosignature candidates.

However, abiotic processes can sometimes generate them.

Scientists therefore search for combinations of gases and environmental context.

## Biosignature Context

A convincing biosignature assessment would consider:

* Host star spectrum

* Planet temperature

* Atmospheric pressure

* Multiple gases

* Geological processes

* Photochemical models

## No Confirmed Exoplanet Life

No life has been confirmed on any exoplanet.

No atmospheric detection currently constitutes universally accepted proof of extraterrestrial biology.

## SETI and Exoplanets

SETI searches for possible signs of extraterrestrial technology.

Known exoplanets provide specific stellar systems that can be targeted for:

* Radio observations

* Optical searches

## Drake Equation

The Drake equation is a framework for discussing the possible number of communicating extraterrestrial civilizations in the Galaxy.

Exoplanet discoveries help constrain some factors, especially:

How common planets are.

## Eta Earth

Eta Earth, often represented conceptually as ??, describes the frequency of Earth-size or roughly Earth-like planets around stars.

Its precise value depends strongly on definitions and observational completeness.

## Are Planets Common?

Modern exoplanet research strongly indicates that planets are common.

Many stars likely host planetary systems.

The Milky Way therefore probably contains:

Billions of planets

## How Many Exoplanets Exist?

Only a small fraction of Galactic planets are currently detectable.

The Milky Way contains hundreds of billions of stars.

Given the observed frequency of planets, the true number of planets is expected to be enormous.

## Planet Occurrence Rate

Occurrence rate measures how frequently stars host planets of particular:

* Sizes

* Orbital periods

* Stellar irradiation levels

This is a major statistical goal of exoplanet surveys.

## Exoplanet Confirmation

A detected signal is not automatically considered a confirmed planet.

Possible false positives include:

* Eclipsing binary stars

* Stellar variability

* Instrumental effects

* Background stars

Follow-up observations and statistical validation are used to confirm candidates.

## Planet Candidate

A planet candidate is an object or signal that may represent a planet but has not yet satisfied confirmation or validation criteria.

## False Positive

A false positive occurs when another phenomenon mimics a planetary signal.

For transit surveys, common examples include:

Eclipsing binary stars.

## Follow-Up Observations

Candidates may be followed with:

* Radial velocity

* Additional photometry

* High-resolution imaging

* Spectroscopy

* Astrometry

## Exoplanet Catalogs

Astronomers maintain databases containing information about:

* Confirmed planets

* Candidates

* Host stars

* Orbital parameters

* Planet properties

The exact number of confirmed exoplanets continually increases as discoveries are validated.

## Discovery Rate

Exoplanet discovery accelerated dramatically after:

* High-precision radial velocity surveys

* Kepler

* TESS

* Improved ground-based instruments

## Exoplanet Science Timeline

Important milestones include:

1992:

Confirmed pulsar planets

51 Pegasi b around a Sun-like star

Rapid growth of radial-velocity and transit detections

## Famous Exoplanets

Important examples include:

* 51 Pegasi b

* Proxima Centauri b

* TRAPPIST-1 planets

* Kepler-186f

* Kepler-452b

* HD 209458 b

* HD 189733 b

* WASP-12b

* K2-18 b

* Beta Pictoris b

* HR 8799 planets

## HD 209458 b

HD 209458 b was historically important because it was among the first transiting exoplanets studied in detail.

Its atmosphere also became one of the earliest exoplanet atmospheres to be detected.

## HD 189733 b

HD 189733 b is a hot Jupiter extensively studied through atmospheric observations.

Its atmosphere contains extreme weather and high-altitude hazes.

## WASP-12b

WASP-12b is an extremely hot gas giant orbiting very close to its star.

It experiences intense tidal and radiative effects.

## Beta Pictoris b

Beta Pictoris b is a directly imaged giant exoplanet orbiting a young star.

The system also contains a prominent debris disk.

## HR 8799

HR 8799 is famous for having multiple directly imaged giant planets.

It provides an important laboratory for studying young planetary systems.

## Kepler-186f

Kepler-186f was one of the first approximately Earth-sized planets found in the habitable zone of another star.

Its actual surface conditions remain unknown.

## Kepler-452b

Kepler-452b received attention because it orbits in the habitable-zone region of a Sun-like star.

However, its exact composition and habitability remain uncertain.

## K2-18 b

K2-18 b is a sub-Neptune-size planet whose atmosphere has been intensively studied.

It is important for understanding atmospheric chemistry on planets unlike those in the Solar System.

Its true surface and interior conditions remain uncertain.

## Hycean Worlds

The term Hycean describes a proposed class of planets with:

* Hydrogen-rich atmosphere

* Possible global ocean

This is a theoretical interpretation for some sub-Neptune planets.

Whether particular planets truly fit this category remains under investigation.

## Exoplanet Interior Models

Because scientists usually cannot observe interiors directly, they use:

* Mass

* Radius

* Atmospheric measurements

to model internal structure.

Possible layers include:

* Iron core

* Silicate mantle

* Water-rich layer

* Gas envelope

## Mass-Radius Relationship

Two planets with the same radius can have very different masses.

Therefore, radius alone cannot determine composition.

Mass and radius together provide much stronger constraints.

## Degeneracy Problem

Different internal compositions can produce similar mass and radius.

For example, a planet could have different combinations of:

* Rock

* Iron

* Water

* Gas

that produce similar bulk properties.

Atmospheric and stellar information helps reduce this uncertainty.

## Exoplanet Geology

Direct geological observations are not yet possible for most exoplanets.

Scientists infer possible geology through:

* Density

* Temperature

* Atmospheric gases

* Tidal heating

* Planet formation models

## Volcanism

Volcanism may influence exoplanet atmospheres by releasing gases such as:

* Water vapor

* Carbon dioxide

* Sulfur compounds

Detecting active volcanism remotely remains difficult.

## Plate Tectonics

Whether plate tectonics is common on rocky exoplanets remains unknown.

Possible controlling factors include:

* Planet size

* Internal heat

* Water

* Mantle properties

* Lithosphere strength

## Carbon Cycle

A long-term carbon cycle can help stabilize planetary climate.

Earth's carbonate-silicate cycle involves:

* Atmosphere

* Oceans

* Rocks

* Volcanism

* Plate tectonics

Whether similar cycles operate elsewhere is uncertain.

## Exoplanet Seasons

A planet's axial tilt can produce seasons.

Orbital eccentricity can also cause strong seasonal temperature variations.

## Obliquity

Obliquity is the angle between a planet's rotation axis and orbital axis.

Earth's obliquity is approximately:

23.4 degrees

Exoplanets may have very different tilts.

## Rotation Rate

Planet rotation influences:

* Atmospheric circulation

* Magnetic dynamo

* Weather

* Temperature distribution

Direct measurement of exoplanet rotation is difficult.

## Exoplanet Day Length

For close-in planets, tidal forces may synchronize rotation with orbital motion.

More distant worlds may rotate independently.

## Planetary Rings

Exoplanets could possess ring systems.

Large rings could influence transit light curves.

Detecting and confirming rings remains challenging.

## Exoplanetary Asteroid Belts

Debris disks suggest that many planetary systems contain populations analogous to:

* Asteroids

* Comets

* Kuiper Belt objects

## Exozodiacal Dust

Exozodiacal dust is dust in the inner regions of other planetary systems.

It is analogous to zodiacal dust in our Solar System.

This dust can make direct imaging of Earth-like exoplanets more difficult.

## Planetary Collisions

Giant impacts probably occur during planet formation.

They can alter:

* Planet mass

* Atmosphere

* Rotation

* Moon formation

* Internal composition

Earth's Moon likely formed following a giant impact.

Similar processes may occur in exoplanet systems.

## Planet Ejection

Gravitational interactions can eject planets from a system.

These bodies can become rogue planets.

## Stellar Evolution and Exoplanets

As stars evolve, their planets also change.

A star may become:

* Red giant

* White dwarf

* Neutron star

depending on its mass.

Planetary orbits and atmospheres can be dramatically altered.

## Red Giant Phase

When Sun-like stars become red giants, they expand enormously.

Close planets may:

* Be engulfed

* Heat dramatically

* Lose atmospheres

More distant planets may migrate outward as the star loses mass.

## White Dwarf Planetary Systems

After a Sun-like star becomes a white dwarf, surviving planets, asteroids, and debris may remain.

Material falling onto white dwarfs provides information about the composition of former planetary bodies.

## Exoplanets and the Future Sun

Studying planets around evolved stars helps predict the long-term future of the Solar System.

The Sun is expected eventually to become:

Red giant

then:

White dwarf

## Exoplanet Habitability Beyond Earth Analogs

Potentially habitable environments might not require exact Earth replicas.

Researchers consider:

* Tidally locked planets

* Ocean worlds

* Large moons

* Planets around red dwarfs

Habitability can exist under a wider range of conditions than simple Earth similarity suggests.

## Subsurface Habitability

A world outside the traditional habitable zone could potentially maintain liquid water underground through:

* Radioactive heating

* Tidal heating

Solar System moons such as Europa and Enceladus demonstrate this possibility.

## Exomoons and Habitability

A moon orbiting a giant exoplanet could potentially maintain habitable conditions.

Possible energy sources include:

* Starlight

* Planetary illumination

* Tidal heating

## Habitability Is Not Binary

Habitability exists on a spectrum.

Different parts of a planet may have very different environments.

A planet could be habitable:

* Temporarily

* Regionally

* Underground

without being globally Earth-like.

## Common Misconceptions

### Every Exoplanet Has Been Photographed

Incorrect.

Most are detected indirectly.

### Exoplanets Are Only Gas Giants

Incorrect.

Known exoplanets range from smaller rocky worlds to giant planets.

### Super-Earth Means Better Earth

Incorrect.

It is primarily a size or mass category.

### A Planet in the Habitable Zone Has Life

Incorrect.

Habitable-zone position alone does not demonstrate habitability or biology.

### Water Vapor Means Oceans

Incorrect.

Water vapor can exist without surface liquid water.

### Oxygen Automatically Proves Life

Incorrect.

Non-biological processes can produce oxygen.

### Earth-Sized Means Earth-Like

Incorrect.

An Earth-sized planet could have very different:

* Atmosphere

* Temperature

* Composition

* Surface

### All Planetary Systems Look Like the Solar System

Incorrect.

Observed planetary architectures are extremely diverse.

### Hot Jupiters Formed Exactly Where They Are Now

Many probably experienced substantial migration.

### Red Dwarf Planets Cannot Be Habitable

Their environments present major challenges, but habitability is not automatically impossible.

### Tidally Locked Planets Have One Frozen and One Burning Side With No Possibility of Habitability

Atmospheric and ocean circulation can redistribute heat.

Actual conditions depend on the planet.

### We Have Found Another Earth

No confirmed exact Earth twin with demonstrated Earth-like environmental conditions has been identified.

### Biosignature Detection Would Immediately Prove Life

No.

Multiple observations and elimination of non-biological explanations would be required.

Star orbited by an exoplanet.

Passage of a planet across its star's disk from the observer's viewpoint.

Movement toward or away from the observer measured through Doppler shifts.

Detection of light directly from an exoplanet.

### Microlensing

Temporary gravitational magnification used to detect planets.

### Astrometry

Precise measurement of stellar positions.

Planet larger or more massive than Earth but smaller than Neptune-like planets, depending on definition.

Planet smaller than Neptune that often retains a substantial gaseous envelope.

Gas giant orbiting extremely close to its star.

### Circumbinary Planet

Planet orbiting two stars.

### Rogue Planet

Planetary-mass object not orbiting a star.

Orbital region where suitable conditions could permit surface liquid water.

### Biosignature

Potentially observable sign of biological activity.

### Technosignature

Potentially observable sign of technological activity.

### Protoplanetary Disk

Gas-and-dust disk in which planets form.

Event when an exoplanet passes behind its star.

Variation in observed planetary light through its orbit.

### Exomoon

Moon orbiting an exoplanet.

## Exoplanets Versus Solar System Planets

Solar System planets:

Orbit the Sun.

Exoplanets:

Orbit other stars.

The physical laws governing them are the same.

Their observed properties reveal a much wider range of planetary environments than the Solar System alone.

## Exoplanet Versus Rogue Planet

Exoplanet:

Normally associated with orbiting a star outside our Solar System.

Rogue planet:

Not gravitationally bound to a star.

## Super-Earth Versus Earth-Like Planet

Super-Earth:

Size or mass classification.

Earth-like:

Broader comparison involving potentially composition and environmental properties.

The terms are not interchangeable.

## Habitable Zone Versus Habitable Planet

Habitable zone:

Orbital concept.

Habitable planet:

Would require suitable physical and chemical conditions.

A habitable-zone planet is not automatically habitable.

## Detection Method Comparison

### Transit

Best for:

Planets whose orbits cross the star from Earth's viewpoint.

Measures:

Radius and orbital period.

### Radial Velocity

Best for:

Planets producing measurable stellar motion.

Measures:

Minimum mass and orbital information.

### Direct Imaging

Best for:

Young massive planets far from stars.

Measures:

Planet light and atmospheric properties.

### Microlensing

Best for:

Distant planets and wider orbits.

Can detect:

Some low-mass and rogue planets.

### Astrometry

Best for:

Stellar positional wobble.

Useful for:

Massive planets with relatively wide orbits.

### Pulsar Timing

Best for:

Planets around pulsars.

Measures:

Extremely precise orbital effects.

## Why Exoplanets Matter

Before the 1990s, scientists knew only the planets of the Solar System.

Exoplanet discoveries revealed that planetary systems are common and extraordinarily diverse.

They have transformed our understanding of:

* Planet formation

* Planet migration

* Atmospheric evolution

* Orbital dynamics

* Stellar systems

* Habitability

Exoplanets also provide the strongest astronomical pathway toward answering one of humanity's largest scientific questions:

Does life exist elsewhere?

## Summary

Exoplanets are planets orbiting stars beyond the Solar System.

The first confirmed exoplanets were discovered around a pulsar in 1992, followed by 51 Pegasi b around a Sun-like star in 1995.

Most exoplanets are discovered indirectly through methods such as:

* Transit observations

* Radial velocity

* Gravitational microlensing

* Astrometry

Direct imaging is possible for selected planets, particularly young massive planets on wide orbits.

Exoplanets include:

* Rocky worlds

* Super-Earths

* Sub-Neptunes

* Gas giants

* Hot Jupiters

* Circumbinary planets

* Ultra-hot planets

Many planetary types have no direct equivalent in our Solar System.

Transit measurements can reveal planetary radius.

Radial velocity can reveal mass.

Together they allow density and composition to be estimated.

Atmospheric spectroscopy can identify molecules such as water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, and other gases.

A planet located in a star's habitable zone is not necessarily inhabited or even truly habitable.

Atmosphere, stellar activity, water, geology, magnetic environment, and many other factors must also be considered.

No life has yet been confirmed on an exoplanet.

The study of exoplanets demonstrates that planets are common throughout the Milky Way and provides the foundation for investigating planetary formation, planetary diversity, and the possibility of life beyond Earth.
