# Impact Melting

Impact melting occurs when shock compression and heating during a hypervelocity collision raise target—and sometimes projectile—material above its melting point.

## Key Points

* Melt production rises strongly with impact energy and velocity and also depends on impactor size and angle, target composition and temperature, and porosity. Vaporization is a still more energetic, distinct outcome.
* Impact melt is predominantly melted target material. It can occur as crater-floor pools, basin-scale sheets, veins, glass, melt-bearing breccias, ejecta deposits, tektites, microtektites, and spherules.
* Melt moves during crater excavation and collapse, so it need not remain on the floor. Thick sheets can cool slowly, crystallize, and sometimes differentiate; their chemistry can mix multiple target layers and projectile material.
* Impact melt differs from volcanic lava and internally generated magma: it forms directly through shock heating, although both impact melt and later volcanism can occupy the same basin.
* Diagnostic context includes shocked minerals, high-pressure phases, breccias, morphology, and ejecta. Weathering, alteration, mixing, burial, and similarity to volcanic rocks can complicate remote or field identification.
* Melt and fractured crust can sustain post-impact hydrothermal circulation where water or ice is present, producing alteration and possible habitable niches. Ice-rich impacts may generate liquid water, vapor, refrozen deposits, and modified crater morphology.
* Melt rocks can preserve impact conditions and may be radiometrically dated, but inherited minerals, later heating, alteration, incomplete isotopic resetting, and mixed events can complicate ages.
* Large basins produce the greatest melt volumes and may thin and fracture crust. Whether impacts directly trigger later volcanism is not automatic and must be distinguished from delayed interior magmatism.
* Lunar melt samples help constrain bombardment and crater chronology; melt, shock darkening, veins, and breccias in meteorites record collisions on asteroid parent bodies.
