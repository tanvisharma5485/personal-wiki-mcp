# Planetary Evolution

Planetary evolution is the coupled, long-term change of a world's interior, surface, atmosphere, volatile reservoirs, magnetic environment, orbit, climate, and habitability. Worlds born from the same protoplanetary disk can follow sharply different paths because their initial conditions and later feedbacks differ.

## Key Points

* Accretion, giant impacts, differentiation, and migration established planets' early mass, composition, rotation, internal structure, volatile inventories, and orbital context. The disk's temperature gradient favored metal and silicate building blocks near the Sun and more solid ices farther out, helping terrestrial and giant planets develop differently.
* Evolution is not a universal linear sequence. Initial composition, formation location, mass, gravity, internal heat, rotation, impacts, atmosphere, water, magnetic history, solar input, tides, and orbital migration interact to create branching pathways.
* A planet's reservoirs are coupled: volcanism outgasses material, tectonics can recycle crust, water weathers rock, surfaces exchange chemicals with atmospheres, and impacts can deliver or remove volatiles.
* Residual formation heat, differentiation, radioactive decay, and tidal friction supply internal energy. Smaller worlds generally cool faster, while larger worlds tend to retain heat longer; cooling can weaken convection, volcanism, tectonics, and magnetic dynamos.
* Atmospheres evolve through outgassing, impact delivery and erosion, condensation, photochemistry, surface reactions, storage in minerals or oceans, and thermal or non-thermal escape. Atmospheric retention depends on several factors—including gravity, temperature, composition, ultraviolet radiation, solar-wind interaction, chemistry, and the magnetic environment—not on magnetism alone.
* Water can move among vapor, surface liquid, subsurface reservoirs, ice, hydrated minerals, and space. Its evolution links geology and climate to [[Planetary Habitability]], which can improve or decline over time rather than remaining fixed.
* Impacts, volcanism, tectonics, erosion, deposition, weathering, glaciation, and resurfacing continually rewrite surfaces. Crater density helps estimate relative surface age, but resurfacing can erase ancient terrain, so visible surface age is not planetary age.
* [[Mercury]] cooled and contracted, forming lobate scarps, while preserving an old cratered surface, volcanic plains, an unusually large core, and a weak intrinsic magnetic field.
* Similar size and rocky composition did not make [[Venus]] and [[Earth]] evolve alike. Earth retained oceans, active plate tectonics, a global dynamo, and life that altered atmospheric chemistry; Venus developed a massive carbon-dioxide atmosphere, extreme greenhouse conditions, and severe water loss. Whether Venus ever had long-lived oceans remains uncertain.
* Smaller [[Mars]] cooled faster than Earth, lost its global dynamo, lost substantial atmosphere, and changed from environments with ancient rivers, lakes, and deltas to today's cold, dry conditions. Atmospheric escape and storage of carbon dioxide and water in crust, minerals, polar deposits, and subsurface ice are all relevant sinks.
* [[Jupiter]] and [[Saturn]] retained massive primordial hydrogen-helium envelopes and evolve through contraction, heat transport, convection, differentiation, rotation, and satellite interactions. [[Uranus]] and [[Neptune]] differ notably in internal heat output despite similar sizes, an unresolved clue to their formation and interiors.
* Moons can alter rotation, orbital evolution, and internal heating through tides. Tidal heating drives extreme activity on [[Io]] and helps sustain subsurface environments on [[Europa]] and [[Enceladus]].
* Planetary histories are reconstructed by combining imaging, spectroscopy, radiometric dating of available samples, crater counting, gravity and magnetic measurements, radar, seismology, atmospheric observations, and isotope ratios. Each method constrains a different part of the history, and resurfacing or incomplete samples limit interpretation.
* Major open questions include Venus's original water and climate, the duration of surface water and timing of dynamo loss on Mars, Earth's long-lived plate tectonics, Mercury's large core fraction, Uranus's weak intrinsic heat output, and the extent and consequences of giant-planet migration. Model-dependent histories should be distinguished from direct observations.
