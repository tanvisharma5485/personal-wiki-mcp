# Planetary Habitability

Planetary habitability is the capacity of a world or local environment to support life's origin, survival, reproduction, or persistence. It is a systems-level property, distinct from evidence that an environment is actually inhabited.

## Key Points

* Earth is the only known inhabited world and therefore the main biological reference, but potentially habitable environments need not resemble Earth's surface.
* Life as known requires liquid water, carbon-based chemistry, accessible biologically important elements (especially carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur), usable energy, chemical disequilibrium, suitable temperature and pressure, nutrient transport, and sufficient environmental stability.
* Habitability is not inhabitation. Water, organic molecules, methane, oxygen, or chemical disequilibrium can arise without life and cannot individually demonstrate biology.
* Surface habitability depends strongly on atmospheric pressure and composition, temperature, stellar energy, radiation, water inventory, and climate stability. Subsurface rock, soil, water, or ice can shield environments from radiation, temperature extremes, impacts, oxidation, and atmospheric loss.
* The circumstellar habitable zone identifies where a rocky planet with a suitable atmosphere might sustain surface liquid water. Its boundaries vary with the star, atmosphere, clouds, albedo, circulation, planetary properties, orbit, and climate feedbacks; location inside it neither guarantees habitability nor life.
* Worlds outside the classical habitable zone may retain subsurface liquid water through formation heat, radioactive decay, or tidal heating. Water-rock reactions and hydrothermal systems can provide minerals, chemical gradients, and energy without sunlight.
* Habitability emerges from coupled stellar, orbital, planetary, atmospheric, geological, chemical, and temporal controls. Mass or magnetic fields alone do not determine atmospheric retention, and Earth-like plate tectonics is not proven to be universally required for life.
* Climate feedbacks can stabilize or destabilize environments. Examples include ice-albedo and water-vapor feedbacks, silicate weathering that can remove carbon dioxide, and atmospheric loss that can reduce pressure, warming, and stable surface water.
* Habitability varies by place and time. A hostile planetary average may conceal local niches, while stellar evolution, atmospheric escape, outgassing, impacts, cooling, water loss, and climate feedbacks can create or end habitable periods.
* [[Earth]] combines surface water, a substantial atmosphere, climate and nutrient cycling, geological activity, and multiple energy sources. [[Venus]] and [[Mars]] show how similar rocky starting materials can diverge toward extreme greenhouse conditions or a cold, thin atmosphere; ancient conditions can differ greatly from present ones.
* [[Europa]] and [[Enceladus]] are leading potential subsurface habitats because oceans, tidal energy, and possible water-rock interaction may coexist. [[Titan]] is important for complex organic and prebiotic chemistry and may also contain a subsurface water-rich ocean.
* Habitability assessments combine imaging, spectroscopy, atmospheric, thermal, gravity and magnetic measurements, mineralogy, isotope studies, plume sampling, experiments, and models. Strong claims require multiple independent lines of evidence and a clear distinction between observations and interpretations.
* A biosignature is a possible sign of past or present life, not automatic proof. Abiotic false positives and biologically weak or hidden false negatives make planetary context essential.
