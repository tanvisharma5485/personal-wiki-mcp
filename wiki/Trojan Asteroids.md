# Trojan Asteroids

Trojan asteroids are small Solar System bodies that share approximately the same orbit as a planet while remaining concentrated near stable gravitational regions located about 60 degrees ahead of or behind the planet.

These regions correspond mainly to the:

L4 Lagrange point

and

L5 Lagrange point

Trojan asteroids are especially famous around [[Jupiter]], which hosts by far the largest known Trojan population.

Trojan populations are also known around other planets, including [[Mars]], [[Neptune]], and Earth.

Trojan asteroids are important because they preserve information about [[Solar System Formation]], [[Planetary Migration]], [[Orbital Dynamics]], and the early movement of the giant planets.

## Key Points

* Trojan asteroids are small bodies that share a planet's orbit while remaining near the stable L4 or L5 regions approximately 60 degrees ahead of or behind the planet.
* They orbit the Sun rather than the planet and remain in a 1:1 mean-motion resonance.
* Jupiter possesses the largest known Trojan population. These asteroids are dark, primitive-looking objects that may preserve material captured during early giant-planet migration.
* Mars, Earth, and Neptune also possess known Trojan objects, while temporary or less stable co-orbital populations occur around other planets.
* Trojan configurations also occur among moons, such as Saturn's Telesto and Calypso sharing Tethys's orbit.
* Because Trojan orbital distributions are extremely sensitive to planetary movement, they provide important evidence about [[Planetary Migration]] and [[Solar System Formation]].
* NASA's Lucy mission is conducting the first dedicated spacecraft exploration of multiple Jupiter Trojans, allowing scientists to compare their geology, composition, density, satellites, and collisional histories.
* Trojan asteroids are therefore not merely unusual orbital objects. They are preserved dynamical records of how the Solar System's planets and small bodies reached their present configuration.
