# Atmospheric Escape

Atmospheric escape is the loss of gas from a planet or moon into space.

An atmosphere is held by gravity, but atmospheric particles are constantly moving. Some particles can gain enough energy to overcome a world's gravitational pull and escape permanently.

Atmospheric escape can strongly change a planet's climate, surface pressure, water inventory, atmospheric chemistry, and long-term [[Planetary Habitability]].

It is especially important for smaller worlds such as [[Mars]], bodies exposed to strong stellar radiation, and planets orbiting close to their stars.

Atmospheric escape is closely connected with [[Planetary Atmospheres]], [[Solar Wind]], [[Space Weather]], [[Planetary Magnetism]], [[Water in the Solar System]], and [[Planetary Evolution]].

## Key Points

* Atmospheric escape is the permanent loss of gases from a planetary atmosphere into space.
* Escape is usually evaluated in the upper atmosphere: the exobase marks the approximate transition from collision-dominated gas to the nearly collisionless exosphere. Light particles such as hydrogen and helium escape most easily, but heavier species can also be removed.
* In Jeans escape, particles in the high-speed tail of a thermal velocity distribution escape individually. Strong heating can instead drive collective hydrodynamic outflow or atmospheric blow-off, sometimes carrying heavier species along with hydrogen.
* Important non-thermal mechanisms include sputtering by energetic particles, ion pickup by plasma and electromagnetic fields, energetic products of photochemical reactions, and charge exchange.
* Escape can be limited by how rapidly a species diffuses upward through heavier gas. Its overall importance depends on both the loss rate and the atmospheric reservoir, not the rate alone.
* High-energy ultraviolet and X-ray radiation can heat, ionize, and dissociate upper-atmospheric gas. Young stars can therefore drive much stronger escape than their present-day activity suggests.
* Water loss can proceed when ultraviolet light splits upper-atmospheric water and hydrogen escapes. Cold traps restrict this pathway, while moist or runaway greenhouse conditions can weaken that protection; retained oxygen may escape, react with rocks, enter minerals, or accumulate abiotically.
* Preferential loss of lighter isotopes can leave heavier-isotope enrichment, providing evidence of past loss when interpreted alongside chemistry and geological reservoirs.
* Atmospheric escape has played a major role in the history of [[Mars]], contributed to the water evolution of [[Venus]], and continuously occurs on [[Earth]].
* A global magnetic field does not simply switch escape off: [[Earth]] still loses particles, [[Venus]] retains a dense atmosphere without an Earth-like intrinsic field, and [[Mars]] evolved through several loss and storage processes rather than magnetic-field decline alone.
* Atmospheric supply from volcanism, impacts, sublimation, surface exchange, chemistry, biology, or plumes can offset loss. [[Io]] and [[Enceladus]] illustrate active replenishment alongside continuing escape.
* Among [[Exoplanets]], stellar photoevaporation and heat left from planetary formation can strip light envelopes, helping shape the super-Earth/sub-Neptune radius valley. Close-in worlds may show extended hydrogen or helium tails or, in extreme cases, Roche-lobe overflow.
* Escape is investigated through spacecraft particle measurements, spectroscopy, isotope ratios, planetary geology, exoplanet population statistics, and numerical models. Present escape may be directly measured, but ancient histories remain model-dependent.
* Atmospheric retention depends on the combined effects of gravity, temperature, composition, stellar radiation, stellar wind, magnetic environment, atmospheric replenishment, and geological history.
* For this reason, atmospheric escape is one of the central processes linking [[Planetary Atmospheres]], [[Space Weather]], [[Planetary Evolution]], [[Water in the Solar System]], and [[Planetary Habitability]].
