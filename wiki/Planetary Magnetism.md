# Planetary Magnetism

Planetary magnetic environments arise from intrinsic dynamos, induced currents, crustal remanence, and interactions between the [[Solar Wind]] and an atmosphere or ionosphere. They reveal otherwise inaccessible information about interiors, thermal history, plasma environments, and possible subsurface oceans.

## Key Points

* A magnetic field describes magnetic forces, while a magnetosphere is the surrounding region where a body's magnetic environment controls charged-particle motion.
* Intrinsic dynamos require moving electrically conducting fluid and an energy source that sustains organized flow; rotation helps organize many dynamos but does not determine their existence by itself.
* Rocky-planet dynamos generally operate in metallic cores. The giant planets instead use deep conducting materials: metallic hydrogen in [[Jupiter]] and [[Saturn]], and probably shallower conducting mixtures in [[Uranus]] and [[Neptune]].
* Fields range from approximately dipolar to strongly multipolar. Field strength, tilt, offset, geometry, temporal variation, solar-wind pressure, and internal plasma sources all matter when comparing magnetic environments.
* A world can lack a present global dynamo yet retain crustal remanence or an induced magnetosphere. [[Venus]] has an induced magnetosphere; [[Mars]] combines an induced interaction with strong crustal records of an ancient dynamo.
* [[Ganymede]] is the only moon known to generate its own intrinsic global field. Changing external fields can also induce currents in conductive layers, providing evidence for salty oceans within [[Europa]], Ganymede, and [[Callisto]].
* Magnetospheres can contain bow shocks, magnetopauses, magnetotails, current systems, radiation belts, and polar cusps. Magnetic reconnection transfers stored magnetic energy into plasma motion, heat, and energetic particles.
* Magnetic fields shape [[Aurora|auroras]], charged-particle access, radiation belts, and some atmospheric-escape processes. They can deflect plasma but can also channel or trap particles, so a strong field neither guarantees atmospheric retention nor habitability.
* Planetary magnetism evolves as interiors cool and flow changes. Crustal remanence and paleomagnetism can preserve ancient fields, although heating and resurfacing can erase that record.
* Spacecraft magnetometers measure field strength, direction, and variation. Mapping must distinguish internal fields and crustal anomalies from external magnetospheric contributions.

## Planetary Comparison

| World | Present global intrinsic field | Distinguishing character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [[Mercury]] | Yes | Weak and offset; evidence of an active dynamo in a small rocky planet |
| [[Venus]] | No strong global field | Solar-wind interaction with its ionosphere creates an induced magnetosphere |
| [[Earth]] | Yes | Liquid-outer-core geodynamo; an approximately dipolar but evolving field |
| [[Mars]] | No | Ancient crustal remanence plus a modern induced interaction |
| [[Jupiter]] | Yes | Strongest planetary field and largest planetary magnetosphere; plasma supplied by [[Io]] |
| [[Saturn]] | Yes | Strong field unusually well aligned with its rotation axis; plasma supplied partly by [[Enceladus]] |
| [[Uranus]] | Yes | Strongly tilted and offset field |
| [[Neptune]] | Yes | Strongly tilted and offset field |
