# Impact Cratering

Impact cratering is a rapid, shock-driven process that excavates and restructures solid planetary surfaces, leaving records of bombardment, crustal properties, resurfacing, and surface age.

## Key Points

* Hypervelocity impactors release kinetic energy according to `E = 1/2 mv²`. Shock pressure can overwhelm ordinary material strength, compressing, fracturing, heating, melting, or vaporizing both projectile and target; the projectile is usually destroyed or mixed into target material.
* Formation proceeds through contact and compression, excavation of a deep transient cavity and ejecta, then modification by gravity. The final crater is normally many times wider and shallower than the impactor and transient cavity.
* Fresh structures may include a raised rim, floor, ejecta blanket, rays, secondary craters, shocked rock, and [[Impact Melting|impact melt]]. Impact evidence such as shocked minerals, shatter cones, breccias, melt, morphology, ejecta, and geophysical anomalies is stronger than circular shape alone.
* Small simple craters are relatively deep bowls. Larger unstable cavities collapse into wider, shallower complex craters with terraced walls, flat floors, and central peaks of uplifted target rock. At greater scale, peak rings and multi-ring impact basins replace a single central uplift.
* The simple-to-complex transition varies by world: stronger gravity promotes collapse at smaller diameters, while target strength, layering, porosity, temperature, and ice content also affect morphology. Weak gravity lets ejecta travel farther or escape; porous targets absorb energy through crushing and compaction.
* Basin-forming impacts can deform hundreds or thousands of kilometers of crust, excavate deep material, thin and fracture crust, spread ejecta and secondaries widely, and create large melt sheets. Later volcanic flooding is distinct from melt generated during the impact.
* Crater survival reflects production minus removal. Volcanism, tectonics, erosion, sedimentation, mass wasting, ice flow, viscous relaxation, later impacts, and burial can degrade or erase craters; dense atmospheres also filter small impactors before they reach the ground.
* Weathering alters crater material in place, whereas erosion transports it. Earth favors physical, chemical, and biological weathering; airless bodies undergo thermal and space weathering plus impact gardening; icy moons also undergo radiation processing, sputtering, and sublimation.
* The Moon, Mercury, and Callisto preserve ancient crater-rich terrain. Earth continually destroys or hides its record; Venus combines atmospheric filtering with resurfacing; Mars preserves old and renewed regions; Io, Europa, and active parts of Enceladus have few surviving craters because their surfaces are renewed.
* Crater density dates the exposed surface rather than the whole world. Secondary craters, saturation, incomplete preservation, and changing impact flux complicate interpretation, so formal age estimates use [[Crater Counting]].
* Impacts excavate subsurface materials and can reveal crustal layering through ejecta, walls, central peaks, and peak rings. They may also disrupt climates and ecosystems, remove atmosphere, deliver volatiles or organics, or create crater lakes, fractures, and hydrothermal systems relevant to [[Planetary Habitability]].
* Solar System collisions progressed from accretion and giant impacts through intense early bombardment to a lower modern flux. The exact early chronology—including whether a sharp Late Heavy Bombardment occurred—remains debated, and visible crater records must be separated from preservation effects.
