# Ring Particles

Ring particles are the separate dust grains, ice-rich fragments, rocky material, aggregates, and embedded bodies whose collective orbits form [[Planetary Rings]].

## Key Points

* Particle populations span submicron dust through millimeter-, centimeter-, and meter-scale material, with larger moonlets in some regions. No single size represents an entire ring.
* Saturn's main-ring particles are dominated by reflective water ice with darker contaminants. Jupiter's rings contain abundant impact-generated dust, while the darker Uranian and Neptunian rings have different surface properties and less certain compositions.
* Particle surfaces evolve through micrometeoroid impacts, irradiation, charged-particle bombardment, sputtering, deposition, collisions, fragmentation, and outside contamination; surface appearance need not reveal the composition of a particle's interior.
* Frequent low-speed collisions dissipate random motion and keep rings thin. They can fragment particles or build temporary porous aggregates, and they transport energy and angular momentum through the disk.
* In dense rings, particle self-gravity competes with tidal shear and collisions to create elongated, temporary self-gravity wakes. These affect observed brightness, opacity, thermal behavior, and inferred ring mass.
* Fine grains can become electrically charged, making magnetic, plasma, and radiation forces important alongside gravity. These effects can transport or remove dust and create structures absent from dense, large-particle rings.
* Micrometeoroid impacts generate dust, contaminate surfaces, and eject material that can land elsewhere in the rings. This ballistic transport can redistribute composition and help shape radial structure.
* Effective ring viscosity arises from collisions, correlated motion, gravity, and self-gravity wakes; it governs momentum transport and ring spreading and is not always well represented by a simple molecular-gas analogy.
* Optical depth measures attenuation along a line of sight rather than a unique particle density. Imaging, spectroscopy, stellar and radio occultations, phase-angle measurements, and thermal observations sample different particle properties and size ranges.
