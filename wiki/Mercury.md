# Mercury

Mercury is the innermost terrestrial planet, distinguished by its large metallic core and ancient, heavily modified surface.

## Key Points

* Its surface includes impact craters, volcanic plains, and long contractional scarps.
* It has a tenuous exosphere, a global magnetic field, and no moons.
* Its rotation is in a 3:2 spin-orbit resonance, and permanently shadowed polar craters contain water ice.

## Detailed Knowledge


### Overview

Mercury is the **closest planet to the Sun** and the **smallest of the eight major planets in the Solar System**.

It is a rocky terrestrial planet with an ancient, heavily cratered surface, an unusually large metallic core, almost no substantial atmosphere, extreme temperature variations, a weak but global magnetic field, and permanently shadowed polar craters containing water ice.

Mercury is one of the most unusual terrestrial planets. Although it is closest to the Sun, it is **not the hottest planet**. Venus is hotter because its dense carbon dioxide atmosphere produces an extreme greenhouse effect.

Mercury has no natural moons and no ring system.

Its surface resembles Earth's Moon in many ways because both worlds contain extensive impact craters, ancient plains, and little atmospheric erosion. However, Mercury differs significantly from the Moon because Mercury is a planet with a very large iron-rich core, a global magnetic field, volcanic plains, enormous contractional cliffs, and a dynamic exosphere.

Mercury completes an orbit around the Sun faster than any other planet, taking only about **88 Earth days**. However, it rotates slowly. One complete solar day on Mercury lasts approximately **176 Earth days**.

Mercury is especially important scientifically because it helps researchers understand terrestrial planet formation, planetary differentiation, extreme solar environments, magnetic-field generation, volatile survival close to the Sun, and the early evolution of the inner Solar System.

### Why Mercury Is So Dense

Mercury has an enormous iron-rich core relative to the planet's overall size.

Possible explanations include:

Its original building materials were unusually metal-rich

High-temperature processes removed some silicate material

A giant impact stripped away part of an early mantle

Solar-nebula processes altered its composition

Multiple early impacts modified the planet

MESSENGER measurements have challenged some simple versions of these models, so Mercury's unusual composition remains an active research topic.

### Why Mercury's Solar Day Is So Long

Mercury rotates slowly while simultaneously orbiting rapidly around the Sun.

Because of this combination, the Sun takes approximately 176 Earth days to return to the same position in Mercury's sky.

### 3:2 Spin-Orbit Resonance

Mercury is locked in a:

3:2 spin-orbit resonance.

This means:

Mercury rotates exactly three times for every two orbits around the Sun.

For two Mercury years:

Mercury completes three rotations.

This resonance is maintained by gravitational interactions with the Sun and Mercury's eccentric orbit.

### Mercury Is Not Tidally Locked in a 1:1 Resonance

Scientists once thought Mercury might always keep the same hemisphere facing the Sun.

This would be ordinary synchronous tidal locking.

Radar observations in the 1960s showed that this was incorrect.

Mercury instead has a 3:2 spin-orbit resonance.

### Size of the Sun in Mercury's Sky

### Seasons

Mercury does not experience Earth-like seasons caused by axial tilt.

Its extremely small axial tilt means seasonal sunlight differences between hemispheres are minimal.

However, Mercury's eccentric orbit produces major changes in solar heating throughout its year.

### Water Ice on Mercury

Despite being the closest planet to the Sun, Mercury contains water ice.

This is one of the planet's most surprising features.

### Polar Ice

Water ice exists inside permanently shadowed craters near Mercury's poles.

These crater floors never receive direct sunlight.

Temperatures there can remain extremely low.

### Why Polar Craters Remain Dark

Mercury's axial tilt is almost zero.

Therefore, sunlight strikes the polar regions at very low angles.

Deep crater floors near the poles can remain permanently shadowed.

### Cold Traps

Permanently shadowed regions can act as:

Cold traps.

Temperatures can be low enough for water ice and other volatile compounds to remain stable for extremely long periods.

### Discovery of Polar Ice

Earth-based radar observations first provided strong evidence for highly reflective deposits near Mercury's poles.

MESSENGER later confirmed that many of these deposits are consistent with water ice.

### MESSENGER Evidence for Ice

MESSENGER used several types of measurements, including:

Together they strongly supported the presence of water ice.

### Dark Material Over Ice

This material may help insulate the underlying ice.

### Formation

Mercury formed from material in the inner region of the protoplanetary disk.

Its unusually high metal-to-rock ratio remains one of the biggest puzzles in planetary science.

### Giant Impact Hypothesis for Mercury

One hypothesis suggests early Mercury was larger and contained a thicker rocky mantle.

A giant impact may have stripped away much of this mantle, leaving a metal-rich planet.

However, MESSENGER's discovery of volatile elements complicates simple high-temperature giant-impact models.

### Solar Heating Hypothesis

Another idea suggests intense early solar heating vaporized portions of Mercury's rocky mantle.

However, Mercury's retained volatile elements again create challenges for simple versions of this explanation.

### Metal-Rich Building Blocks

Mercury may instead have formed from naturally metal-rich materials in the inner protoplanetary disk.

Chemical and physical processes in the early Solar System could have separated metal-rich material from silicates before Mercury finished forming.

### Mercury Formation Remains Unsolved

No single formation model currently explains all observed properties perfectly.

A successful model must explain:

Large metallic core

Thin silicate mantle

Surface chemistry

High sulfur abundance

Retained volatile elements

Orbital location

### Mercury's Orbit and General Relativity

Mercury played an important role in testing Einstein's theory of general relativity.

Astronomers discovered that Mercury's orbit slowly precesses.

Most of this precession can be explained by gravitational interactions with other planets.

However, a small additional amount could not be explained by Newtonian gravity alone.

### Perihelion Precession

Mercury's perihelion slowly shifts around the Sun.

The unexplained residual precession is approximately:

43 arcseconds per century.

Einstein's general relativity successfully explained this discrepancy.

### Importance to General Relativity

The successful prediction of Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession became one of the early major confirmations of general relativity.

### Transit of Mercury

A transit occurs when Mercury passes directly between Earth and the Sun.

Mercury then appears as a small dark dot moving across the Sun's disk.

### Frequency of Mercury Transits

Mercury transits occur approximately:

13 or 14 times per century.

They occur only when the orbital geometry is correctly aligned.

### Observing a Mercury Transit

A Mercury transit must never be viewed by looking directly at the Sun without appropriate certified solar viewing equipment.

Direct solar observation can cause permanent eye damage.

### Hill Sphere

A planet's Hill sphere is the region where its gravity can dominate over the Sun strongly enough for satellites to remain gravitationally bound.

Mercury's Hill sphere is relatively small because it is:

Close to the Sun

### Common Misconceptions

#### Mercury Is the Hottest Planet

Incorrect.

Venus is hotter.

Mercury is closer to the Sun but lacks a thick heat-trapping atmosphere.

#### Mercury Has No Atmosphere at All

Not exactly.

Mercury has an extremely thin exosphere.

It does not have a substantial conventional atmosphere.

#### Mercury Always Keeps One Side Facing the Sun

Incorrect.

Mercury is not in a 1:1 synchronous rotation.

It is in a 3:2 spin-orbit resonance.

#### One Mercury Day Equals 58.6 Earth Days

This requires clarification.

Its sidereal rotation period is about 58.6 Earth days.

Its solar day is about 176 Earth days.

#### Mercury Has No Water

Incorrect.

Water ice exists in permanently shadowed polar craters.

#### Water Ice Cannot Exist Close to the Sun

Incorrect.

Permanently shadowed polar regions can remain extremely cold despite Mercury's proximity to the Sun.

#### Mercury Has No Magnetic Field

Incorrect.

Mercury has a weak global intrinsic magnetic field.

#### Mercury Is Smaller Than Every Moon

Incorrect.

Only a few moons, particularly Ganymede and Titan, are larger than Mercury in diameter.

Most moons are much smaller.

#### Ganymede Is More Massive Than Mercury

Incorrect.

Ganymede is larger in diameter but Mercury is much more massive.

#### Mercury Is Just Like Earth's Moon

Incorrect.

Their surfaces look similar in some respects, but Mercury has:

A global magnetic field

A much larger metallic core

Different chemistry

Different tectonic history

#### Mercury's Surface Is Molten

Incorrect.

Mercury has a solid rocky surface.

Some daytime locations become extremely hot, but not hot enough to melt the entire surface.

#### Mercury Has Active Earth-Like Weather

Incorrect.

It lacks a substantial atmosphere required for conventional weather.

#### Mercury Has Seasons Like Earth

Not significantly.

Its axial tilt is nearly zero.

Orbital eccentricity causes major changes in solar intensity, but these are different from Earth's axial-tilt seasons.

#### Mercury Has Moons

Incorrect.

Mercury has no known natural moons.

#### Mercury Has Rings

Incorrect.

Mercury has no known ring system.

### Final Wiki Summary

Mercury is the smallest and innermost major planet of the Solar System. It is a dense rocky world dominated internally by an unusually large metallic core. Mercury orbits the Sun every 88 Earth days but rotates only once every 58.6 Earth days, producing a solar day lasting approximately 176 Earth days. Its 3:2 spin-orbit resonance is one of its most distinctive orbital characteristics.

Its almost nonexistent atmosphere allows the surface to experience extreme temperature changes, from approximately 430°C during the hottest daytime conditions to around −180°C during the night. Despite Mercury's proximity to the Sun, permanently shadowed polar craters remain cold enough to preserve substantial deposits of water ice.

Mercury's ancient surface records billions of years of impacts, volcanism, tectonic deformation, and planetary contraction. Caloris basin represents one of its greatest impact structures, while widespread lobate scarps reveal that the planet shrank as its interior cooled. Hollows, pyroclastic deposits, volatile elements, and polar ice demonstrate that Mercury is chemically and geologically more complex than early models predicted.

Beneath the surface lies an enormous iron-rich core occupying most of Mercury's radius. Part of this core remains liquid and generates a weak global magnetic field. Mercury is therefore the only terrestrial planet other than Earth with a presently active global intrinsic magnetic field generated internally, although its field is much weaker than Earth's.

Mariner 10 provided humanity's first close observations of Mercury. MESSENGER later became the first spacecraft to orbit the planet and transformed scientific understanding of its geology, composition, interior, exosphere, magnetic field, and polar ice. BepiColombo is designed to extend this investigation.

Mercury remains crucial for understanding rocky planet formation, planetary cores, magnetic dynamos, volatile survival, atmospheric loss, extreme stellar environments, general relativity, and metal-rich rocky exoplanets.
