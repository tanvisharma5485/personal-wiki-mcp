# Planetary Atmospheres

Planetary atmospheres are gravitationally associated layers of gas, vapor, aerosols, or suspended particles around planets, some moons, and dwarf planets. They range from dense, weather-producing envelopes to tenuous exospheres and evolve through exchanges among planetary interiors, surfaces, space, and—on [[Earth]]—life.

## Key Points

* Atmospheric origin and survival depend on interacting controls: mass and gravity, temperature, molecular weight, volatile inventory, stellar radiation and wind, chemistry, geology, impacts, replenishment, escape, and time. Giant planets retained primordial hydrogen-helium envelopes; rocky planets developed substantially modified secondary atmospheres through processes such as outgassing, impact delivery, sublimation, chemistry, and biological activity.
* An exosphere is an extremely tenuous outer region where particle collisions are rare. [[Mercury]] and the [[Moon]] have exospheres rather than substantial collisional atmospheres, so describing them as having “no atmosphere” is an oversimplification.
* Composition shapes molecular weight, pressure, greenhouse warming, clouds, photochemistry, escape, spectra, climate, and possible habitability. [[Earth]] is nitrogen-oxygen dominated; [[Venus]] and [[Mars]] are carbon-dioxide dominated; [[Titan]] is nitrogen dominated with methane; and the giant planets are primarily hydrogen and helium, with methane important on [[Uranus]] and [[Neptune]]. Trace gases and aerosols can have effects disproportionate to their abundance.
* Pressure is the weight of overlying atmospheric material under gravity and affects whether substances such as water can remain liquid. On giant planets, which lack a conventional solid surface at visible cloud level, pressure levels serve as reference altitudes as temperature and density increase with depth.
* Distance from the Sun alone does not determine temperature. Absorbed stellar energy, albedo, greenhouse absorption, clouds, circulation, surface properties, internal heat, rotation, latitude, altitude, seasons, and orbit all matter. Highly reflective [[Venus]] is hotter than [[Mercury]] because its massive carbon-dioxide atmosphere produces an extreme greenhouse effect.
* Atmospheres have vertical structure because pressure, density, temperature, chemistry, and radiation vary with altitude. Pressure falls approximately exponentially in hydrostatic conditions; scale height increases with temperature and lower molecular mass and decreases with stronger gravity. Tropospheres, stratospheres, thermospheres, and exospheres describe process-dependent regions and are not identical on every world.
* Clouds and aerosols both reflect incoming radiation and interact with outgoing infrared radiation. Their net effect depends on altitude, thickness, coverage, composition, and particle properties; examples include water clouds on Earth, sulfuric-acid clouds on Venus, ammonia-bearing clouds on giant planets, Martian dust, and Titan's photochemical haze.
* Ultraviolet-driven photochemistry, condensation, surface and ocean reactions, volcanic inputs, energetic particles, and biological processes continually transform atmospheric composition. Sources, sinks, reaction networks, transport, and [[Atmospheric Escape]] must therefore be considered together.
* Uneven heating drives pressure differences and winds, while rotation, friction, convection, topography, latent heat, atmospheric mass, seasons, and internal heat organize circulation. Venus and Titan superrotate; Jupiter and Saturn have powerful east-west jets; Mars has major dust storms; and Neptune shows that intense weather can persist under weak sunlight when internal heat contributes energy.
* Atmospheres exchange volatiles and energy with surfaces, oceans, and ice. Weathering can remove gases, volcanism can replenish them, impacts can add or erode volatiles, and seasonal condensation and sublimation can change pressure, as with carbon dioxide on Mars and nitrogen-rich volatiles on [[Pluto]]. A planet does not require Earth-like plate tectonics to possess an atmosphere.
* Atmospheric retention is not controlled by gravity or magnetism alone. Thermal escape includes Jeans and hydrodynamic escape; non-thermal loss includes ion pickup, sputtering, photochemical escape, charge exchange, and plasma interactions. Venus's massive atmosphere despite lacking an Earth-like global field shows that a magnetic field is not a simple requirement for atmospheric survival.
* Atmospheres are products of evolution rather than static shells. [[Venus]], Earth, and Mars form a natural comparison: similar rocky worlds developed an extreme dense greenhouse, a temperate atmosphere-ocean-geology-life system, and a cold thin atmosphere shaped by loss and volatile storage, respectively.
* Atmospheres affect [[Planetary Habitability]] through temperature, pressure, liquid-water stability, climate, radiation exposure, and chemical cycling, but an atmosphere—or an individual gas—is not evidence of life. Biosignature candidates require planetary and stellar context because abiotic false positives and observational false negatives are possible.
* Exoplanet atmospheres can be investigated with transmission and emission spectroscopy, secondary eclipses, phase curves, direct imaging in favorable cases, and high-resolution spectroscopy. Within the Solar System, occultations, multiwavelength remote sensing, landers, and entry probes constrain composition, temperature, pressure, winds, and vertical structure. Clouds, hazes, stellar activity, weak signals, and model assumptions can make interpretations degenerate, so direct measurements should be distinguished from model-dependent inference.
* Important feedbacks include water-vapor, ice-albedo, cloud, and carbon-cycle feedbacks. Atmospheres can also partially condense onto cold surfaces, while sufficiently substantial atmospheres on tidally locked worlds can redistribute heat between permanent day and night hemispheres.
