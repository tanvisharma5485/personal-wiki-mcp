# Crater Counting

Crater counting measures crater populations within mapped geological units to estimate relative surface ages and, through calibrated chronology models, approximate numerical ages.

## Key Points

* A longer-exposed surface generally accumulates more primary craters under a comparable impact environment. The result describes surface exposure or crater-retention age, not the age of the world or necessarily the underlying rock.
* Analysts map a geologically coherent unit, measure its area, identify crater rims and topography, measure diameters rim-to-rim, and construct a crater size-frequency distribution (CSFD). Cumulative plots, differential distributions, and R-plots reveal density, distribution slopes, saturation, and resurfacing signatures.
* Production functions model the expected sizes of newly formed craters; chronology functions relate density to time. Apollo and Luna sample ages calibrate lunar chronology, which is adjusted for impact probability, populations, velocity, gravity, atmosphere, and target properties when applied elsewhere.
* Relative-age comparisons are usually more robust than absolute model ages. Numerical ages depend on impact-flux history, scaling laws, chronology calibration, target properties, and geological interpretation and are not direct radiometric dates.
* Secondary craters can inflate counts, especially at small diameters. Saturation causes new impacts to erase old ones, while erosion, burial, weathering, and partial resurfacing preferentially remove or preserve different sizes and can create mixed populations.
* Image resolution establishes a minimum reliable diameter; illumination, shadows, topographic data, and radar affect detection. Small areas, young surfaces, and rare large craters have substantial statistical uncertainty.
* Manual and automated detection can use imagery, digital elevation models, and radar. Machine learning accelerates large surveys but still needs validation against volcanic pits, tectonic structures, shadows, artifacts, and degraded landforms.
* [[Planetary Observation|Observational]] counts are strongest when combined with stratigraphy: cross-cutting, burial, and superposition establish event sequences. Returned samples from well-characterized units can add calibration points and improve chronology beyond the Moon.
* Applications include dating lava flows, basin ejecta, sedimentary and tectonic units, icy terrain, and resurfacing on the Moon, Mercury, Mars, Venus, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, and Enceladus; outer-system absolute ages remain especially model-dependent.
