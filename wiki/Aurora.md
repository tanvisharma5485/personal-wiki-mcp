# Aurora

## Key Points

* Auroras form when energetic charged particles excite upper-atmospheric gases, which emit light at characteristic colors and altitudes.
* Planetary magnetic fields, magnetic reconnection, particle precipitation, and solar-wind conditions control where and when auroras occur.
* Auroral processes occur on Earth and other magnetized or locally magnetized worlds and are useful probes of atmospheres, magnetic fields, and space weather.

## Overview

An aurora is a natural light display produced when energetic charged particles interact with the upper atmosphere of a planet.

On Earth, auroras are most common near the polar regions because Earth's magnetic field guides charged particles toward high latitudes.

The two best-known terrestrial auroras are:

* Aurora Borealis – Northern Lights

* Aurora Australis – Southern Lights

Auroras are closely connected with:

* Solar wind

* Earth's magnetosphere

* Magnetic reconnection

* Geomagnetic storms

* Space weather

* Solar activity

Auroras are not unique to Earth.

They have also been observed on several other planets and moons.

## Basic Definition

An aurora is light emitted by atmospheric atoms and molecules after they are excited by energetic charged particles.

The basic process is:

Solar or magnetospheric particles → guided by magnetic fields → enter upper atmosphere → collide with atoms and molecules → atmospheric particles become excited → light is emitted

## Aurora Borealis

Aurora Borealis is the aurora occurring primarily in Earth's Northern Hemisphere.

It is commonly called:

Northern Lights

It is frequently visible at high northern latitudes.

Examples include regions of:

* Alaska

* Canada

* Greenland

* Iceland

* Norway

* Sweden

* Finland

* Russia

During strong geomagnetic storms, auroras can be visible much farther south.

## Aurora Australis

Aurora Australis occurs primarily in Earth's Southern Hemisphere.

It is commonly called:

Southern Lights

It can be observed from high southern latitudes, including:

* Antarctica

* Southern Australia

* New Zealand

* Southern Ocean regions

## Where Auroras Occur

Earth's auroras usually occur in the upper atmosphere.

Typical auroral emissions occur roughly between:

80 km and several hundred kilometers altitude

The exact altitude depends on:

* Particle energy

* Atmospheric composition

* Type of auroral emission

## Auroral Oval

Auroras are usually concentrated in ring-shaped regions around Earth's magnetic poles.

These regions are called:

Auroral ovals

There is one in each hemisphere.

The auroral oval expands and contracts depending on geomagnetic activity.

## Magnetic Poles Versus Geographic Poles

Auroras are organized mainly around Earth's magnetic poles rather than the geographic North and South Poles.

Earth's magnetic and geographic axes are not perfectly aligned.

Therefore, the auroral oval is offset from the geographic poles.

## Source of Auroral Energy

The energy responsible for most terrestrial auroras ultimately comes from the interaction between:

Solar wind

and:

Earth's magnetosphere

Solar wind transfers energy into Earth's magnetic environment.

That energy can later accelerate charged particles toward the atmosphere.

## Solar Wind

The solar wind is a continuous stream of plasma flowing outward from the Sun.

It consists primarily of:

* Protons

* Electrons

* Alpha particles

It also carries the interplanetary magnetic field.

## Magnetosphere

Earth's magnetosphere is the region where Earth's magnetic field strongly influences charged particles.

The solar wind shapes the magnetosphere.

On the side facing the Sun, the magnetosphere is compressed.

On the night side, it is stretched into a long:

Magnetotail

## Magnetotail

The magnetotail extends far behind Earth on the side opposite the Sun.

It contains stored magnetic energy.

During geomagnetic activity, this energy can be released and accelerate particles toward Earth's polar atmosphere.

## Magnetic Reconnection

Magnetic reconnection is one of the key processes connecting solar wind to auroral activity.

It occurs when magnetic-field structures change topology and release stored magnetic energy.

Reconnection can occur:

* On the dayside magnetopause

* In the magnetotail

## Dayside Reconnection

When the interplanetary magnetic field has a favorable orientation, especially a strong southward component, it can reconnect with Earth's magnetic field on the dayside.

This allows solar-wind energy to enter the magnetosphere more effectively.

## Magnetotail Reconnection

Energy stored in the magnetotail can later be released through reconnection.

This process can accelerate particles toward Earth.

These particles can produce intense auroral displays.

## Are Aurora Particles Directly From the Sun?

Some energetic particles ultimately originate in the solar wind, but the process is not simply solar particles traveling directly along straight paths into the atmosphere.

Earth's magnetosphere:

* Stores energy

* Redirects particles

* Accelerates particles

* Controls where particles enter

Therefore, auroras are a magnetosphere-atmosphere phenomenon driven strongly by solar activity.

## Particle Precipitation

When energetic electrons and ions travel into the upper atmosphere, the process is called:

Particle precipitation

These particles collide with atmospheric atoms and molecules.

## Excitation

A collision can transfer energy to an atmospheric atom or molecule.

The particle enters an excited energy state.

Later, it returns to a lower-energy state.

The energy difference can be released as a photon.

This produces visible auroral light.

## Main Atmospheric Gases

Earth's atmosphere consists mainly of:

* Nitrogen

* Oxygen

Auroral colors are therefore strongly influenced by emissions from:

* Atomic oxygen

* Molecular nitrogen

* Ionized molecular nitrogen

## Green Aurora

Green is the most commonly observed auroral color.

It is primarily produced by atomic oxygen.

A major green emission occurs near a wavelength of approximately:

557.7 nanometers

Green aurora is common at altitudes around:

100–150 km

although the altitude can vary.

## Red Aurora

Atomic oxygen can also produce red auroral light.

A major red emission occurs near:

630.0 nanometers

Red aurora commonly forms at higher altitudes than green aurora.

It can occur above approximately:

200 km

and extend much higher.

## Blue Aurora

Blue auroral emissions are commonly associated with molecular nitrogen and ionized nitrogen.

Blue light tends to occur at relatively lower altitudes where energetic particles penetrate deeply.

## Purple and Violet Aurora

Purple or violet colors often result from mixtures of:

* Nitrogen emissions

* Blue light

* Red light

These colors can be especially visible near the lower edges of intense auroral curtains.

## Pink Aurora

Pink auroras can occur when red and blue emissions combine.

They may appear during energetic displays when particles penetrate lower into the atmosphere.

## Why Green Is Common

Green oxygen emission is strong and lies within a region where human vision is highly sensitive.

This makes green the dominant visible color in many auroral displays.

## Why Red Auroras Can Be Rare

Red auroras often occur at high altitude where atmospheric density is very low.

The relevant oxygen transition has a relatively long lifetime.

At lower altitudes, collisions may remove the excitation before red photons are emitted.

## Collision Quenching

Collision quenching occurs when an excited atom collides with another particle before it can emit a photon.

This can suppress particular auroral emissions at lower altitudes.

## Aurora Shapes

Auroras appear in many forms.

Common structures include:

* Arcs

* Curtains

* Bands

* Rays

* Spirals

* Patches

* Diffuse glows

* Corona-like forms

## Auroral Arc

An auroral arc is a long, relatively narrow band of light.

It often extends east-west across the sky.

## Auroral Curtain

Auroral curtains appear as vertically structured sheets of light.

They may look like:

* Drapes

* Curtains

* Ribbons

Their vertical structure reflects alignment with Earth's magnetic field.

## Auroral Rays

Auroral rays are narrow vertical-looking features.

The particles producing them move along Earth's magnetic-field lines.

Perspective makes these structures appear as long rays in the sky.

## Auroral Corona

When an observer looks almost directly along magnetic-field-aligned auroral rays, they may appear to converge toward one point.

This creates a visual structure called an:

Auroral corona

The apparent convergence is a perspective effect.

## Diffuse Aurora

Diffuse aurora appears as a broad, faint glow rather than sharply defined arcs.

It can result from widespread particle precipitation.

## Pulsating Aurora

Pulsating aurora consists of patches of light that repeatedly brighten and fade.

These changes can occur on timescales of seconds.

Wave-particle interactions in Earth's magnetosphere contribute to the process.

## Discrete Aurora

Discrete auroras include well-defined structures such as:

* Arcs

* Curtains

* Rays

They differ from diffuse auroral glows.

## Substorm

A magnetospheric substorm is a temporary release of energy stored in Earth's magnetotail.

Substorms can dramatically intensify auroral activity.

During a substorm, auroral arcs may:

* Brighten

* Expand

* Move

* Break into complex structures

## Auroral Substorm Sequence

A simplified sequence is:

Solar wind transfers energy to magnetosphere

→

Energy accumulates in magnetotail

→

Magnetic reconnection occurs

→

Particles accelerate toward Earth

→

Aurora intensifies

## Geomagnetic Storm

A geomagnetic storm is a larger-scale disturbance of Earth's magnetic environment.

It can occur when strong solar-wind structures or interplanetary coronal mass ejections interact with Earth's magnetosphere.

Geomagnetic storms can cause auroras to expand far from their usual polar regions.

## Aurora During Geomagnetic Storms

During strong geomagnetic storms, the auroral oval expands toward lower latitudes.

Auroras may then become visible in places where they are normally rare.

## Coronal Mass Ejection

A Coronal Mass Ejection, or CME, is a massive eruption of plasma and magnetic field from the Sun.

Earth-directed CMEs can strongly disturb the magnetosphere.

They may produce:

* Geomagnetic storms

* Strong auroras

* Satellite disturbances

* Communication effects

* Power-grid effects

## Interplanetary Coronal Mass Ejection

A CME traveling through interplanetary space is often called:

ICME

When an ICME reaches Earth, its magnetic-field orientation strongly influences the severity of geomagnetic activity.

## Southward Interplanetary Magnetic Field

A strong southward interplanetary magnetic field can reconnect efficiently with Earth's northward-oriented dayside magnetic field.

This enhances energy transfer into the magnetosphere.

Therefore, southward IMF conditions often favor strong geomagnetic activity.

## Coronal Holes

Coronal holes can release high-speed solar-wind streams.

These streams can also produce recurring geomagnetic activity and auroras.

## Corotating Interaction Regions

Fast solar wind can catch up with slower solar wind.

The compressed interaction regions are called:

Corotating Interaction Regions

or:

CIRs

They can cause geomagnetic disturbances and enhance auroral activity.

## Solar Flares and Aurora

Solar flares are sudden releases of electromagnetic radiation from the Sun.

A solar flare itself does not directly create an aurora simply because its light reaches Earth.

However, flares can occur together with:

* CMEs

* Solar energetic particles

which may contribute to space-weather disturbances.

## Space Weather

Auroras are one visible manifestation of space weather.

Space weather includes changes caused by:

* Solar wind

* CMEs

* Solar energetic particles

* Solar magnetic fields

These effects can influence technological systems.

## Geomagnetic Activity

Geomagnetic activity refers to changes in Earth's magnetic field produced by interactions within the solar wind-magnetosphere system.

Auroral activity often increases during stronger geomagnetic disturbances.

## Kp Index

The Kp index is commonly used to describe global geomagnetic activity.

It ranges approximately from:

0 to 9

Low values indicate relatively quiet conditions.

High values indicate stronger geomagnetic disturbance.

Higher Kp values generally increase the chance of auroras being visible at lower latitudes.

## Kp and Aurora Visibility

A high Kp value does not guarantee that a specific observer will see aurora.

Visibility also depends on:

* Latitude

* Darkness

* Clouds

* Moonlight

* Light pollution

* Direction of auroral oval

* Local geomagnetic conditions

## Geomagnetic Latitude

Auroral probability is better related to geomagnetic latitude than simple geographic latitude.

Locations closer to the auroral oval have more frequent auroras.

## Best Time to See Aurora

Auroras require dark skies for good visibility.

Conditions are generally favorable when:

* It is nighttime

* Sky is clear

* Light pollution is low

* Geomagnetic activity is elevated

## Seasonal Visibility

Auroras occur throughout the year.

However, they are easier to observe during seasons with long dark nights.

For high northern latitudes, autumn through spring generally provides better viewing than bright summer nights.

## Equinox Effect

Statistically, geomagnetic activity often shows enhanced occurrence around:

* March equinox

* September equinox

Several physical mechanisms contribute to this seasonal variation.

This does not mean auroras occur only near equinoxes.

## Aurora and Moonlight

Bright moonlight can reduce visual contrast.

Aurora may still be present but faint features become harder to see.

## Light Pollution

City lights reduce auroral visibility.

Dark locations provide much better contrast.

## Camera Versus Human Eye

Modern cameras can often detect auroral colors more strongly than human eyes.

Reasons include:

* Long exposure

* Higher low-light sensitivity

* Image processing

Therefore, photographs can appear more colorful than what an observer saw directly.

## Aurora Motion

Auroral structures can change rapidly.

They may:

* Ripple

* Twist

* Expand

* Brighten

* Fade

* Move across the sky

These changes reflect evolving magnetospheric plasma and electric currents.

## Magnetic Field-Aligned Motion

Charged particles spiral around magnetic-field lines.

Their motion includes:

* Movement along the field

* Circular or spiral motion around the field

This guides energetic particles toward polar regions.

## Lorentz Force

Charged-particle motion in electric and magnetic fields is governed by the Lorentz force.

The magnetic part of the force can be expressed as:

F = q(v × B)

where:

F = force

q = electric charge

v = particle velocity

B = magnetic field

## Why Aurora Occurs Near Poles

Earth's magnetic-field geometry channels charged particles toward high latitudes.

Magnetic-field lines connect the magnetosphere to the upper atmosphere more directly near the polar regions.

Therefore, auroral particle precipitation is concentrated there.

## Auroral Electrons

Electrons are major contributors to many visible auroras.

They can be accelerated to energies ranging from hundreds of electron volts to many thousands of electron volts.

Their collisions with atmospheric gases produce light.

## Proton Aurora

Protons can also produce auroral emissions.

When energetic protons enter the atmosphere, they can undergo charge-exchange processes and contribute to proton aurora.

Proton aurora can differ in appearance and spectral properties from electron-driven aurora.

## Hydrogen Emission

Proton auroras can generate hydrogen emission lines.

These may be detected using specialized instruments.

## Auroral Currents

Auroras are closely associated with powerful electrical-current systems.

Important examples include:

* Field-aligned currents

* Ionospheric currents

* Auroral electrojets

## Field-Aligned Currents

Field-aligned currents flow along Earth's magnetic-field lines between the magnetosphere and ionosphere.

They are also known as:

Birkeland currents

They are fundamental to magnetosphere-ionosphere coupling.

## Kristian Birkeland

Norwegian physicist Kristian Birkeland proposed that charged particles from the Sun could be guided by Earth's magnetic field and produce auroral phenomena.

His ideas helped establish the modern understanding of auroral physics.

## Birkeland Currents

Birkeland currents connect magnetospheric plasma with Earth's ionosphere.

Satellite observations later confirmed the existence of large field-aligned current systems.

## Auroral Electrojet

The auroral electrojets are strong electrical currents flowing through the high-latitude ionosphere.

They can produce significant magnetic disturbances measured on the ground.

## Ionosphere

The ionosphere is an electrically charged region of Earth's upper atmosphere.

Auroral particle precipitation strongly affects the polar ionosphere.

## Aurora and Radio Communication

Auroral activity can change ionospheric conditions.

This may affect:

* High-frequency radio

* Satellite signals

* Radar propagation

## Aurora and GPS

Strong geomagnetic disturbances can produce irregularities in the ionosphere.

These can reduce GPS accuracy.

The effect is not caused by visible auroral light itself but by the same disturbed space environment.

## Aurora and Power Grids

Strong geomagnetic storms can induce electrical currents in long conductors.

These currents can affect:

* Electrical transmission lines

* Transformers

* Pipelines

Aurora therefore serves as a visible sign of broader geomagnetic activity.

## Geomagnetically Induced Currents

Rapid changes in Earth's magnetic field can induce currents in conducting infrastructure.

These are called:

Geomagnetically Induced Currents

or:

GICs

Severe GICs can damage electrical equipment.

## Carrington Event

The Carrington Event of 1859 was one of the most intense geomagnetic storms in recorded history.

Auroras were observed at unusually low latitudes.

Telegraph systems experienced major electrical disturbances.

A similar event today could significantly affect technological infrastructure.

## 1989 Quebec Blackout

A geomagnetic storm in March 1989 contributed to the collapse of the Hydro-Québec electrical grid in Canada.

The event demonstrated that space weather can directly affect modern power infrastructure.

## Aurora and Satellites

Strong geomagnetic activity can affect satellites through:

* Radiation exposure

* Surface charging

* Electronic disturbances

* Changes in atmospheric drag

## Atmospheric Heating

Geomagnetic storms deposit energy into Earth's upper atmosphere.

This heats and expands the thermosphere.

The expanded atmosphere increases drag on low-Earth-orbit satellites.

## Aurora and Astronauts

Visible auroras themselves are not dangerous.

However, strong solar activity associated with major space-weather events can increase radiation hazards for astronauts.

## Aurora Seen From Space

Astronauts aboard spacecraft can observe auroras from above.

From space, auroras often appear as:

* Glowing rings

* Arcs

* Bands around polar regions

## Aurora and Earth's Atmosphere

Auroras occur mainly in:

* Thermosphere

* Upper mesosphere in some lower-altitude emissions

The atmosphere is extremely thin at these heights.

## Thermosphere

The thermosphere begins approximately around:

80–90 km altitude

and extends hundreds of kilometers upward.

Much auroral emission occurs within this region.

## Aurora and the Ozone Layer

Auroras generally occur far above the main ozone layer.

The ozone layer is concentrated primarily in the stratosphere.

## Can Aurora Be Heard?

Reports of sounds associated with very strong auroras have existed for centuries.

Some modern studies have investigated possible mechanisms that could create faint sounds near the ground during auroral activity.

However, auroral light itself occurs far too high for ordinary sound from the auroral region to travel directly to an observer in synchronization with the visual display.

The phenomenon remains unusual and difficult to study.

## Historical Understanding

Auroras have been observed for thousands of years.

Ancient cultures developed many interpretations involving:

* Spirits

* Gods

* Fire

* Celestial battles

* Ancestors

Modern science explains auroras using plasma physics and atmospheric emission.

## Galileo and the Term Aurora

The name "aurora" comes from Aurora, the Roman goddess of dawn.

The term Aurora Borealis means approximately:

Northern dawn

Aurora Australis means:

Southern dawn

## Aurora on Other Planets

Auroral phenomena are not unique to Earth.

They have been observed on:

* Jupiter

* Saturn

* Uranus

and evidence of auroral processes exists in other planetary environments.

## Jupiter's Aurora

Jupiter has extremely powerful auroras.

They are driven by both:

* Solar wind

* Jupiter's own magnetosphere

A major energy source is plasma supplied by the moon Io.

## Io and Jupiter's Aurora

Io is extremely volcanically active.

Its volcanoes release material into space.

Much of this material becomes ionized and forms a plasma torus around Jupiter.

Interaction between Io and Jupiter's magnetic field produces auroral features known as the:

Io footprint

## Jupiter's Main Auroral Oval

Jupiter has persistent auroral emissions around its magnetic poles.

Its auroras can be observed at wavelengths including:

* Ultraviolet

* Infrared

* X-ray

Jupiter's auroral system is much more powerful than Earth's.

## Jupiter X-Ray Aurora

Jupiter produces strong X-ray auroras.

These involve energetic ions precipitating into the upper atmosphere.

They demonstrate the extreme particle energies present in Jupiter's magnetosphere.

## Saturn's Aurora

Saturn also has auroras around its poles.

Its auroral activity is influenced by:

* Solar wind

* Saturn's magnetic field

* Magnetospheric plasma

Auroras have been observed at:

* Ultraviolet

* Infrared

* Visible wavelengths in some observations

## Uranus Aurora

Uranus has an unusual magnetic field that is:

* Strongly tilted

* Offset from the planet's center

Auroral emissions have been detected on Uranus.

Its unusual magnetic geometry produces complex magnetospheric behavior.

## Neptune Aurora

Neptune's strongly tilted and offset magnetic field suggests complex auroral processes.

Auroral and related upper-atmospheric emissions are much more difficult to observe because of Neptune's great distance.

## Mercury

Mercury has a magnetosphere but almost no substantial atmosphere.

It therefore does not produce bright atmospheric auroras like Earth.

Instead, energetic particles interact with its surface and extremely thin exosphere.

## Venus

Venus does not have a strong global intrinsic magnetic field.

It has an induced magnetic environment created through solar-wind interaction with its ionosphere.

Aurora-like emissions have been observed or studied in its atmosphere, although the mechanism differs from Earth's classic polar aurora.

## Mars

Mars lacks a global intrinsic magnetic field today.

However, parts of its crust retain localized magnetic fields.

Several types of auroral emissions have been detected on Mars.

## Martian Discrete Aurora

Discrete auroras on Mars can occur near regions of strong crustal magnetic fields.

These local magnetic structures guide charged particles into the atmosphere.

## Martian Diffuse Aurora

Solar energetic particles can produce diffuse auroral emissions over large regions of Mars.

## Martian Proton Aurora

Mars also exhibits proton aurora caused by solar-wind protons interacting with the planet's extended hydrogen corona.

## Aurora on Moons

Some moons interact strongly with planetary magnetic fields.

These interactions can produce auroral footprints in the atmosphere of the parent planet.

Examples include:

* Io

* Europa

* Ganymede

in Jupiter's magnetosphere.

## Ganymede Aurora

Ganymede has its own intrinsic magnetic field.

Auroral emissions have been observed in its very thin atmosphere.

Changes in its auroral behavior have also provided evidence supporting the presence of a subsurface ocean.

## Aurora and Exoplanets

Scientists are interested in whether auroral emissions could eventually help study exoplanet magnetic fields.

Potential auroral signatures may occur at:

* Radio wavelengths

* Ultraviolet wavelengths

* Optical wavelengths

Detecting such signals is challenging.

## Exoplanet Magnetic Fields

If auroral radio emissions from an exoplanet were detected, they could provide information about:

* Planetary magnetic fields

* Stellar-wind interaction

* Magnetosphere size

* Space-weather environment

## Brown Dwarf Auroras

Powerful auroral radio and optical emissions have been observed from some brown dwarfs.

These objects can produce auroral processes even though they are not planets orbiting within the Solar System.

## Aurora and Habitability

Planetary auroras can reveal interactions between:

* Atmosphere

* Magnetic field

* Stellar wind

These interactions are relevant to atmospheric evolution and potential habitability.

However, the presence of an aurora alone does not indicate that a planet is habitable.

## Magnetic Fields and Atmospheric Protection

A magnetic field can redirect charged particles and create auroral regions.

However, planetary atmospheric loss is complex.

Magnetic fields do not automatically guarantee complete atmospheric protection.

## Aurora Photography

Aurora photography commonly uses:

* Wide-angle lenses

* High ISO

* Long exposures

* Tripods

Modern cameras can reveal structures and colors too faint for the unaided eye.

## Aurora Prediction

Auroral forecasts consider several factors, including:

* Solar-wind speed

* Solar-wind density

* Interplanetary magnetic field

* Geomagnetic indices

* CME arrival predictions

* Coronal-hole streams

## Bz Component

The north-south component of the interplanetary magnetic field is often labeled:

Bz

When Bz becomes strongly southward for a sustained period, magnetic reconnection with Earth's field can become more effective.

This can increase auroral activity.

## Solar Wind Speed and Aurora

Fast solar wind can enhance geomagnetic activity.

However, speed alone does not determine auroral intensity.

Magnetic-field orientation is also extremely important.

## Solar Wind Density and Aurora

Higher solar-wind density increases dynamic pressure on Earth's magnetosphere.

Sudden pressure changes can produce magnetospheric disturbances.

## Aurora and Solar Maximum

Auroral activity often becomes more widespread during periods of high solar activity because strong CMEs occur more frequently.

However, auroras also occur during solar minimum.

## Aurora and Coronal Holes

Long-lived coronal holes can cause repeated high-speed solar-wind streams.

These can produce recurring auroral activity even when solar flare activity is relatively low.

## Aurora and Earth's Magnetic Field Changes

Earth's magnetic field changes slowly over time.

The magnetic poles move.

Therefore, the geographic location of optimal auroral viewing also evolves gradually.

## Aurora and Magnetic Reversal

Earth's magnetic field has reversed polarity many times in geological history.

A reversal does not mean the magnetic field completely disappears everywhere.

The detailed effect of a future reversal on auroral distributions would be complex.

## Aurora on the Day Side

Auroras are not restricted to the night side of Earth.

Day-side auroras occur as well.

They are simply much harder to see against daylight.

Satellites can observe auroral emissions regardless of whether the ground below is in darkness.

## Dayglow Versus Aurora

Dayglow is atmospheric light produced largely by sunlight-driven processes.

Aurora is primarily produced by energetic particle precipitation.

They are distinct upper-atmospheric phenomena.

## Airglow Versus Aurora

Airglow is a faint natural atmospheric emission visible even without strong geomagnetic activity.

It is produced by chemical and radiative processes in the upper atmosphere.

Aurora is generally brighter and associated with energetic particle precipitation.

## STEVE

STEVE stands for:

Strong Thermal Emission Velocity Enhancement

It appears as a narrow purple or mauve arc in the night sky.

Although it can occur during geomagnetic activity, STEVE is physically different from ordinary particle-precipitation aurora.

It is associated with very hot, fast-moving plasma in the upper atmosphere.

## STEVE and Green Picket Fence

STEVE can sometimes appear with green vertical structures known informally as:

Picket fence

Research continues into the exact mechanisms responsible for these emissions.

## SAR Arc

SAR stands for:

Stable Auroral Red arc

Despite the name, SAR arcs are physically different from ordinary aurora.

They consist largely of red oxygen emission produced through heating in the upper atmosphere during geomagnetic disturbances.

## Aurora and Atmospheric Chemistry

Energetic particle precipitation can alter upper-atmospheric chemistry.

It can produce compounds such as nitrogen oxides.

During strong events, these chemical changes can influence ozone chemistry at high altitudes.

## Energetic Particle Precipitation

High-energy particles can penetrate more deeply into the atmosphere than ordinary auroral electrons.

They can affect:

* Ionization

* Chemical reactions

* Atmospheric composition

## Aurora and Climate

Auroral light itself does not control Earth's climate.

Solar activity can influence the upper atmosphere and space environment, but ordinary auroral displays are not a major driver of surface climate.

## Aurora and Weather

Auroras do not cause ordinary weather such as:

* Rain

* Snow

* Storms

* Wind

These phenomena occur much lower in Earth's atmosphere.

## Can Aurora Harm Humans on the Ground?

The visible aurora itself is harmless to people on Earth's surface.

Earth's atmosphere provides strong protection from the energetic particles responsible for auroral emissions.

Associated severe space weather can affect technology, but observing aurora is generally safe.

## Common Misconceptions

### Aurora Is Caused Directly by Sunlight

Incorrect.

Auroras are produced primarily by energetic charged particles interacting with atmospheric gases.

### Aurora Is Caused Only by Solar Flares

Incorrect.

Auroral activity can be driven by several solar-wind structures, including:

* CMEs

* High-speed solar-wind streams

* CIRs

### Aurora Happens Only at the North Pole

Incorrect.

Auroras occur around both magnetic poles.

### Aurora Borealis and Aurora Australis Are Different Physical Phenomena

They are produced by essentially the same basic physical processes in opposite hemispheres.

### Every Solar Flare Produces Aurora

Incorrect.

The resulting space-weather effect depends on whether Earth encounters disturbed solar plasma and its magnetic orientation.

### Green Aurora Is Produced by Nitrogen

Green aurora is primarily associated with atomic oxygen.

### Red Aurora Always Means a More Dangerous Storm

Incorrect.

Color primarily depends on atmospheric species, altitude, and particle energies.

It is not a simple hazard scale.

### Auroras Occur Near the Ground

Incorrect.

Most auroras occur tens to hundreds of kilometers above Earth's surface.

### Aurora Makes Audible Noise Directly From the Sky

Ordinary sound cannot travel directly from auroral altitudes quickly enough to match visual changes.

Reported auroral sounds, where real, require different near-ground mechanisms.

### Aurora Is Earth's Atmosphere Burning

Incorrect.

Auroral light comes mainly from excited atoms and molecules emitting photons.

### The Magnetic Field Completely Blocks Solar Wind

Incorrect.

Earth's magnetosphere redirects much of the solar wind but also exchanges energy and particles with it.

Process that changes magnetic topology and releases magnetic energy.

Global index of geomagnetic activity ranging from roughly 0 to 9.

## Aurora Versus Related Phenomena

### Aurora Versus Airglow

Aurora:

Driven mainly by energetic charged particles.

Airglow:

Faint atmospheric emission caused by chemical and radiative processes.

### Aurora Versus Dayglow

Aurora:

Particle-driven.

Dayglow:

Primarily sunlight-driven atmospheric emission.

### Aurora Versus STEVE

Aurora:

Produced by particle precipitation.

STEVE:

Associated mainly with hot, fast ionospheric plasma and different emission physics.

### Aurora Versus Lightning

Aurora:

Upper-atmospheric plasma phenomenon.

Lightning:

Electrical discharge mainly within or between lower-atmospheric storm systems.

### Aurora Versus Solar Flare

Aurora:

Occurs in a planetary atmosphere.

Solar flare:

Occurs on the Sun.

A solar flare may accompany solar events that later influence auroral activity.

## Why Auroras Matter

Auroras are more than beautiful atmospheric displays.

They provide visible evidence of the interaction between:

* Sun

* Solar wind

* Earth's magnetic field

* Magnetosphere

* Ionosphere

* Atmosphere

Studying auroras helps scientists understand:

* Plasma physics

* Magnetic reconnection

* Particle acceleration

* Space weather

* Planetary magnetic fields

* Atmospheric chemistry

* Stellar-planet interactions

Auroras also provide a natural laboratory for studying physical processes that occur throughout the Universe.

## Summary

Auroras are atmospheric light displays produced when energetic charged particles interact with gases in the upper atmosphere.

On Earth, magnetic fields guide particles toward high-latitude auroral ovals.

Collisions excite oxygen and nitrogen, which emit light at characteristic wavelengths.

Atomic oxygen produces much of the familiar green and red auroral light, while nitrogen contributes blue, violet, and other colors.

Solar wind supplies energy to Earth's magnetosphere.

Magnetic reconnection, magnetotail dynamics, particle acceleration, and ionospheric currents control much of the resulting auroral activity.

Geomagnetic storms can expand the auroral oval and make auroras visible at unusually low latitudes.

Auroras also occur on other planets, including Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Mars, though their detailed mechanisms differ.

Auroras are therefore important both as spectacular natural phenomena and as diagnostic tools for understanding how magnetic fields, plasmas, atmospheres, and stellar winds interact.
