\# Planetary Volcanism



\## Overview



Planetary volcanism is the movement of molten or partially molten material from the interior of a planet, moon, dwarf planet, or other body toward or onto its surface.



On rocky worlds, volcanism usually involves magma made of molten silicate rock. On icy worlds, similar processes can involve water, ammonia, methane, or other volatile-rich materials and are called \[\[Cryovolcanism]].



Volcanism is one of the most important ways planetary interiors exchange heat and material with their surfaces. It influences crust formation, atmospheric evolution, tectonics, surface renewal, mineral composition, and potentially \[\[Planetary Habitability]].



\## Why Volcanism Happens



Volcanism occurs when material inside a planetary body melts and becomes buoyant enough to rise.



Melting can be produced by internal heat from radioactive decay, leftover heat from \[\[Planet Formation]], gravitational differentiation, mantle convection, decompression, large impacts, or tidal heating.



Once magma forms, it may move upward through fractures or weak zones in the crust.



If it reaches the surface, an eruption occurs.



If it cools underground, it forms intrusive igneous structures.



\## Magma



Magma is molten or partially molten rock beneath a planetary surface.



It usually contains a mixture of liquid rock, crystals, and dissolved gases.



When magma erupts onto the surface, it is called:



Lava



\## Lava



Lava is magma that has reached the surface.



Its temperature, composition, viscosity, and gas content determine how it flows and erupts.



Different planets can produce very different types of lava.



\## Partial Melting



Planetary mantles do not always melt completely.



Often only part of the rock melts.



This is called:



Partial melting



The melt can have a different chemical composition from the solid mantle material left behind.



This process contributes to \[\[Planetary Differentiation]].



\## Why Magma Rises



Magma is often less dense than the surrounding solid rock.



This buoyancy helps it rise.



Pressure from dissolved gases and stresses in the crust can also drive magma upward.



Fractures and faults can provide pathways toward the surface.



\## Magma Chambers



Magma can temporarily accumulate beneath the surface in regions called:



Magma chambers



These reservoirs can feed repeated eruptions.



Not every magma chamber produces an eruption.



Some cool and solidify underground.



\## Intrusive Volcanism



When magma solidifies beneath the surface, it forms intrusive bodies.



Examples include:



Dikes



Sills



Plutons



These structures can deform surrounding rock and influence tectonic activity.



\## Dikes



A dike is a sheet of magma that cuts across existing rock layers.



Dikes can extend for enormous distances.



On planets such as Mars and Venus, some long fracture systems may have formed above large dike intrusions.



\## Sills



A sill is a sheet of magma injected roughly parallel to existing layers.



Sills can spread laterally beneath the surface.



\## Volcanic Eruptions



An eruption occurs when magma or volcanic material reaches the surface.



Eruptions can be:



Effusive



Explosive



or intermediate between these styles.



The eruption style depends strongly on magma viscosity and gas content.



\## Effusive Eruptions



Effusive eruptions produce relatively fluid lava flows.



The lava spreads across the surface rather than fragmenting violently.



Basaltic volcanism is commonly effusive.



Large portions of the surfaces of Earth, Moon, Mars, Venus, and Mercury contain ancient basaltic lava plains.



\## Explosive Eruptions



Explosive eruptions occur when gases trapped in magma expand rapidly.



High-viscosity magma can prevent gases from escaping easily.



Pressure builds until fragmentation occurs.



Explosive eruptions produce material such as:



Ash



Blocks



Volcanic gases



Pyroclastic deposits



\## Volcanic Explosivity



The strength of an explosive eruption depends on several factors, including:



Magma composition



Viscosity



Gas abundance



Pressure



Vent geometry



Interaction with water or ice



\## Viscosity



Viscosity describes resistance to flow.



Low-viscosity magma flows easily.



High-viscosity magma moves more slowly and can trap gases more effectively.



Temperature and composition strongly affect viscosity.



\## Basaltic Magma



Basaltic magma is generally relatively low in silica and has low to moderate viscosity.



It can travel large distances before cooling.



Basaltic volcanism dominates many planetary surfaces.



\## Silica-Rich Magma



Magma containing more silica can be more viscous.



On Earth, silica-rich magma can produce explosive eruptions and steep volcanic structures.



Evidence for more evolved or silica-rich volcanic compositions has also been identified on some other worlds.



\## Shield Volcanoes



Shield volcanoes are broad volcanoes with gentle slopes.



They form mainly from repeated eruptions of low-viscosity lava.



The Hawaiian volcanoes on Earth are familiar examples.



Mars contains the largest known shield volcanoes in the Solar System.



\## Olympus Mons



\[\[Mars]] contains Olympus Mons, the largest known volcano in the Solar System.



It rises roughly 20 kilometers or more above surrounding plains depending on the reference level used and extends hundreds of kilometers across.



Its enormous size was made possible by several factors, including low Martian gravity and the absence of Earth-like plate tectonics moving the crust over a mantle hotspot.



\## Tharsis



The Tharsis region of Mars is an enormous volcanic province.



It contains several giant shield volcanoes, including:



Olympus Mons



Ascraeus Mons



Pavonis Mons



Arsia Mons



The development of Tharsis also caused major tectonic stresses across Mars.



\## Why Martian Volcanoes Became So Large



On Earth, tectonic plates move across many long-lived mantle melting regions.



This produces chains of volcanoes such as Hawaii.



Mars lacks modern global plate tectonics.



A volcanic source could therefore feed approximately the same region for very long periods, allowing extremely large volcanic mountains to develop.



Lower gravity also allows larger structures before gravitational stresses become limiting.



\## Martian Volcanism



Mars experienced extensive volcanism throughout much of its history.



Major volcanic provinces include:



Tharsis



Elysium



Ancient highland volcanic regions



Some Martian lava flows are geologically much younger than the oldest cratered terrains.



This suggests volcanic activity persisted for a very long period.



\## Elysium



Elysium is another major volcanic province on Mars.



It contains volcanoes and young volcanic plains.



The nearby Cerberus Fossae region contains fractures and evidence for relatively recent geological activity.



\## Cerberus Fossae



Cerberus Fossae includes large fissures cutting the Martian surface.



Some deposits nearby have been interpreted as evidence for geologically recent volcanic activity.



This region is also associated with marsquakes detected by the InSight mission.



\## Lava Tubes on Mars



Long basaltic lava flows can develop insulated channels beneath solidified surfaces.



These are called:



Lava tubes



Possible lava-tube caves on Mars are scientifically important because they could provide natural protection from radiation and temperature extremes for future exploration.



\## Lunar Volcanism



The \[\[Moon]] experienced extensive volcanism early in its history.



The dark lunar maria consist largely of ancient basaltic lava flows.



These lavas erupted into enormous impact basins.



\## Lunar Maria



The word maria means:



Seas



Early observers mistakenly thought the dark regions contained water.



They are actually large plains dominated by basaltic volcanic rocks.



Most major maria occur on the Moon's near side.



\## Why Lunar Maria Are Dark



Basaltic rocks contain minerals and compositions that reflect less visible light than the brighter anorthositic highlands.



The darker appearance is therefore mainly compositional.



\## Lunar Volcanic Timing



Most mare volcanism occurred billions of years ago.



However, sample-return missions such as Chang'e 5 have shown that volcanic activity continued in some regions until around two billion years ago.



This means the Moon remained volcanically active much longer than once assumed.



\## Lunar Rilles



The Moon contains long channels and valleys called:



Rilles



Some formed through volcanic processes.



Sinuous rilles are especially associated with ancient lava channels.



\## Hadley Rille



Hadley Rille was explored by Apollo 15 astronauts.



It is a famous example of a sinuous lunar rille likely formed by flowing lava or collapse of a lava channel system.



\## Lunar Lava Tubes



Some lunar lava flows may have formed lava tubes.



When the roof of a tube collapses, it can create openings known as:



Skylights



These features are of interest for both geology and possible future exploration.



\## Lunar Domes



The Moon contains small rounded volcanic structures called:



Lunar domes



They formed through eruption of relatively viscous lava.



\## Explosive Lunar Volcanism



Although most lunar volcanism was effusive, explosive eruptions also occurred.



Evidence includes deposits of:



Volcanic glass



Pyroclastic material



Apollo 17 astronauts discovered orange volcanic glass near Taurus-Littrow.



\## Venusian Volcanism



\[\[Venus]] has one of the most volcanically modified surfaces in the Solar System.



Thousands of volcanic structures have been identified.



These include:



Shield volcanoes



Lava plains



Domes



Coronae



Volcanic rises



Long lava flows



\## Venusian Surface



Much of Venus's surface appears to have been resurfaced by volcanic and tectonic activity.



Its relatively low density of impact craters indicates that much of the visible surface is geologically younger than the oldest terrains on bodies such as Mercury and the Moon.



\## Active Volcanism on Venus



Observations from spacecraft have provided evidence consistent with geologically recent and probably ongoing volcanic activity.



Changes observed in radar images of some volcanic features have strengthened the case that Venus remains volcanically active.



The exact global rate of present-day volcanism is still being investigated.



\## Maat Mons



Maat Mons is one of the large volcanoes on Venus.



Radar observations of this region have been studied for possible evidence of recent volcanic changes.



\## Venusian Lava Flows



Venus contains extremely long lava flows.



Its high surface temperature means lava cools differently than it does on Earth.



However, the exact properties of Venusian lava depend on composition and eruption conditions.



\## Pancake Domes



Venus contains unusual broad, flat-topped volcanic domes often called:



Pancake domes



They may have formed from relatively viscous lava.



\## Coronae and Volcanism



Many Venusian coronae contain volcanic flows and tectonic fractures.



They may develop above mantle upwellings.



This demonstrates the close relationship between \[\[Planetary Tectonics]] and volcanism.



\## Mercury Volcanism



\[\[Mercury]] experienced widespread ancient volcanism.



Large smooth plains cover extensive portions of its surface.



MESSENGER observations showed that volcanism played a major role in Mercury's geological evolution.



\## Mercury Smooth Plains



Mercury's smooth plains are largely interpreted as volcanic deposits.



Some resemble lunar maria, although Mercury's overall geological history is different.



\## Explosive Volcanism on Mercury



MESSENGER discovered irregular depressions surrounded by bright deposits that provide evidence for explosive volcanic eruptions.



This was surprising because Mercury is highly depleted in some volatile elements, but it still retained enough volatile material to drive explosive activity.



\## Mercury Hollows



Mercury contains unusual bright, shallow depressions called:



Hollows



They may form through loss of volatile-bearing material from the surface.



Although not simply volcanic vents, they show that volatile-related processes remain important in Mercury's geology.



\## Earth Volcanism



\[\[Earth]] is currently one of the most volcanically active rocky planets.



Volcanism occurs in several major tectonic settings.



These include:



Mid-ocean ridges



Subduction zones



Continental rifts



Hotspots



\## Mid-Ocean Ridge Volcanism



Most volcanic material produced on Earth forms underwater at mid-ocean ridges.



As tectonic plates separate, mantle material rises.



The decrease in pressure allows partial melting.



This is called:



Decompression melting



\## Decompression Melting



Hot mantle rock can remain solid at high pressure.



If it rises, pressure decreases.



The rock can partially melt without requiring a large temperature increase.



This is one of the most important magma-generation mechanisms on Earth.



\## Subduction Zone Volcanism



At subduction zones, water and other volatiles released from the descending slab enter the overlying mantle.



These volatiles lower the melting temperature of mantle rock.



Magma forms and can rise to create volcanic arcs.



\## Flux Melting



Melting promoted by the addition of water or other volatile components is called:



Flux melting



It is a major process in subduction zones.



\## Hotspot Volcanism



Some volcanoes form above long-lived mantle upwellings or other deep thermal anomalies.



These are commonly called:



Hotspots



Hawaii is a famous example.



\## Mantle Plumes



Some hotspots are associated with:



Mantle plumes



These are proposed columns of unusually hot material rising through the mantle.



The details of plume dynamics remain an active area of geophysical research.



\## Volcanic Arcs



Chains of volcanoes above subduction zones are called:



Volcanic arcs



Examples include:



Andes



Cascades



Japan



Aleutian Islands



\## Island Arcs



When subduction occurs between oceanic plates, volcanic island chains can develop.



These are called:



Island arcs



\## Continental Volcanic Arcs



When oceanic lithosphere subducts beneath continental crust, volcanoes can form on the continent.



The Andes are a major example.



\## Rift Volcanism



Continental rifting stretches and thins the lithosphere.



Mantle upwelling and decompression melting can produce extensive volcanism.



The East African Rift is an important modern example.



\## Flood Basalts



Extremely large volumes of basalt can erupt over geologically short intervals.



These events produce:



Flood basalt provinces



Examples on Earth include the Deccan Traps and Siberian Traps.



Such eruptions can have major environmental consequences.



\## Large Igneous Provinces



A large region produced by enormous volumes of magma is called a:



Large Igneous Province



or:



LIP



Large igneous provinces can occur on Earth and other planets.



\## Deccan Traps



The Deccan Traps formed through enormous volcanic eruptions near the end of the Cretaceous Period.



Their environmental effects occurred around the same broad time as the Chicxulub impact.



Scientists continue studying how these events contributed to environmental change.



\## Siberian Traps



The Siberian Traps formed around the time of the end-Permian mass extinction.



Large emissions of greenhouse gases and other volcanic products likely contributed to severe climate and environmental disruption.



\## Volcanic Gases



Volcanoes release gases from planetary interiors.



Common terrestrial volcanic gases include:



Water vapor



Carbon dioxide



Sulfur dioxide



Hydrogen sulfide



Other gases



Volcanic gases can have major atmospheric and climatic effects.



\## Outgassing



The release of gases from the interior is called:



Outgassing



Volcanic outgassing helped contribute to the development of secondary atmospheres on rocky planets.



\## Atmosphere Formation



Early volcanic activity may have supplied substantial quantities of:



Water vapor



Carbon dioxide



Nitrogen-bearing compounds



Sulfur compounds



to planetary atmospheres.



Atmospheric evolution later depends on escape, chemistry, impacts, condensation, and biological processes where applicable.



\## Volcanism and Oceans



Volcanic outgassing may have contributed water vapor to early planetary atmospheres.



If conditions allowed cooling and condensation, this could contribute to oceans.



Water can also be delivered by asteroids and other bodies, so planetary water usually has multiple possible sources.



\## Volcanic Carbon Cycle



On Earth, volcanoes return carbon from the interior to the atmosphere and oceans.



Weathering and tectonic processes can eventually move some of that carbon back into the crust and mantle.



This is part of Earth's long-term carbon cycle.



\## Volcanism and Climate



Volcanoes can affect climate in different ways.



Large explosive eruptions can inject sulfur compounds into the stratosphere.



These form aerosols that reflect sunlight and can temporarily cool Earth's surface.



Long-term volcanic carbon dioxide emissions can contribute to warming over geological timescales.



\## Volcanic Aerosols



Sulfur dioxide can react in the atmosphere to form sulfate aerosols.



Large stratospheric aerosol clouds can persist for months or years.



They reduce the amount of sunlight reaching the surface.



\## Ash



Volcanic ash consists of fragmented volcanic material.



It is not the same as ordinary combustion ash.



Particles can include:



Glass



Crystals



Rock fragments



Ash can damage aircraft engines, affect breathing, contaminate water supplies, and disrupt infrastructure.



\## Pyroclastic Flows



Pyroclastic flows are fast-moving mixtures of:



Hot gas



Ash



Rock fragments



They are among the most dangerous volcanic phenomena on Earth.



\## Lava Flows



Lava flows usually move much more slowly than pyroclastic flows, although they can still destroy buildings, roads, and landscapes.



Flow speed depends on:



Viscosity



Slope



Eruption rate



Cooling



\## Lava Fountains



Low-viscosity magma can produce jets of molten rock known as:



Lava fountains



They are common in some basaltic eruptions.



\## Calderas



A caldera is a large volcanic depression.



It often forms when a magma reservoir is partly emptied and the overlying ground collapses.



Calderas are different from ordinary impact craters.



\## Supereruptions



Very large explosive eruptions are sometimes informally called:



Supereruptions



They can eject enormous volumes of material.



The term is used mainly for terrestrial volcanology and does not represent a completely separate physical type of volcano.



\## Volcanic Explosivity Index



On Earth, explosive eruptions are sometimes classified using the:



Volcanic Explosivity Index



or:



VEI



It considers factors such as erupted volume and eruption-column height.



It is mainly designed for explosive terrestrial eruptions and is not a universal scale for all planetary volcanism.



\## Io



\[\[Io]], a moon of Jupiter, is the most volcanically active known world in the Solar System.



Its volcanism is powered primarily by:



Tidal heating



rather than long-lived radioactive heat alone.



\## Why Io Is So Active



Io moves in a slightly eccentric orbit around Jupiter.



Jupiter's enormous gravity repeatedly changes the tidal force acting on Io.



Io flexes continuously.



Internal friction converts part of this mechanical deformation into heat.



This generates widespread melting.



\## Laplace Resonance



Io's orbital eccentricity is maintained partly by its orbital resonance with:



\[\[Europa]]



and:



\[\[Ganymede]]



The three moons participate in the Laplace resonance.



This prevents Io's orbit from becoming completely circular and allows ongoing tidal heating.



\## Io Volcanoes



Io contains hundreds of volcanic centers.



Its eruptions include:



Lava flows



Volcanic plumes



Lava lakes



Sulfur-rich deposits



Silicate volcanism



\## Loki Patera



Loki Patera is one of the largest and most powerful volcanic features on Io.



It contains a large active lava lake or lava-lake-like volcanic system.



\## Io's Volcanic Plumes



Some Io eruptions send plumes hundreds of kilometers above the surface.



Material falls back and produces large surface deposits.



Some escaping particles also contribute to the plasma environment around Jupiter.



\## Io Plasma Torus



Material released from Io becomes ionized and forms a ring of plasma around Jupiter called:



The Io plasma torus



This strongly affects Jupiter's magnetosphere.



\## Cryovolcanism



Not all volcanism involves molten rock.



On icy worlds, volatile-rich liquids or slurries can erupt.



This is called:



\[\[Cryovolcanism]]



Possible materials include:



Water



Ammonia-rich mixtures



Methane-related compounds



Salty water



\## Enceladus



\[\[Enceladus]] is one of the clearest examples of active cryovolcanism.



Jets of water vapor and ice particles emerge from fractures near its south pole.



These fractures are known as:



Tiger stripes



\## Enceladus Plumes



The plumes contain:



Water vapor



Ice grains



Salts



Organic molecules



Other compounds



Their composition indicates communication with a subsurface liquid-water environment.



\## Enceladus and Saturn's E Ring



Material escaping Enceladus contributes particles to Saturn's:



E ring



This demonstrates that volcanic or cryovolcanic activity on a moon can influence an entire planetary system.



\## Europa



\[\[Europa]] may experience cryovolcanic or plume activity.



Evidence for active water-vapor plumes has been investigated, but the frequency and exact nature of these events remain uncertain.



Its fractured surface strongly indicates active or recent ice-shell processes.



\## Titan



\[\[Titan]] has several landforms that have been proposed as possible cryovolcanic features.



Examples include structures around Sotra Patera.



However, the extent of active cryovolcanism on Titan remains uncertain.



\## Triton



\[\[Triton]], Neptune's largest moon, showed active plume-like eruptions when Voyager 2 flew past in 1989.



Dark material was observed rising above the surface.



These eruptions may have been driven partly by solar heating of volatile-rich surface layers rather than deep internal cryovolcanism alone.



\## Pluto



\[\[Pluto]] contains large mountains interpreted as possible cryovolcanic structures.



Examples include:



Wright Mons



Piccard Mons



Their morphology suggests eruption of viscous icy material, although their detailed history remains under study.



\## Ceres



\[\[Ceres]] contains evidence for cryovolcanism and brine-related geological activity.



Ahuna Mons is interpreted as a cryovolcanic dome.



Bright deposits in Occator Crater are associated with salts and brines.



\## Ahuna Mons



Ahuna Mons is an isolated mountain on Ceres.



Its shape and composition indicate that it likely formed through upward movement and eruption of material from Ceres's interior.



\## Volcanism on Asteroids



Large differentiated asteroids and early planetesimals could experience volcanism if they melted sufficiently.



Vesta is a major example of an early differentiated body with a basaltic crust.



\## Vesta



\[\[Vesta]] experienced extensive melting early in Solar System history.



Its basaltic surface and linked HED meteorites show that volcanic and intrusive processes occurred on a relatively small protoplanet.



\## Ancient Planetesimal Volcanism



Short-lived radioactive isotopes such as aluminum-26 heated early planetesimals.



Some became hot enough for:



Core formation



Mantle melting



Basaltic volcanism



Many were later destroyed by collisions.



\## Volcanism and Planet Size



Large planets generally retain internal heat longer than small bodies because they have a smaller surface-area-to-volume ratio.



However, size alone does not determine volcanic activity.



Tidal heating can keep small moons active.



\## Cooling of Small Bodies



Small worlds lose internal heat relatively quickly.



For this reason, many small rocky bodies became volcanically inactive early.



The Moon is a major example of a world whose large-scale volcanism declined as its interior cooled.



\## Radioactive Heating



Decay of radioactive elements generates heat inside rocky planets.



Important long-lived isotopes include those of:



Uranium



Thorium



Potassium



This heat contributes to mantle convection and melting.



\## Primordial Heat



Heat remaining from planetary formation is called:



Primordial heat



It includes energy from accretion, impacts, compression, and differentiation.



This energy gradually escapes over geological time.



\## Tidal Heating



Tidal heating occurs when changing gravitational forces repeatedly deform a planetary body.



This is especially important for moons.



Major examples include:



Io



Europa



Enceladus



\## Impact-Generated Volcanism



Large impacts generate enormous heat.



They can melt crust and mantle material.



Impact events may therefore trigger temporary magmatic activity.



However, not every impact basin is volcanic in origin.



\## Impact Melts



Rock melted directly by impact energy forms:



Impact melt



This is different from magma generated by normal mantle melting, even though the resulting rock may also crystallize from a melt.



\## Volcanic Versus Impact Craters



Volcanic calderas and impact craters can sometimes appear superficially similar.



Impact craters often contain diagnostic structures such as:



Raised rims



Ejecta



Central peaks



Shock features



Volcanic calderas are related to eruption and subsurface magma withdrawal.



\## Volcanic Resurfacing



Lava can cover older terrain.



This process is called:



Volcanic resurfacing



It can bury:



Impact craters



Faults



Sediments



Older lava flows



The density of surviving craters can therefore provide clues about when resurfacing occurred.



\## Crater Counting



Planetary scientists often estimate relative surface ages by counting impact craters.



A surface with many craters is generally older than a nearby surface with fewer craters, assuming comparable impact histories.



Young lava flows can therefore be identified partly because they cover older cratered terrain.



\## Volcanic Stratigraphy



By observing which lava flows cover other features, scientists can establish a sequence of volcanic events.



This uses principles such as:



Superposition



Cross-cutting relationships



\## Superposition



If one lava flow lies on top of another, the upper flow is generally younger.



This simple principle is extremely important in planetary geological mapping.



\## Remote Detection of Volcanism



Scientists identify volcanism on other worlds using:



Images



Topography



Radar



Thermal measurements



Spectroscopy



Gravity data



Atmospheric gases



Surface changes over time



\## Thermal Anomalies



Active lava is much hotter than surrounding terrain.



Infrared instruments can detect thermal anomalies associated with eruptions.



This is commonly used to monitor Io and Earth.



\## Spectroscopy



Spectroscopy reveals the chemical composition of volcanic surfaces and gases.



It can identify minerals, sulfur compounds, silicates, and other materials.



\## Radar



Radar is especially valuable for studying Venus.



Its thick clouds block visible-light observations of the surface.



Radar can map volcanoes, lava flows, fractures, and possible changes between observations.



\## Seismology



Movement of magma can create seismic signals.



On Earth, earthquake patterns often help monitor volcanoes.



Future seismic networks on other planets could identify subsurface magma movement.



\## Ground Deformation



Rising magma can inflate the surface.



Satellite measurements on Earth can detect changes of only centimeters or less.



Such deformation can provide warning of volcanic unrest.



\## Atmospheric Gas Detection



Changes in gases such as sulfur dioxide can indicate volcanic activity.



Remote detection of volcanic gases may eventually help identify active volcanism on exoplanets.



\## Volcanism and Magnetic Fields



Volcanic rocks can preserve information about ancient magnetic fields.



When lava cools, magnetic minerals can record the direction and strength of the surrounding field.



This process is important in studies of Earth's magnetic reversals and ancient magnetic fields on Mars and the Moon.



\## Paleomagnetism



The study of magnetic information preserved in rocks is called:



Paleomagnetism



It helps reconstruct:



Past magnetic fields



Plate movement



Dynamo histories



\## Mars Crustal Magnetism



Ancient Martian crust contains strong magnetic anomalies.



Some volcanic rocks may have cooled while Mars still possessed an active global magnetic dynamo.



This provides clues about the timing of early Martian geology.



\## Volcanism and Tectonics



Volcanism and \[\[Planetary Tectonics]] are closely linked.



Tectonic extension can create fractures through which magma rises.



Volcanic intrusions can create new stresses and faults.



Large volcanoes can bend the lithosphere under their weight.



\## Volcanism and Differentiation



\[\[Planetary Differentiation]] produces chemically distinct cores, mantles, and crusts.



Partial melting of differentiated mantle material generates magma.



Volcanism then transfers this material toward the surface.



\## Volcanism and Atmospheres



Volcanic outgassing can create or replenish atmospheres.



This is particularly important for planets that have lost their original hydrogen-rich atmospheres.



Venus, Earth, and Mars all experienced major volcanic contributions to atmospheric evolution.



\## Volcanism and Habitability



Volcanism can support habitability by:



Providing atmospheric gases



Recycling nutrients



Supplying chemical energy



Maintaining geological activity



Creating hydrothermal environments



However, extreme volcanism can also make environments hostile.



\## Hydrothermal Systems



When heat from magma interacts with water, it can create:



Hydrothermal systems



These environments contain strong chemical gradients and can support life on Earth.



They are therefore important in astrobiology.



\## Seafloor Hydrothermal Vents



Earth's ocean floor contains hydrothermal vents heated by underlying magma.



These environments support ecosystems that do not depend directly on sunlight.



This demonstrates that chemical energy from geology can sustain biological communities.



\## Ocean Worlds and Hydrothermal Activity



If icy moons such as Europa or Enceladus contain rocky seafloors interacting with liquid water, hydrothermal processes could occur.



Such environments are important targets in the search for extraterrestrial habitability.



\## Volcanic Nutrients



Volcanic processes release and redistribute elements such as:



Sulfur



Iron



Phosphorus



Carbon



These can influence biological productivity.



\## Excessive Volcanism



Very intense volcanism can produce:



Toxic gases



Acidic conditions



Rapid climate change



Surface burial



High temperatures



Volcanism therefore has both beneficial and destructive potential for habitability.



\## Volcanism and Mass Extinctions



Several large terrestrial extinction events occurred near periods of enormous flood-basalt volcanism.



Volcanic greenhouse gases, sulfur emissions, ocean acidification, and other environmental changes may have contributed.



\## Planetary Volcanism and Climate Feedbacks



Long-term volcanism supplies atmospheric carbon dioxide.



On Earth, chemical weathering removes carbon dioxide.



The balance contributes to long-term climate regulation.



A planet without efficient carbon removal could experience very different atmospheric evolution.



\## Venus and Runaway Greenhouse



Venus's dense carbon dioxide atmosphere is not simply the result of volcanoes alone.



Volcanic outgassing contributed gases, but atmospheric evolution also involved solar heating, water loss, chemical processes, and lack of an Earth-like long-term carbon cycle.



\## Mars Atmospheric Evolution



Ancient Martian volcanism released gases into the atmosphere.



As Mars lost much of its atmosphere over time, volcanic activity may have periodically replenished some gases.



Volcanic provinces may also have temporarily affected Martian climate.



\## Volcanic Caves



Lava tubes can create caves after lava drains away.



These environments are important because they may preserve:



Ice



Minerals



Organic material



Protected habitats



\## Astrobiological Importance of Lava Tubes



On Mars and the Moon, lava tubes could shield their interiors from:



Radiation



Micrometeorites



Extreme temperature changes



This makes them important for future exploration and possible searches for preserved biosignatures on Mars.



\## Exoplanet Volcanism



Volcanism is expected on many rocky \[\[Exoplanets]].



The amount of activity depends on:



Planet mass



Age



Interior composition



Radioactive elements



Tidal forces



Orbital eccentricity



\## Super-Earth Volcanism



Super-Earths may retain large amounts of internal heat because of their size.



However, high pressure inside their mantles can alter melting and convection.



Therefore, greater planetary mass does not automatically mean stronger surface volcanism.



\## Tidally Heated Exoplanets



A close-in planet on an eccentric orbit can experience powerful tidal heating.



Some exoplanets may therefore resemble an extreme version of Io.



Such worlds could undergo continuous global volcanism.



\## Lava Worlds



Some rocky exoplanets orbit so close to their stars that parts of their surfaces may be molten.



These are sometimes called:



Lava worlds



Their geological behavior differs greatly from ordinary volcanic planets because stellar heating itself can melt the surface.



\## Detecting Exoplanet Volcanism



Possible future indicators of exoplanet volcanism include:



Atmospheric sulfur gases



Changing thermal emission



Transient aerosols



Surface hot spots



Repeated atmospheric chemical changes



These observations are extremely challenging.



\## Stellar Activity Versus Volcanic Signals



When studying exoplanets, atmospheric changes caused by stellar activity can sometimes mimic or obscure volcanic signals.



Careful repeated observations are therefore necessary.



\## Volcanic Lightning



Explosive eruptions on Earth can generate electrical discharges inside ash clouds.



This is known as:



Volcanic lightning



It results from charge separation among particles.



Similar processes may be possible wherever explosive particle-rich eruptions occur.



\## Volcanic Glass



Rapidly cooled lava can form glass.



Volcanic glass occurs on:



Earth



Moon



and probably other volcanic worlds.



It can preserve information about eruption conditions and volatile content.



\## Pillow Lava



When basaltic lava erupts underwater on Earth, rapid cooling can form rounded structures called:



Pillow lavas



These provide evidence for underwater volcanic activity.



\## Submarine Volcanism



Much of Earth's volcanism occurs beneath the oceans.



Because it is hidden from ordinary view, the total amount of terrestrial volcanic activity is much larger than surface-land observations alone suggest.



\## Fissure Eruptions



Magma can erupt from long cracks instead of a central volcanic cone.



These are called:



Fissure eruptions



They can produce enormous lava plains.



\## Lava Plains



Repeated fluid lava eruptions can create broad, relatively flat volcanic surfaces.



Examples occur on:



Moon



Mercury



Mars



Venus



Earth



\## Volcanic Provinces



A volcanic province is a large region containing numerous related volcanic structures or deposits.



Examples include:



Tharsis on Mars



Elysium on Mars



Large volcanic rises on Venus



Large igneous provinces on Earth



\## Volcanic Hotspots



A hotspot is a long-lived region of enhanced volcanism that is not necessarily located at a plate boundary.



On Earth, hotspots can produce chains of volcanoes as tectonic plates move.



On stagnant-lid worlds, the volcanic center may remain concentrated in one region for very long periods.



\## Volcanic Loading



Large volcanoes can deform the crust beneath them.



Their weight produces:



Flexure



Faulting



Subsidence



This connects volcanism with lithospheric strength.



\## Caldera Collapse



When a large magma reservoir loses material during an eruption, the roof can collapse.



This produces a caldera.



Calderas can later contain:



Lava lakes



New volcanic domes



Lakes



Additional vents



\## Lava Lakes



Some volcanic systems maintain exposed bodies of molten lava.



These are called:



Lava lakes



Examples occur on Earth and Io.



\## Volcanic Plumes



A volcanic plume is a column or cloud of erupted material rising above a vent.



On Earth, gas and ash interact with the atmosphere.



On nearly airless Io, volcanic material follows ballistic trajectories before falling back to the surface.



\## Gravity and Eruption Behavior



Gravity strongly affects volcanic landforms.



Low gravity allows:



Ejected material to travel farther



Lava flows to spread differently



Volcanic mountains to grow taller before structural failure



This helps explain differences between Earth and Mars.



\## Atmospheric Pressure



Atmospheric pressure also influences eruptions.



Low surface pressure allows gases to expand more strongly.



This can affect magma fragmentation and eruption style.



Mars's very thin atmosphere therefore creates eruption conditions different from those on Earth or Venus.



\## Venus Surface Pressure



Venus has extremely high surface atmospheric pressure.



This suppresses gas expansion compared with Earth.



As a result, explosive eruption behavior can differ greatly, although explosive volcanism may still occur under suitable conditions.



\## Volcanic Weathering



Fresh volcanic rock reacts with:



Water



Atmospheric gases



Temperature cycles



On Earth, volcanic weathering contributes to the carbon cycle.



On airless bodies, space weathering modifies volcanic surfaces instead.



\## Space Weathering of Lava



On the Moon and Mercury, solar wind and micrometeorite impacts gradually alter exposed volcanic surfaces.



This changes their:



Color



Reflectance



Mineralogy signatures



\## Volcanic Ages



Scientists estimate volcanic ages using several techniques.



On Earth, radioactive dating of rocks can provide absolute ages.



On other worlds, crater counting and stratigraphic relationships are commonly used.



Sample return provides the strongest absolute calibration.



\## Radiometric Dating



Radioactive isotopes decay at predictable rates.



By measuring parent and daughter isotopes in volcanic rocks, scientists can determine when minerals crystallized.



Apollo and Chang'e samples provide crucial absolute dates for lunar volcanism.



\## Volcanic Monitoring



On Earth, active volcanoes are monitored using:



Seismometers



GPS



Satellite radar



Gas measurements



Thermal imaging



Ground observations



No single signal can predict every eruption perfectly.



\## Common Misconceptions



\### Volcanoes Exist Only on Earth



Incorrect. Volcanic landforms occur on Mercury, Venus, the Moon, Mars, Io, and many other worlds.



\### Every Volcano Erupts Explosively



Incorrect. Many eruptions are primarily effusive and produce flowing lava.



\### Mars Is Completely Geologically Dead



Incorrect. Most Martian volcanism is ancient, but some surfaces are geologically young, and Mars remains seismically active.



\### The Moon Never Had Volcanoes



Incorrect. The lunar maria are dominated by enormous ancient basaltic lava flows.



\### Io Is Hot Because It Is Close to the Sun



Incorrect. Io's intense volcanic activity is driven mainly by tidal heating from Jupiter.



\### Cryovolcanoes Erupt Molten Rock



Incorrect. Cryovolcanism involves volatile-rich materials such as water, brines, or other ices.



\### Venus's Volcanoes Are All Extinct



Evidence indicates geologically recent and likely ongoing volcanic activity on Venus.



\### Large Volcanoes Require Plate Tectonics



Incorrect. Mars contains enormous volcanoes despite lacking present Earth-like plate tectonics.



\### A Volcanic Planet Is Automatically Habitable



Incorrect. Volcanism can support useful geochemical cycles but can also create extreme and hostile environments.



\## Important Terminology



\*\*Volcanism:\*\* Movement and eruption of molten or volatile-rich interior material.



\*\*Magma:\*\* Molten or partially molten rock beneath a surface.



\*\*Lava:\*\* Magma erupted onto a surface.



\*\*Partial Melting:\*\* Melting of only part of a rock.



\*\*Effusive Eruption:\*\* Eruption dominated by flowing lava.



\*\*Explosive Eruption:\*\* Eruption dominated by fragmentation and rapid gas expansion.



\*\*Shield Volcano:\*\* Broad low-slope volcano built mainly by fluid lava.



\*\*Caldera:\*\* Large volcanic depression often formed by collapse above a magma reservoir.



\*\*Dike:\*\* Sheet-like magma intrusion cutting across surrounding material.



\*\*Lava Tube:\*\* Tunnel formed within a lava flow.



\*\*Pyroclastic Material:\*\* Fragmented material produced during explosive volcanism.



\*\*Cryovolcanism:\*\* Volcanic-like eruption of volatile-rich material on cold worlds.



\*\*Outgassing:\*\* Release of gases from a planetary interior.



\*\*Decompression Melting:\*\* Melting caused by decreasing pressure on rising hot material.



\*\*Tidal Heating:\*\* Internal heating caused by repeated gravitational deformation.



\## Simplified Rocky-Planet Volcanic Process



Heat inside a rocky planet causes part of the mantle or crust to melt. The resulting magma is commonly less dense than surrounding rock and begins moving upward.



Magma may accumulate beneath the crust, intrude into fractures, or erupt at the surface.



Low-viscosity magma can spread as broad lava flows, while gas-rich or high-viscosity magma can erupt explosively.



The lava cools into new crust, while gases released from the magma enter the atmosphere where one exists.



\## Simplified Tidal Volcanism on Io



Io follows a slightly eccentric orbit around Jupiter.



Jupiter's gravitational pull changes as Io moves through its orbit.



The moon repeatedly flexes.



Internal friction generates enormous heat.



Rock melts inside Io.



Magma rises through the crust and erupts.



Orbital resonance with Europa and Ganymede helps maintain Io's eccentricity, allowing the heating process to continue.



\## Simplified Cryovolcanic Process



An icy moon contains internal liquid water or volatile-rich material.



Internal or tidal heat keeps part of this material mobile.



Pressure builds or fractures open in the outer ice shell.



Liquid or vapor moves upward.



It erupts as plumes, ice particles, vapor, or viscous icy material.



This occurs dramatically on Enceladus.



\## Scientific Importance



Planetary volcanism provides direct evidence of internal heat and geological activity.



Volcanic surfaces reveal how planetary mantles melt, how crust forms, and how heat escapes from planetary interiors.



Volcanic gases influence atmospheres and climate, while hydrothermal systems can provide chemical energy relevant to life.



Comparing volcanism on Earth, Venus, Mars, Mercury, the Moon, Io, Enceladus, Pluto, and Ceres shows that geological activity can be powered by many different energy sources and can occur on both rocky and icy worlds.



\## Open Questions



Scientists continue investigating how volcanically active Venus is today, when the most recent eruptions occurred on Mars and the Moon, and whether magma remains present beneath some young Martian volcanic regions.



Major questions also concern the depth and composition of Io's magma reservoirs, how Enceladus transports ocean material through its ice shell, whether Europa has active cryovolcanic plumes, and how common cryovolcanism is on dwarf planets.



For exoplanets, researchers want to determine whether volcanic gases can be detected remotely, how volcanism changes on super-Earths, and whether intense tidal heating commonly creates Io-like worlds.



\## Summary



Planetary volcanism occurs when heat generates molten or mobile material inside a planetary body and that material rises toward the surface.



Rocky planets commonly produce silicate magma and lava, while cold icy worlds can experience \[\[Cryovolcanism]] involving water and other volatile-rich materials.



Earth's volcanism is strongly connected with plate tectonics. Venus has widespread volcanic terrain and evidence for ongoing geological activity. Mars contains enormous shield volcanoes such as Olympus Mons, while lunar maria preserve vast ancient basaltic eruptions. Mercury also experienced extensive volcanic activity.



Io is the most volcanically active known world because of intense tidal heating, while Enceladus actively erupts water-rich plumes from a subsurface ocean.



Volcanism transfers heat and chemicals from planetary interiors to their surfaces. It contributes to crust formation, atmospheric outgassing, tectonic deformation, climate evolution, and potentially habitable hydrothermal environments.



For these reasons, volcanism is one of the most important processes connecting \[\[Planetary Interiors]], \[\[Planetary Differentiation]], \[\[Planetary Tectonics]], surface geology, atmospheres, and \[\[Planetary Habitability]].
