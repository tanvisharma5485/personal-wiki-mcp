\# Phobos



\## Overview



Phobos is the larger and innermost of the two natural satellites of \[\[Mars]]. The other Martian moon is \[\[Deimos]].



Phobos is a small, irregularly shaped body with a heavily cratered surface. It orbits extremely close to Mars, much closer to its planet than Earth's Moon orbits Earth.



Its orbit is slowly shrinking because of tidal interactions with Mars. Over very long timescales, Phobos is expected either to break apart and contribute to a temporary ring around Mars or eventually collide with the planet.



Phobos is important for understanding the origin of the Martian moon system, tidal evolution, small-body geology, planetary rings, impact processes, and the history of Mars.



\## Size, Shape and Physical Properties



Phobos is approximately:



27 × 22 × 18 kilometers



in overall dimensions.



It is not massive enough for gravity to pull it into a sphere, so it has an irregular shape.



Its mean radius is about 11 kilometers.



Phobos has very weak surface gravity. A person standing on its surface would weigh only a tiny fraction of their weight on Earth.



Its escape velocity is only around:



11 meters per second



This extremely low gravity strongly affects the behavior of dust, impact ejecta, and surface material.



Phobos has a relatively low bulk density compared with solid rock. This suggests that its interior may contain significant porosity rather than being one fully solid monolithic object.



Its internal structure remains uncertain and could include fractures, voids, rubble-like material, or a combination of more coherent and fragmented regions.



\## Orbit Around Mars



Phobos orbits Mars at an altitude of only about:



6,000 kilometers



above the Martian surface.



Its distance from the center of Mars is roughly:



9,400 kilometers.



This makes Phobos one of the closest-orbiting natural satellites relative to the surface of its parent planet.



It completes one orbit in approximately:



7 hours 39 minutes



which is much shorter than Mars's rotation period of about 24.6 hours.



Because Phobos moves around Mars faster than Mars rotates, it has unusual apparent motion when seen from the Martian surface.



\## Appearance From Mars



For an observer standing on Mars, Phobos would rise in the west and set in the east.



This is the opposite of the usual apparent daily motion of the Sun and most celestial objects.



The reason is that Phobos orbits Mars faster than Mars rotates.



It can cross the Martian sky in only a few hours.



Because its orbital period is less than one Martian day, Phobos can rise and set more than once during a single Martian day from suitable locations.



\## Synchronous Orbit Boundary



Phobos lies inside Mars's synchronous-orbit radius.



The synchronous radius is where a satellite's orbital period would match the planet's rotation period.



A satellite outside this boundary generally experiences tidal evolution different from a satellite inside it.



Phobos is inside the synchronous radius, so tidal interactions cause it to gradually spiral inward.



\[\[Deimos]], by contrast, orbits outside the synchronous radius and is slowly moving outward.



\## Tidal Evolution



Mars raises tides on Phobos, and Phobos also raises small tidal distortions on Mars.



Because Phobos orbits faster than Mars rotates, tidal interactions transfer orbital angular momentum away from Phobos.



Its orbit therefore gradually decreases in radius.



Phobos is currently moving toward Mars by roughly:



1.8 centimeters per year



The precise value can vary slightly among measurements and models.



This inward migration is one of the most important long-term features of the Phobos system.



\## Future of Phobos



Phobos cannot remain in its current orbit forever.



As it moves closer to Mars, Martian tidal forces become stronger.



Eventually one of two broad outcomes is expected.



If Phobos is structurally weak, tidal forces may pull it apart before impact.



The resulting debris could form a temporary ring around Mars.



If it remains sufficiently coherent, it could survive longer and eventually impact Mars.



Current estimates generally place the final disruption or collision tens of millions of years in the future.



\## Roche Limit



The Roche limit is the approximate distance within which tidal forces from a planet can overcome the self-gravity holding a satellite together.



The exact Roche limit depends on the satellite's:



Density



Internal strength



Structure



Rigidity



Phobos's future therefore depends strongly on whether it behaves as a cohesive body or a weak rubble pile.



A fragmented Phobos could gradually disintegrate as it enters a region where tidal stress becomes stronger than its ability to remain intact.



\## Possible Martian Ring



If Phobos is tidally disrupted, much of its material may spread into orbit around Mars.



This could create a planetary ring.



Some debris would gradually fall toward Mars while other material could temporarily remain in orbit.



Mars may therefore possess a ring in the distant future.



Some models have also proposed that Mars may have experienced previous cycles involving rings and moons, although the details remain hypothetical.



\## Stickney Crater



The most prominent feature on Phobos is:



Stickney Crater



Stickney is approximately:



9 kilometers



across.



This is enormous compared with Phobos itself.



The crater covers a significant fraction of one side of the moon.



It was named after Chloe Angeline Stickney Hall, the wife of astronomer Asaph Hall, who discovered Phobos and Deimos.



\## Stickney Impact



The impact that created Stickney released a large amount of energy.



Because Phobos is so small, a somewhat larger impact might have disrupted the entire moon.



The body survived, but the event may have produced extensive fracturing.



Scientists have investigated whether some of Phobos's grooves and internal fractures are related to this impact.



\## Grooves



Phobos is covered with long, narrow grooves.



Some extend for many kilometers across the surface.



Early interpretations connected many grooves directly with the Stickney impact.



Modern models suggest a more complicated origin.



Some grooves may have been produced when impact ejecta from Mars landed on Phobos.



Others may reflect structural stresses, rolling boulders, or tidal effects.



The full origin of the groove system remains an active area of study.



\## Martian Impact Ejecta



Large impacts on Mars can launch rock fragments into space.



Because Phobos orbits close to Mars, some of this material can strike its surface.



This means Phobos's regolith may contain pieces of Martian rock.



If a future spacecraft returns samples from Phobos, those samples could potentially contain material originally ejected from Mars.



This makes Phobos unusually valuable for studying both a moon and its parent planet.



\## Surface Regolith



Phobos is covered by a layer of loose fragmented material called:



Regolith



This material is produced through billions of years of impacts and surface breakdown.



Because Phobos has extremely low gravity, impact ejecta behaves very differently than on larger worlds.



Some particles escape completely.



Others move slowly over large distances before returning to the surface.



\## Dust Movement



Dust on Phobos may move because of:



Impacts



Electrostatic charging



Tidal effects



Vibration



Local slopes



Very weak gravity means even small forces can significantly affect surface particles.



This can contribute to redistribution of regolith across the moon.



\## Surface Slopes



Phobos's irregular shape produces a complex gravitational environment.



The effective direction of "downhill" depends on both gravity and rotation.



Loose material tends to migrate toward gravitationally stable regions.



This can create differences in regolith thickness between different parts of the surface.



\## Composition



Phobos has a dark surface with low reflectivity.



Its visible and infrared spectrum resembles some primitive carbon-rich asteroids, especially certain C-type or D-type small bodies.



This similarity contributed to the long-standing hypothesis that Phobos may be a captured asteroid.



However, surface appearance alone does not determine origin.



\## Captured Asteroid Hypothesis



One possible origin is that Phobos formed elsewhere in the Solar System and was later captured by Mars.



Its dark surface and primitive-looking composition initially supported this idea.



However, simple gravitational capture is difficult.



An object approaching Mars normally either escapes again or collides unless a mechanism removes orbital energy.



Possible capture mechanisms could involve:



Atmospheric drag in an early Martian atmosphere



Three-body interactions



Collisions



Interactions with a debris disk



These scenarios require specific early conditions.



\## Formation From Martian Debris



Another major hypothesis is that Phobos formed from material orbiting Mars after a giant impact.



A large body may have struck early Mars and launched debris into orbit.



The debris could form a disk around the planet.



Material within that disk could then accrete into one or more moons.



This model naturally helps explain why Phobos and Deimos have nearly circular, equatorial orbits.



\## Giant Impact Model



In a giant-impact scenario, an early collision with Mars creates an orbiting debris disk.



Material close to Mars can fall back onto the planet.



Material farther out can collect into satellites.



The original satellite system may have included more or larger moons than exist today.



Tidal evolution could later cause inner moons to spiral inward and outer moons to move outward.



Phobos and Deimos may represent surviving products of this evolution.



\## Captured Asteroid Versus Impact Origin



The origin problem remains unresolved because different evidence points in different directions.



Phobos's dark spectrum resembles primitive asteroids.



Its orbit, however, is nearly circular and lies close to Mars's equatorial plane, which is easier to explain if it formed from a Martian debris disk.



Its density and composition are also compatible with a mixture of primitive and Martian material under some models.



Determining its exact origin is one of the main goals of future sample-return exploration.



\## Orbit Circularization Problem



A randomly captured asteroid would likely begin on a highly eccentric and inclined orbit.



Phobos today has a nearly circular orbit very close to the Martian equatorial plane.



Substantial energy would have to be removed to transform a captured asteroid into this configuration.



This difficulty is one of the strongest arguments against a simple capture scenario.



\## Relation to Deimos



\[\[Deimos]] is Mars's smaller and more distant moon.



Both moons are:



Small



Irregular



Dark



Heavily cratered



Their similarities suggest they may share a common origin.



However, their tidal evolution differs because Phobos lies inside Mars's synchronous orbit while Deimos lies outside it.



Phobos spirals inward.



Deimos gradually moves outward.



\## Discovery



Phobos was discovered in:



1877



by American astronomer:



Asaph Hall



Hall also discovered Deimos during the same observing campaign.



The two moons were found using a large refracting telescope at the United States Naval Observatory.



\## Name



The name Phobos comes from Greek mythology.



Phobos was associated with fear or panic and accompanied the war god Ares.



Mars is the Roman counterpart of Ares.



The second Martian moon, Deimos, was named after another mythological companion associated with terror or dread.



\## Rotation



Phobos is tidally locked to Mars.



This means its rotation period matches its orbital period.



The same general hemisphere therefore faces Mars throughout its orbit.



This is called:



Synchronous rotation



Many major moons in the Solar System are similarly tidally locked to their planets.



\## Libration



Although Phobos keeps roughly the same face toward Mars, its orbital eccentricity and irregular shape produce small apparent oscillations called:



Librations



These allow slightly different regions to become visible from Mars over time.



\## Temperature



Phobos has no substantial atmosphere to redistribute heat.



Surface temperature therefore changes considerably between sunlight and darkness.



Daytime regions can become much warmer than nighttime areas.



Temperature also depends on:



Surface material



Solar distance



Local slope



Thermal inertia



Because Phobos is small, no thick atmosphere or global weather system exists.



\## No Significant Atmosphere



Phobos cannot retain a substantial atmosphere because its gravity is extremely weak.



Gas molecules released from the surface generally escape into space.



At most, an extremely tenuous temporary exosphere could exist from sputtering or impact-generated particles.



\## Space Weathering



The surface is continuously altered by the \[\[Space Environment]].



Important processes include:



Solar wind bombardment



Cosmic radiation



Micrometeorite impacts



Temperature cycling



These processes gradually change the optical and chemical properties of exposed material.



\## Cratering



Phobos is heavily cratered.



Because it has little geological resurfacing, impact structures can remain visible for very long periods.



The crater population records part of the impact history of the Martian system.



However, interpreting crater ages is difficult because ejecta from Mars may also strike Phobos.



\## Secondary Impacts



Material launched from a primary impact can later fall elsewhere and create:



Secondary craters



On Phobos, Mars itself may be a source of impact ejecta.



This complicates attempts to infer the moon's age from crater counts alone.



\## Internal Structure



Phobos's interior is still poorly known.



Possible models range from a fractured coherent body to a highly porous rubble pile.



Its low density suggests significant internal void space.



A rubble-pile structure would have important consequences for its future tidal disruption.



\## Porosity



Porosity is the fraction of a body's volume occupied by empty spaces.



High porosity can result from loosely packed impact fragments.



If Phobos contains substantial internal voids, its bulk density would be lower than the density of its individual mineral grains.



\## Gravity Measurements



Spacecraft tracking during close flybys can measure tiny changes in velocity caused by Phobos's gravity.



These data help estimate:



Mass



Internal mass distribution



Gravity field



Combining gravity with shape data constrains its internal structure.



\## Mars Express Observations



ESA's \[\[Mars Exploration|Mars Express]] spacecraft has performed close flybys of Phobos.



These observations have improved knowledge of:



Shape



Gravity



Surface composition



Internal structure



Orbital evolution



High-resolution images have revealed details of grooves, craters, and regolith.



\## Viking Observations



NASA's Viking orbiters obtained important early close images of Phobos during the 1970s.



These images revealed its irregular shape, Stickney crater, and extensive groove system.



\## Soviet Phobos Missions



The Soviet Union launched:



Phobos 1



and



Phobos 2



in 1988.



Both missions were intended to investigate Mars and Phobos.



Phobos 1 was lost during the cruise to Mars.



Phobos 2 successfully entered Mars orbit and returned observations but lost contact before its planned close encounter and landing operations at Phobos.



Despite the loss, it returned valuable data about Mars and its environment.



\## Phobos-Grunt



Russia launched:



Phobos-Grunt



in 2011.



Its goal was to collect samples from Phobos and return them to Earth.



The spacecraft failed to leave Earth orbit and eventually re-entered the atmosphere.



The mission nevertheless demonstrated continued scientific interest in obtaining direct samples.



\## MMX Mission



Japan's Martian Moons eXploration mission, commonly called:



MMX



is designed to study Phobos and Deimos and return samples from Phobos to Earth.



The mission is especially important because laboratory analysis of returned material could help determine the moons' origin.



Measurements can compare Phobos material with:



Martian rocks



Primitive meteorites



Carbonaceous asteroids



Other Solar System materials



\## Why Sample Return Matters



Remote spectra can identify broad mineral groups but often cannot determine precise isotopic composition.



Laboratory analysis of returned samples could measure:



Oxygen isotopes



Hydrogen isotopes



Mineralogy



Organic compounds



Elemental abundances



Microscopic textures



Formation ages



These measurements could distinguish much more clearly between captured-asteroid and giant-impact origins.



\## Martian Samples on Phobos



A sample-return mission may recover more than native Phobos material.



Large impacts on Mars can eject pieces of the Martian crust into orbit.



Some fragments may land on Phobos.



Because Phobos has little geological recycling, these fragments could remain preserved for extremely long periods.



A returned sample could therefore contain ancient Martian material from locations inaccessible to Mars landers.



\## Ancient Mars Record



Martian ejecta on Phobos may preserve rocks from very old Martian terrains.



This could potentially provide information about:



Ancient Martian crust



Early volcanism



Past water



Early atmospheric conditions



Impact history



Such samples would make Phobos a natural archive of Mars.



\## Phobos Eclipses



Because Phobos orbits very close to Mars, it frequently passes between the Sun and locations on the Martian surface.



This produces:



Solar transits



or small eclipses



Phobos is not large enough in apparent size to produce the same kind of long total solar eclipse seen from Earth.



Instead, its irregular silhouette quickly crosses the Sun.



\## Rover Observations



Mars rovers such as Opportunity, Curiosity, and Perseverance have photographed Phobos passing across the Sun.



These observations are scientifically useful because the exact timing of the transit can improve measurements of Phobos's orbit.



Long-term orbital observations help determine the rate of tidal decay.



\## Phobos Shadow



Phobos also casts a shadow onto Mars.



Orbiting spacecraft can sometimes observe this shadow moving across the Martian surface.



These observations provide another way to refine its orbital position.



\## Tidal Acceleration



Because Phobos is moving inward, its orbital motion gradually becomes faster.



As orbital radius decreases, orbital speed and mean motion increase.



This effect is sometimes described as:



Tidal acceleration



even though the moon is losing orbital energy and moving toward Mars.



\## Mars Rotation and Angular Momentum



Tidal evolution transfers angular momentum between Mars's rotation and Phobos's orbit.



Because Phobos lies inside the synchronous orbit, the direction of transfer causes the moon's orbit to shrink.



This contrasts with Earth's Moon.



The Moon orbits outside Earth's synchronous radius and is gradually moving away from Earth.



\## Comparison With Earth's Moon



Earth's Moon recedes at roughly 3.8 centimeters per year.



Phobos moves inward at roughly 1.8 centimeters per year.



The difference arises mainly from whether the satellite orbits inside or outside its planet's synchronous radius.



This makes Phobos an important natural example of tidal orbital evolution.



\## Comparison With Deimos



Deimos orbits farther from Mars and takes about 30 hours to complete an orbit.



Because this is longer than Mars's rotation period, Deimos lies outside synchronous orbit.



Tides therefore slowly push it outward.



Phobos and Deimos provide a useful pair for demonstrating the two opposite directions of tidal migration.



\## Mars Ring-Moon Cycle Hypothesis



Some models propose that Mars may have repeatedly alternated between inner moons and rings.



An inner satellite could migrate inward and be tidally disrupted.



Its debris would form a ring.



Material beyond the Roche limit could later accrete into a new moon.



That moon might then begin another tidal evolution cycle.



Phobos could be part of the final stage of such a history.



This remains a hypothesis rather than a confirmed chronology.



\## Impact on Mars If Phobos Survives



If Phobos remains sufficiently cohesive to avoid complete disruption, it could eventually impact Mars.



Such an impact would release enormous energy compared with ordinary asteroid impacts because of the moon's size.



However, current structural models often consider tidal disruption before direct impact plausible.



The exact outcome depends on Phobos's internal strength.



\## Planetary Protection and Exploration



Phobos is attractive as a future exploration destination because of its extremely weak gravity.



Landing and departure require much less energy than from Mars itself.



However, weak gravity creates unusual challenges.



A spacecraft that moves too quickly can escape.



Surface contact can disturb large amounts of loose regolith.



Navigation near an irregular rotating body also requires precise gravitational modeling.



\## Human Exploration



Phobos has sometimes been proposed as a staging point for human missions to Mars.



Astronauts on Phobos could potentially operate robotic systems on the Martian surface with very short communication delay.



However, Phobos provides essentially no atmosphere, little radiation shielding at the surface, and extremely low gravity.



Long-term habitation would require substantial engineered infrastructure.



\## Radiation Environment



Phobos has no global magnetic field or atmosphere capable of strongly shielding radiation.



The surface is exposed to:



Solar energetic particles



Galactic cosmic rays



Solar wind



A habitat would need shielding, possibly using local regolith.



\## Scientific Importance



Phobos is important for far more than its small size might suggest.



Its unusual orbit provides one of the clearest examples of inward tidal migration.



Its future offers a natural experiment in satellite disruption and possible ring formation.



Its surface may contain a mixture of native material and fragments ejected from Mars.



Its unresolved origin tests competing models of moon formation, impact-generated debris disks, and asteroid capture.



Understanding Phobos can therefore improve knowledge of \[\[Mars]], moon formation, tidal dynamics, small-body structure, \[\[Planetary Rings]], and early Solar System evolution.



\## Common Misconceptions



Phobos is not an asteroid currently orbiting the Sun. It is a true gravitationally bound moon of Mars, although one hypothesis proposes that its original material may have come from an asteroid-like body.



Phobos will not crash into Mars in the near future. Its inward migration occurs over millions of years.



Its low gravity does not mean it has no gravity. It has enough gravity to retain loose surface material and control nearby spacecraft, but escape velocity is extremely low.



The grooves on Phobos are not proven to have been created entirely by Stickney crater. Several mechanisms may have contributed.



Phobos does not remain fixed in the Martian sky. Because it orbits faster than Mars rotates, it crosses the sky rapidly from west to east.



Its dark surface does not prove a captured-asteroid origin. Surface composition must be interpreted together with orbital dynamics, density, internal structure, and isotopic evidence.



\## Open Questions



The biggest unresolved question is the origin of Phobos.



Scientists do not yet know whether it is primarily captured primitive material, debris produced by a giant impact on Mars, or the result of a more complicated sequence involving an ancient debris disk and repeated generations of Martian moons.



Its internal structure is also uncertain. Determining whether it is a coherent fractured body or highly porous rubble pile is essential for predicting how it will eventually break apart.



The exact origin of its grooves remains debated, as does the proportion of Martian impact ejecta mixed into the regolith.



Future sample-return analysis may answer many of these questions by measuring isotopic and mineralogical signatures impossible to determine reliably from orbit.



\## Summary



Phobos is the larger and inner moon of \[\[Mars]].



It is a small, irregular, dark, heavily cratered body roughly 27 kilometers across at its longest dimension.



It orbits only about 6,000 kilometers above Mars and completes one revolution in about 7 hours 39 minutes.



Because Phobos orbits faster than Mars rotates, it rises in the west and sets in the east when viewed from the Martian surface.



Phobos lies inside Mars's synchronous-orbit radius, causing tidal forces to slowly pull it inward by roughly 1.8 centimeters per year.



Tens of millions of years from now, it is expected either to break apart through tidal forces and contribute to a Martian ring or eventually collide with Mars.



Its most famous surface feature is the enormous Stickney crater, and its surface contains a complex network of grooves.



The moon's origin remains uncertain. Its dark primitive-looking surface resembles some asteroids, but its nearly circular equatorial orbit is easier to explain through formation from a Martian impact-generated debris disk.



Future sample-return exploration is expected to provide critical evidence about this origin and may also recover ancient pieces of Mars that were blasted onto Phobos by impacts.



Phobos therefore provides a unique link among \[\[Mars]], small-body geology, tidal evolution, impact processes, moon formation, and the possible future development of planetary rings.
