\# Space Weather



\## Overview



Space weather describes changing conditions in the space environment that are driven mainly by activity from the Sun.



These conditions can affect:



\- Earth

\- Earth's magnetosphere

\- Earth's ionosphere

\- Satellites

\- Navigation systems

\- Radio communication

\- Power grids

\- Aviation

\- Astronauts

\- Spacecraft

\- Other planets



Space weather is different from ordinary atmospheric weather.



Ordinary weather involves processes such as:



\- Rain

\- Wind

\- Clouds

\- Temperature

\- Storms



Space weather involves:



\- Plasma

\- Magnetic fields

\- Radiation

\- Energetic particles

\- Solar eruptions



\## Main Source of Space Weather



The Sun is the dominant source of space weather in the Solar System.



Important solar drivers include:



\- Solar flares

\- Coronal Mass Ejections

\- Solar wind

\- Solar energetic particles

\- Coronal holes

\- High-speed solar-wind streams

\- Solar magnetic-field changes



\## Solar Activity



Solar activity changes continuously.



Major activity can originate from:



\- Sunspots

\- Active regions

\- Magnetic reconnection

\- Filament eruptions

\- Prominence eruptions



The likelihood of strong events generally increases near:



Solar maximum



\## Solar Flares



Solar flares are sudden releases of magnetic energy in the solar atmosphere.



They can produce intense:



\- X-rays

\- Extreme-ultraviolet radiation

\- Radio emission

\- Energetic particles



Flare radiation reaches Earth in approximately:



8 minutes



because electromagnetic radiation travels at the speed of light.



\## Flare Effects



Strong flare radiation can rapidly alter Earth's ionosphere.



Possible effects include:



\- HF radio blackouts

\- Navigation errors

\- Increased ionization

\- Communication disruption



\## Coronal Mass Ejections



A Coronal Mass Ejection, or CME, is a large eruption of magnetized plasma from the Sun.



An Earth-directed CME can strongly disturb Earth's magnetosphere.



\## CME Travel Time



CMEs travel much slower than light.



An Earth-directed CME commonly takes:



One to several days



to reach Earth.



Very fast events may arrive sooner.



\## CME Effects



Possible consequences include:



\- Geomagnetic storms

\- Strong auroras

\- Satellite disturbances

\- Power-grid currents

\- Ionospheric changes

\- Increased satellite drag



\## Solar Wind



The solar wind is the continuous outward flow of plasma from the Sun.



It contains mainly:



\- Protons

\- Electrons

\- Helium nuclei

\- Magnetic field



The solar wind surrounds the planets throughout the heliosphere.



\## Fast Solar Wind



Fast solar wind commonly originates from:



Coronal holes



It can reach speeds of roughly:



700–800 km/s



\## Slow Solar Wind



Slow solar wind generally travels around:



300–500 km/s



Its origins are more complex and are often associated with boundaries between open and closed magnetic structures.



\## Solar Energetic Particles



Solar Energetic Particles, abbreviated:



SEP



are high-energy charged particles associated with solar eruptive activity.



They can include:



\- Protons

\- Electrons

\- Heavy ions



\## SEP Hazards



Strong SEP events can threaten:



\- Astronauts

\- Spacecraft electronics

\- Satellites

\- High-altitude aviation

Important regions involved in space weather include:



Sun

\## Earth's Magnetosphere



Earth's magnetosphere is the region dominated by Earth's magnetic field.



It protects the planet from much of the direct solar wind.



However, it is highly dynamic.



\## Magnetopause



The magnetopause is the boundary between:



\- Earth's magnetosphere

\- Solar wind



Its position changes with solar-wind pressure.



\## Magnetosphere Compression



Strong solar-wind pressure can push the magnetopause closer to Earth.



During severe events, satellites at high altitude may experience significantly altered space conditions.



\## Bow Shock



Because the solar wind moves faster than relevant plasma wave speeds near Earth, a shock forms upstream of the magnetosphere.



This is called the:



Bow shock



\## Magnetosheath



The magnetosheath is the region of disturbed solar-wind plasma between:



\- Bow shock

\- Magnetopause



\## Magnetic Reconnection



Magnetic reconnection occurs when magnetic fields change their connectivity.



At Earth, reconnection allows energy from the solar wind to enter the magnetosphere.



\## Interplanetary Magnetic Field



The solar wind carries the Sun's magnetic field through space.



This is called the:



Interplanetary Magnetic Field



or:



IMF



\## Bz Component



The north-south component of the IMF is commonly called:



Bz



Strong sustained southward Bz can interact efficiently with Earth's magnetic field.



This increases the likelihood of major geomagnetic disturbances.



\## Magnetotail



The solar wind stretches Earth's magnetosphere away from the Sun.



This creates the:



Magnetotail



The magnetotail can store substantial magnetic energy.



\## Magnetotail Reconnection



Magnetic reconnection in the magnetotail can release stored energy.



This can produce:



\- Particle acceleration

\- Auroral substorms

\- Plasma flows



\## Geomagnetic Storm



A geomagnetic storm is a large disturbance of Earth's magnetic environment.



Major causes include:



\- Earth-directed CMEs

\- Strong solar-wind magnetic fields

\- High-speed solar-wind streams



\## Geomagnetic Storm Phases



A geomagnetic storm can include:



\- Sudden commencement

\- Main phase

\- Recovery phase



\## Sudden Storm Commencement



The arrival of a strong interplanetary shock can compress Earth's magnetosphere.



This may produce a rapid change in the measured geomagnetic field.



\## Main Phase



During the main phase:



\- Ring current strengthens

\- Earth's surface magnetic field can decrease

\- Magnetospheric energy increases



\## Recovery Phase



During recovery, the disturbed magnetosphere gradually returns toward quieter conditions.



This can take:



Hours to days



or longer for major events.



\## Ring Current



The ring current consists of energetic charged particles circulating around Earth.



It becomes stronger during geomagnetic storms.



\## Dst Index



The Dst index measures large-scale magnetic disturbances near Earth's equatorial region.



Strong negative values indicate intense geomagnetic storms.



\## Kp Index



The Kp index measures global geomagnetic activity.



It ranges from:



0 to 9



Higher values indicate stronger disturbance.



\## Geomagnetic Storm Scale



Geomagnetic storms are often categorized as:



G1

G2

G3

G4

G5



where G5 represents the most extreme category.



\## Radio Blackout Scale



Solar flare radio effects are often categorized as:



R1

R2

R3

R4

R5



Higher levels indicate more severe radio communication effects.



\## Solar Radiation Storm Scale



Solar energetic particle events may be classified as:



S1

S2

S3

S4

S5



Higher categories represent more severe radiation conditions.



\## Earth's Ionosphere



The ionosphere is a partially ionized region of Earth's upper atmosphere.



It is strongly affected by:



\- Solar ultraviolet radiation

\- X-rays

\- Energetic particles

\- Geomagnetic activity



\## Ionospheric Layers



Important ionospheric regions include:



\- D region

\- E region

\- F region



Their ionization varies with:



\- Time of day

\- Solar activity

\- Latitude

\- Space weather



\## D Region



The D region lies relatively low in the ionosphere.



Strong solar X-rays can increase its ionization.



This can cause:



HF radio absorption



\## HF Radio Blackout



High-frequency radio waves are often used for long-distance communication.



Strong solar flares can cause rapid HF radio blackouts, particularly on Earth's sunlit side.



\## E Region



The E region contributes to radio-wave propagation and contains ionized plasma influenced by solar and geomagnetic activity.



\## F Region



The F region contains high electron densities and is especially important for:



\- Long-distance radio

\- Satellite communication

\- GNSS signals



\## Total Electron Content



Total Electron Content, abbreviated:



TEC



measures the number of electrons along a path through the ionosphere.



Changes in TEC can affect satellite navigation signals.



\## GNSS



Global Navigation Satellite Systems include:



\- GPS

\- Galileo

\- GLONASS

\- BeiDou



Space weather can reduce their accuracy.



\## Navigation Errors



Ionospheric disturbances can cause:



\- Signal delays

\- Positioning errors

\- Loss of signal lock

\- Scintillation



\## Ionospheric Scintillation



Small-scale irregularities in ionospheric electron density can cause rapid variations in:



\- Radio-signal amplitude

\- Signal phase



This is called:



Ionospheric scintillation



\## Satellite Communication



Space weather can interfere with satellite communication through:



\- Ionospheric disturbances

\- Radio noise

\- Satellite charging

\- Radiation damage



\## Satellite Charging



Spacecraft can accumulate electric charge.



Two major forms include:



\- Surface charging

\- Internal charging



\## Surface Charging



Plasma can create electrical charge on spacecraft surfaces.



Discharges can cause:



\- Instrument errors

\- Electronic anomalies

\- Component damage



\## Internal Charging



High-energy particles can penetrate spacecraft materials and deposit charge inside electronics.



This can produce damaging electrical discharges.



\## Single-Event Effects



Energetic particles can strike microelectronic components.



This can produce:



Single-event effects



Examples include:



\- Bit flips

\- Processor resets

\- Component failures



\## Single-Event Upset



A temporary change in electronic data caused by energetic radiation is called a:



Single-Event Upset



or:



SEU



\## Radiation Damage



Long-term radiation exposure can gradually degrade:



\- Solar panels

\- Electronics

\- Detectors

\- Materials



\## Satellite Safe Mode



Operators may place spacecraft into:



Safe mode



during hazardous space-weather conditions.



This can protect sensitive systems.



\## Atmospheric Drag



Geomagnetic storms can heat Earth's upper atmosphere.



The thermosphere expands upward.



Low-Earth-orbit satellites then encounter denser atmosphere.



This increases:



Orbital drag



\## Satellite Orbital Decay



Increased drag can cause satellites to lose altitude faster than expected.



Operators may need to:



\- Recalculate trajectories

\- Perform orbital corrections



\## Thermosphere



The thermosphere is a region of Earth's upper atmosphere strongly influenced by solar EUV radiation and geomagnetic activity.



\## Thermospheric Heating



Energy deposited during geomagnetic storms can substantially heat the thermosphere.



This can increase atmospheric density at satellite altitudes.



\## Power Grids



Geomagnetic storms can induce electric currents in Earth's surface and technological infrastructure.



These are called:



Geomagnetically Induced Currents



or:



GICs



\## Geomagnetically Induced Currents



GICs can flow through:



\- Power lines

\- Transformers

\- Pipelines

\- Communication infrastructure



\## Transformer Effects



GICs can cause:



\- Transformer heating

\- Saturation

\- Voltage instability

\- Protective equipment activation



\## 1989 Quebec Blackout



A severe geomagnetic storm in March 1989 contributed to the collapse of the Hydro-Québec electrical grid.



Millions of people lost electricity.



The event demonstrated the vulnerability of modern power systems to space weather.



\## Pipelines



Geomagnetically induced currents can affect pipeline systems.



They may interfere with:



\- Corrosion protection

\- Electrical measurements



\## Aviation



Space weather can affect aviation, particularly:



\- Polar routes

\- High-altitude flights



Potential concerns include:



\- Radio communication

\- Navigation

\- Radiation exposure



\## Polar Aviation



HF radio is especially important for flights over polar regions where satellite communication coverage can be limited.



Strong solar activity can disrupt these radio links.



\## Aviation Radiation



Energetic particles and cosmic rays produce secondary radiation in Earth's atmosphere.



Radiation exposure generally increases with:



\- Altitude

\- Latitude



Strong solar energetic particle events can temporarily increase exposure.



\## Astronauts



Astronauts outside Earth's atmosphere can experience much higher radiation exposure than people on the surface.



Major SEP events are especially important hazards.



\## Human Spaceflight



Future missions to:



\- Moon

\- Mars

\- Deep space



must include strategies for space-weather radiation protection.



\## Radiation Shelter



Spacecraft and habitats can include shielded regions where astronauts can shelter during strong particle events.



\## Moon



The Moon lacks:



\- Thick atmosphere

\- Global Earth-like magnetic field



Its surface is therefore directly exposed to energetic solar particles.



\## Mars



Mars has:



\- Thin atmosphere

\- No strong global magnetic field



Solar energetic particles can significantly increase radiation conditions.



\## Mars Atmospheric Escape



Solar storms can increase the loss of ions from the Martian atmosphere.



This contributes to research into Mars's long-term atmospheric evolution.



\## MAVEN



NASA's MAVEN mission studies:



\- Martian upper atmosphere

\- Solar-wind interaction

\- Atmospheric escape



It has observed atmospheric responses to solar activity.



\## Mercury



Mercury is located very close to the Sun.



Its small magnetosphere can be strongly compressed during severe solar-wind events.



\## Venus



Venus lacks an intrinsic global magnetic field.



Its ionosphere interacts directly with the solar wind and forms an:



Induced magnetosphere



\## Jupiter



Jupiter has an enormous magnetosphere.



Its dynamics are driven strongly by:



\- Rapid planetary rotation

\- Plasma from Io



Solar-wind pressure still influences its outer magnetosphere.



\## Saturn



Saturn's magnetosphere also responds to changes in solar-wind conditions.



\## Uranus and Neptune



Solar-wind disturbances eventually propagate to Uranus and Neptune.



Because of their large distances from the Sun, these disturbances arrive much later than at Earth.



\## Comets



Comet ion tails respond strongly to solar-wind magnetic fields.



Space-weather disturbances can produce:



\- Tail deformation

\- Tail disconnection



\## Comet Tail Disconnection



A sudden change in solar-wind magnetic conditions can cause magnetic reconnection in a comet's plasma tail.



This can disconnect part of the tail.



\## Aurora



Auroras are visible manifestations of energy entering a planetary atmosphere along magnetic field lines.



At Earth they include:



\- Aurora Borealis

\- Aurora Australis



\## Aurora Formation



A simplified process is:



Solar-wind energy enters magnetosphere



↓



Charged particles are accelerated



↓



Particles move along magnetic field lines



↓



Particles collide with atmospheric gases



↓



Atoms and molecules become excited



↓



Light is emitted



\## Aurora Colors



Common auroral colors include:



Green:



Often produced by atomic oxygen.



Red:



Can also be produced by atomic oxygen at higher altitudes.



Blue and purple:



Can involve molecular nitrogen and ionized nitrogen.



\## Aurora Oval



Auroras usually occur within ring-shaped regions around the magnetic poles called:



Auroral ovals



\## Auroral Expansion



During major geomagnetic storms, auroral ovals can expand toward lower latitudes.



This allows auroras to become visible far from polar regions.



\## Substorm



A magnetospheric substorm is a shorter disturbance involving rapid energy release in the magnetotail.



It can cause:



\- Bright auroras

\- Rapid auroral movement

\- Particle acceleration



\## Geomagnetic Storm Versus Substorm



Geomagnetic storm:



Large global disturbance often lasting hours to days.



Substorm:



Shorter magnetospheric energy-release process often lasting tens of minutes to hours.



They can occur independently or together.



\## Solar Wind High-Speed Streams



Fast solar wind from coronal holes can interact with slower solar wind.



This creates compressed structures.



\## Corotating Interaction Region



A Corotating Interaction Region, or:



CIR



forms when high-speed solar wind catches slower wind ahead of it.



Because coronal holes can persist for multiple solar rotations, CIR effects can recur.



\## Recurrent Geomagnetic Storms



CIRs can produce recurring geomagnetic activity approximately once per solar rotation when a long-lived coronal hole repeatedly faces Earth.



\## Solar Rotation



The Sun rotates approximately once every:



27 days



as viewed from Earth, depending on latitude and reference frame.



This periodicity can appear in recurring space-weather conditions.



\## Parker Spiral



Because the Sun rotates while the solar wind moves outward, its magnetic field develops a spiral geometry.



This is called the:



Parker spiral



\## Magnetic Connectivity



The Parker spiral affects how Earth and spacecraft are magnetically connected to active regions on the Sun.



This is important for energetic-particle propagation.



\## Interplanetary Shock



Fast CMEs can drive shocks through the solar wind.



When a shock reaches Earth, it can cause sudden magnetosphere compression.



\## CME Sheath



The region behind a CME shock and ahead of the main magnetic ejecta is called the:



Sheath



It often contains:



\- Compressed plasma

\- Strong magnetic fields

\- Turbulence



The sheath itself can drive strong geomagnetic activity.



\## Magnetic Cloud



Some ICMEs contain organized magnetic structures called:



Magnetic clouds



Typical features include:



\- Smooth field rotation

\- Strong magnetic field

\- Low proton temperature



\## ICME



Once a CME travels into interplanetary space, it may be called an:



Interplanetary Coronal Mass Ejection



or:



ICME



\## Southward Magnetic Field



A strong southward magnetic field inside a CME or sheath is particularly important for severe geomagnetic storms.

\## WIND

\## GOES

\## Solar Orbiter

\## Space Weather Prediction Center

\## Space Weather Models

\- Solar wind

\## ENLIL



ENLIL is a large-scale heliospheric model used to simulate:



\- Solar wind

\## CME Arrival Forecasting



Forecasting requires estimates of:



\- CME launch time

\- Speed

\- Direction

\- Width

\- Background solar wind



Arrival-time predictions still contain uncertainty.



\## Magnetic Field Forecasting



Predicting a CME's magnetic orientation before it reaches Earth is particularly difficult.



This limits advance prediction of geomagnetic storm severity.



\## Why Bz Is Difficult to Forecast



Coronagraphs can estimate CME:



\- Shape

\- Speed

\- Direction



but cannot easily determine the exact magnetic field that will arrive at Earth.



\## Space Weather Nowcasting



Nowcasting describes assessment of current space-weather conditions using real-time observations.



\## Space Weather Forecasting Versus Nowcasting



Forecasting:



Predicts future conditions.



Nowcasting:



Describes current conditions.



Both are important for operational decision-making.



\## Solar Flare Warning Time



Solar flare X-rays arrive at Earth almost as soon as the flare is observed from near Earth.



Therefore there is little useful advance warning for the initial electromagnetic effects.



\## CME Warning Time



CMEs travel much more slowly.



Once an Earth-directed CME is detected, forecasters may have:



Many hours to several days



before arrival.



\## SEP Warning Time



High-energy particles can travel from the Sun to Earth much faster than CMEs.



The highest-energy particles may arrive within:



Tens of minutes



after acceleration.



This can provide limited warning.

\## Carrington Event



The Carrington Event occurred in:



1859



It is one of the most famous extreme space-weather events.



It involved:



\- A major solar eruption

\- Intense geomagnetic storm

\- Very bright auroras

\- Telegraph disturbances



\## Carrington Flare



Richard Carrington and Richard Hodgson independently observed a bright solar flare associated with the 1859 event.



\## Telegraph Effects



During the Carrington Event:



\- Telegraph equipment malfunctioned

\- Operators reported electrical shocks

\- Some systems operated using geomagnetically induced currents even after power sources were disconnected



\## Carrington Event Importance



The event is used as a benchmark for studying:



Extreme space-weather risk



Modern society is much more dependent on electrical and satellite infrastructure than society was in 1859.



\## May 1921 Geomagnetic Storm



A major geomagnetic storm occurred in:



May 1921



It disrupted:



\- Telegraph systems

\- Railway signaling



and produced widespread auroras.



\## March 1989 Storm



In March 1989, a geomagnetic storm caused the Hydro-Québec power-grid failure.



The event demonstrated modern infrastructure vulnerability.



\## Halloween Storms



Powerful solar activity occurred during:



October and November 2003



These events included:



\- X-class solar flares

\- CMEs

\- Solar energetic particles

\- Geomagnetic storms



\## Halloween Storm Effects



Reported effects included:



\- Satellite anomalies

\- Communication disruptions

\- Aviation impacts

\- Enhanced auroras



\## July 2012 Solar Event



In July 2012, an extremely strong CME traveled through Earth's orbital distance but did not hit Earth because Earth was elsewhere in its orbit.



The event was observed by spacecraft.



It is often studied as an example of a potentially extreme modern solar storm.



\## Space Weather and Probability



Extreme space-weather events are rare.



However, their potential consequences can be large.



Risk assessment therefore considers both:



\- Probability

\- Impact



\## Extreme Space Weather



Extreme events may simultaneously affect:



\- Satellites

\- Power grids

\- Navigation

\- Communication

\- Aviation

\- Human spaceflight



\## Infrastructure Interdependence



Modern infrastructure systems depend on each other.



For example:



Power outages can affect communication systems.



Navigation disruption can affect transportation.



Satellite failure can affect timing used by power and financial networks.



\## Precision Timing



GNSS satellites provide precise timing used by:



\- Telecommunications

\- Power grids

\- Financial systems

\- Data networks



Space-weather disruptions can therefore have effects beyond navigation.



\## Space Weather and Earth Surface



People on Earth's surface are generally well protected by:



\- Atmosphere

\- Magnetosphere



Most space-weather hazards are technological rather than direct biological hazards at ground level.



\## Atmospheric Protection



Earth's atmosphere absorbs:



\- Solar X-rays

\- Extreme ultraviolet

\- Most energetic particle radiation



This protects surface life.



\## Magnetic Protection



Earth's magnetic field redirects many charged particles.



However, it does not completely block solar-wind energy.



\## Space Weather and Climate



Space weather involves short-term changes in the near-Earth space environment.



It is different from:



Climate



which describes long-term patterns in Earth's atmosphere and oceans.



\## Solar Cycle



Space-weather frequency varies with the approximately:



11-year solar cycle



\## Solar Maximum



Near solar maximum:



\- Sunspot numbers rise

\- Flares are more common

\- CMEs are more common

\- SEP events are more frequent



\## Solar Minimum



Near solar minimum:



\- Major eruptions are less frequent

\- Long-lived coronal holes can produce recurring high-speed streams



Therefore space weather does not disappear during minimum.



\## Galactic Cosmic Rays



Galactic cosmic rays are high-energy particles arriving from outside the Solar System.



They are distinct from solar energetic particles.



\## Solar Modulation of Cosmic Rays



The heliospheric magnetic field modulates cosmic-ray entry into the inner Solar System.



Near solar maximum:



Galactic cosmic-ray intensity generally decreases.



Near solar minimum:



It generally increases.



\## Forbush Decrease



A strong ICME can temporarily reduce galactic cosmic-ray intensity.



This is called:



Forbush decrease



\## Ground-Level Enhancement



Rare high-energy solar particle events can cause detectable increases in secondary particles measured at Earth's surface.



These are called:



Ground-Level Enhancements



or:



GLEs



\## Neutron Monitors



Ground-based neutron monitors detect secondary particles generated when cosmic rays interact with Earth's atmosphere.



They are useful for studying:



\- Cosmic rays

\- Extreme SEP events



\## Space Weather on Other Planets



Every planet with an atmosphere, magnetosphere, or exposed surface responds differently to solar activity.



\## Planetary Space Weather



The study of solar-driven environmental changes around other planets can be called:



Planetary space weather



\## Mercury Space Weather



Mercury experiences:



\- Strong solar wind

\- Intense radiation

\- Magnetosphere compression

\- Surface sputtering



\## Venus Space Weather



Venus develops an induced magnetosphere because it lacks a strong intrinsic global magnetic field.



Solar activity can influence:



\- Ionosphere

\- Atmospheric escape



\## Mars Space Weather



Mars experiences:



\- Atmospheric ionization

\- Radiation variations

\- Atmospheric escape

\- Crustal magnetic interactions



\## Jupiter Space Weather



Jupiter's magnetosphere is strongly internally driven.



However, solar-wind changes can:



\- Compress the magnetosphere

\- Modify auroral activity



\## Saturn Space Weather



Solar-wind pressure and magnetic disturbances can modify Saturn's magnetosphere and auroras.



\## Uranus Space Weather



Uranus has a highly tilted and offset magnetic field.



Its interaction with the solar wind changes dramatically as the planet rotates.



\## Neptune Space Weather



Neptune also has a strongly tilted and offset magnetic field.



Solar-wind disturbances reaching Neptune are much more evolved than those observed near Earth.



\## Lunar Space Weather



The Moon has no global magnetosphere.



Solar wind interacts directly with much of the lunar surface.



Effects include:



\- Surface charging

\- Sputtering

\- Radiation exposure



\## Lunar Magnetic Anomalies



Some regions of the Moon contain localized crustal magnetic fields.



These can create small-scale interactions with solar wind.



\## Space Weather and Surface Charging



Airless bodies such as:



\- Moon

\- Asteroids



can develop electrical surface charging due to:



\- Solar wind

\- Solar ultraviolet radiation

\- Plasma environments



\## Space Weather and Asteroids



Solar wind and radiation interact directly with asteroid surfaces.



Long-term exposure contributes to:



Space weathering



\## Space Weather Versus Space Weathering



Space weather:



Changing plasma, radiation, and magnetic environment.



Space weathering:



Long-term alteration of exposed planetary surfaces by solar wind, micrometeorites, and radiation.



They are different concepts.



\## Space Weathering



Space weathering can alter:



\- Surface color

\- Reflectance

\- Spectra

\- Mineral properties



on airless objects.



\## Exoplanet Space Weather



Planets orbiting other stars can experience:



\- Stellar winds

\- Stellar flares

\- Stellar energetic particles

\- Possible stellar CMEs



This environment is sometimes called:



Exoplanet space weather



\## Red Dwarf Stars



Red dwarf stars can produce frequent powerful flares.



Their habitable zones lie relatively close to the star.



This can expose planets to intense space-weather conditions.



\## Exoplanet Atmospheres



Strong stellar activity can affect:



\- Atmospheric escape

\- Atmospheric chemistry

\- Ionization

\- Surface radiation



\## Exoplanet Magnetospheres



Planetary magnetic fields may influence how stellar wind interacts with an atmosphere.



However, magnetospheric protection is complex and depends on many factors.



\## Space Weather and Habitability



Long-term stellar space weather may influence whether planets can:



\- Retain atmospheres

\- Maintain surface conditions

\- Support stable environments



\## Young Sun



The young Sun likely produced:



\- Stronger magnetic activity

\- More intense high-energy radiation

\- Stronger solar wind



This may have influenced early planetary atmospheres.



\## Early Earth



Earth's early atmosphere and magnetic field evolved under stronger solar activity.



Understanding ancient space weather helps study early habitability.



\## Early Mars



Mars likely had a thicker atmosphere in the distant past.



Loss processes influenced by solar activity contributed to its atmospheric evolution.

Space-weather science combines:



\- Solar physics

\## Common Misconceptions



\### Space Weather Is Ordinary Weather in Space



Incorrect.



Space weather involves plasma, magnetic fields, radiation, and energetic particles rather than rain or atmospheric wind.



\### Every Solar Flare Causes a Geomagnetic Storm



Incorrect.



Geomagnetic storms are primarily driven by solar-wind and magnetic-field disturbances reaching Earth.



\### CMEs Reach Earth in Eight Minutes



Incorrect.



Flare radiation arrives in roughly eight minutes.



CME plasma generally requires many hours to several days.



\### Earth's Magnetic Field Blocks All Space Weather



Incorrect.



The magnetosphere provides substantial protection but also interacts dynamically with solar wind.



\### Space Weather Only Affects Satellites



Incorrect.



It can affect:



\- Power grids

\- Aviation

\- Radio

\- Navigation

\- Astronauts



\### Aurora Is Dangerous Radiation Reaching the Ground



Incorrect.



Aurora is light emitted high in the atmosphere after energetic particles excite atmospheric gases.



\### Solar Maximum Is the Only Time Space Weather Occurs



Incorrect.



Significant events can occur at any phase of the solar cycle.



\### Solar Minimum Is Completely Safe



Incorrect.



High-speed solar-wind streams and cosmic-ray exposure remain important.



\### Space Weather Directly Causes Earthquakes



There is no established evidence that ordinary solar space-weather events directly trigger major earthquakes.



\### Space Weather Causes Normal Hurricanes



Space weather is not the direct driver of ordinary tropical storms.



\## Important Terminology



\### Space Weather



Changing conditions in space driven mainly by solar activity.



\### Solar Flare



Rapid release of magnetic energy and electromagnetic radiation from the Sun.



\### Coronal Mass Ejection



Large eruption of magnetized solar plasma.



\### Solar Wind



Continuous plasma flow from the Sun.



\### Solar Energetic Particle



High-energy charged particle associated with solar activity.



\### Geomagnetic Storm



Large disturbance of Earth's magnetosphere.



\### Magnetosphere



Region dominated by a planet's magnetic field.



\### Magnetopause



Boundary between Earth's magnetosphere and solar wind.



\### Bow Shock



Shock formed upstream of Earth's magnetosphere.



\### Ionosphere



Partially ionized region of Earth's upper atmosphere.



\### Interplanetary Magnetic Field



Solar magnetic field carried outward by solar wind.



\### Bz



North-south component of the interplanetary magnetic field.



\### Geomagnetically Induced Current



Electrical current produced in conducting infrastructure by changing geomagnetic fields.



\### Ionospheric Scintillation



Rapid radio-signal fluctuations caused by ionospheric irregularities.



\### Corotating Interaction Region



Compressed solar-wind region formed where fast wind overtakes slow wind.



\### Forbush Decrease



Temporary reduction in galactic cosmic-ray intensity after an interplanetary disturbance.



\### Ground-Level Enhancement



Increase in ground-detectable secondary particles from an unusually energetic solar particle event.

\## Summary



Space weather describes changing conditions in space caused mainly by solar activity.



Major drivers include:



\- Solar flares

\- Coronal Mass Ejections

\- Solar wind

\- Solar energetic particles

\- Coronal holes



Solar flare electromagnetic radiation can reach Earth in approximately eight minutes.



CMEs usually require many hours to several days.



When solar-wind disturbances reach Earth, they interact with:



Earth's magnetosphere



Strong southward interplanetary magnetic fields can produce efficient magnetic reconnection and major geomagnetic storms.



Space-weather effects can include:



\- Aurora

\- Radio blackouts

\- Navigation errors

\- Satellite charging

\- Radiation damage

\- Increased orbital drag

\- Power-grid currents

\- Astronaut radiation hazards



Space weather is monitored using:



\- Solar telescopes

\- Spacecraft near Earth and the Sun

\- Magnetometers

\- Radio instruments

\- GNSS networks

\- Ionospheric sensors



Forecasting has improved significantly, but predicting the exact magnetic properties of incoming CMEs remains a major challenge.



Space weather is essential to understanding the connection between the Sun and planetary environments and is increasingly important for modern technology and future human exploration.
