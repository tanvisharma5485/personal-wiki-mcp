\# Planetary Migration



\## Overview



Planetary migration is the process by which a planet changes its orbital distance from its star after or during formation.



A planet does not necessarily remain at the location where it first formed.



Its orbit can change because of interactions with:



Gas in a protoplanetary disk



Planetesimals and smaller bodies



Other planets



Stellar companions



Tidal forces



Planetary migration is one of the most important concepts in modern planetary science because it helps explain the architecture of both the \[\[Solar System]] and many \[\[Exoplanets]].



\## Why Planetary Migration Happens



A planet exchanges angular momentum with surrounding material.



If the planet loses angular momentum, it usually moves inward toward the star.



If it gains angular momentum, it can move outward.



The surrounding material also changes in response, so migration is fundamentally a two-way gravitational interaction.



\## Angular Momentum



Orbital angular momentum depends on a planet's mass, orbital distance, and orbital velocity.



Changing angular momentum changes the orbit.



Migration therefore occurs when torques act on the planet.



\## Torque



A torque is a force that changes angular momentum.



In a protoplanetary disk, gravitational interactions between a planet and nearby gas create torques.



These torques can either push the planet inward or outward.



\## Disk-Planet Interaction



A planet embedded in a protoplanetary disk gravitationally disturbs the surrounding gas.



The planet creates:



Density waves



Spiral structures



Pressure disturbances



These structures exert gravitational forces back on the planet.



The imbalance between forces from inner and outer disk regions determines the direction and speed of migration.



\## Spiral Density Waves



A planet can generate spiral waves in the disk.



One spiral wave extends inward toward the star and another outward.



These waves carry angular momentum through the disk.



Their interaction with the planet is central to disk-driven migration.



\## Lindblad Torques



Important gravitational torques arise near locations called:



Lindblad resonances



The outer disk often exerts a negative torque that encourages inward migration, while the inner disk can exert a positive torque.



The net result depends on the detailed disk structure.



\## Corotation Torque



Gas near the planet's orbit can also produce:



Corotation torque



This torque depends strongly on gradients in:



Density



Temperature



Entropy



Vorticity



Under some conditions, corotation torque can slow, halt, or reverse inward migration.



\## Type I Migration



Type I migration generally describes migration of relatively low-mass planets that do not open a deep gap in the disk.



The planet excites waves but remains embedded in the surrounding gas.



The resulting migration can be rapid compared with disk lifetimes.



\## Direction of Type I Migration



In simple disk models, Type I migration is often inward.



However, realistic temperature and density structures can produce regions where migration becomes:



Slower



Zero



Outward



Therefore Type I migration is not always purely inward.



\## Migration Timescale



Migration timescales depend on:



Planet mass



Disk density



Disk temperature



Disk thickness



Gas viscosity



Local gradients



A planet can migrate a substantial distance during the few-million-year lifetime of a gas disk.



\## Migration Problem



Early models sometimes predicted that growing planetary cores would spiral into their stars too quickly.



This became known as a:



Migration problem



Modern models include more complex disk physics that can slow or reverse migration.



\## Planet Traps



Some disk locations can stop or slow migration.



These are called:



Planet traps



They may occur near:



Snow lines



Pressure transitions



Dead-zone boundaries



Disk edges



Heating transitions



A planet can remain near such a region while the disk evolves.



\## Type II Migration



Massive planets can strongly disturb the surrounding disk and create a deep gap.



Their migration has traditionally been called:



Type II migration



This is most relevant to giant planets.



\## Gap Formation



A giant planet can open a gap when its gravitational influence is strong enough to overcome:



Gas pressure



Disk viscosity



The ability to open a gap depends on both planet mass and disk properties.



\## Classical Type II Picture



In the classical picture, a gap-opening planet moves inward at roughly the same rate as the disk gas evolves viscously.



Modern simulations show that giant planet migration can be more complicated.



The planet may migrate faster or slower than the local viscous flow depending on conditions.



\## Type III Migration



A rapid form of migration sometimes called:



Type III migration



or:



Runaway migration



can occur under particular disk conditions.



It involves strong interaction between the planet and gas moving through the planet's co-orbital region.



It is not expected to dominate every planetary system.



\## Co-Orbital Region



The co-orbital region contains disk material moving near the same orbital distance as the planet.



Its dynamics can strongly influence migration.



\## Horseshoe Orbits



Gas close to a low-mass planet can follow paths that resemble horseshoes in a frame rotating with the planet.



This region contributes to corotation torques.



\## Horseshoe Drag



Angular momentum exchange between gas executing horseshoe turns and the planet creates:



Horseshoe drag



It can significantly alter the migration direction.



\## Disk Temperature Effects



The thermal structure of a disk affects migration.



Heating and cooling determine:



Pressure gradients



Entropy gradients



Disk thickness



These influence the balance of torques.



\## Planet Heating



A rapidly accreting planet releases heat.



This can modify nearby disk gas and create an additional torque sometimes called:



Heating torque



For some low-mass growing planets, this may slow inward migration or produce outward migration.



\## Eccentricity and Inclination Damping



Gas disks do more than change orbital radius.



Interactions with gas generally tend to reduce:



Orbital eccentricity



Orbital inclination



This helps young planetary systems become relatively flat and circular.



\## Disk-Driven Eccentricity



Under some circumstances, giant planets and disk structures can instead excite eccentricity.



The final outcome depends on:



Planet mass



Gap structure



Disk properties



Multiple-planet interactions



\## Migration During Planet Formation



Migration can begin while planets are still growing.



Therefore planet formation and migration occur simultaneously.



A planetary core may:



Form in one region



Migrate



Accrete new material



Continue growing elsewhere



This complicates attempts to infer a planet's birthplace from its present composition.



\## Migration and Composition



As a planet moves through a disk, it can accrete material from regions with different:



Temperatures



Ices



Dust compositions



Gas chemistry



A planet's final chemical composition may therefore reflect multiple disk locations.



\## Snow Line Migration



The snow line itself also moves as the protoplanetary disk cools.



This means both:



Planets move through the disk



and:



Important chemical boundaries move through the disk



Planet formation is therefore highly dynamic.



\## Hot Jupiters



One of the strongest pieces of evidence that large-scale migration occurs is the existence of:



Hot Jupiters



These are Jupiter-sized planets orbiting extremely close to their stars.



It is difficult for such planets to form directly in their present hot environments using standard giant planet formation models.



Many likely formed farther out and later moved inward.



\## Disk Migration of Hot Jupiters



One possible pathway is migration through the gas disk.



A young giant planet forms beyond the snow line and then exchanges angular momentum with disk gas.



It gradually moves closer to its star.



\## High-Eccentricity Migration



Not all hot Jupiters necessarily migrated smoothly through gas.



Another pathway is:



High-eccentricity migration



A planet's orbit becomes extremely eccentric through gravitational interactions.



Tidal forces near the star then shrink and circularize the orbit.



\## Planet-Planet Scattering



Strong gravitational encounters between planets can increase orbital eccentricity.



A planet may be thrown onto an orbit that passes very close to the star.



Tidal interaction can then transform this into a short-period orbit.



\## Kozai-Lidov Mechanism



A distant inclined companion can cause large periodic changes in a planet's:



Eccentricity



Inclination



This process is called the:



Kozai-Lidov mechanism



It can contribute to high-eccentricity migration in some systems.



\## Tidal Circularization



When a highly eccentric planet repeatedly passes close to its star, tidal forces dissipate orbital energy.



The orbit can gradually become:



Smaller



More circular



This process helps explain some close-in exoplanets.



\## Spin-Orbit Misalignment



Some hot Jupiters orbit in planes strongly misaligned with their star's rotation.



Some even have retrograde orbits.



These systems may indicate violent dynamical histories rather than simple smooth disk migration.



\## Resonant Migration



When two planets migrate at different rates, their orbital periods can approach a simple ratio.



They may then become captured into an:



Orbital resonance



\## Mean-Motion Resonance



A mean-motion resonance occurs when orbital periods have a near-integer relationship.



Examples include ratios such as:



2:1



3:2



4:3



Resonant capture is common in migration models.



\## Convergent Migration



When two planets move toward one another in orbital spacing, this is called:



Convergent migration



It favors resonance capture.



\## Divergent Migration



When orbital spacing increases, the migration is:



Divergent



Planets can move away from resonances or alter previously established resonant configurations.



\## Resonant Chains



Several planets can become linked through multiple resonances.



This produces a:



Resonant chain



Such configurations are commonly produced in simulations of disk migration.



\## TRAPPIST-1



The \[\[Exoplanets|TRAPPIST-1]] system contains seven approximately Earth-sized planets in a compact resonant architecture.



Its orbital relationships provide strong evidence that migration played an important role during system formation.



\## Kepler Systems



Many compact multi-planet systems discovered by the Kepler mission contain planets close to simple orbital resonances.



Their architectures provide important tests of migration theory.



\## Resonance Breaking



Not all systems preserve their original resonances.



After the gas disk disappears, gravitational interactions can break resonant chains.



This may produce the orbital spacing observed in many mature planetary systems.



\## Migration and Planetary Instability



Migration can pack multiple planets into compact configurations.



Once the stabilizing gas disappears, the system may become unstable.



Planets can then:



Collide



Scatter



Change eccentricity



Be ejected



This creates a transition from smooth disk migration to chaotic gravitational evolution.



\## Planetesimal-Driven Migration



Planets can also migrate by gravitationally scattering large populations of planetesimals.



This is called:



Planetesimal-driven migration



Unlike gas-driven migration, it can continue after much of the disk gas disappears.



\## How Planetesimal Migration Works



Suppose a planet scatters a planetesimal inward toward the star.



The planet may receive angular momentum and move slightly outward.



If it scatters material outward, the planet may lose angular momentum and move inward.



Repeated interactions with enormous numbers of planetesimals can move the planet substantially.



\## Neptune Migration



\[\[Neptune]] is believed to have migrated significantly during early Solar System evolution.



Its outward migration had profound effects on the:



\[\[Kuiper Belt]]



Trans-Neptunian objects



Orbital resonances



\## Neptune and Pluto



\[\[Pluto]] is trapped in a:



3:2 orbital resonance



with Neptune.



Pluto completes approximately two orbits for every three completed by Neptune.



One leading explanation is that Pluto was captured into this resonance while Neptune migrated outward.



\## Resonant Sweeping



As a planet migrates, the locations of its orbital resonances move through the surrounding small-body population.



This is called:



Resonance sweeping



Objects can be:



Captured



Excited



Scattered



Removed



\## Kuiper Belt Structure



The Kuiper Belt contains populations strongly influenced by Neptune's migration.



Examples include:



Classical objects



Resonant objects



Scattered objects



Their orbital distribution preserves information about early migration.



\## Plutinos



Objects in the 3:2 resonance with Neptune are called:



Plutinos



Pluto is the most famous example.



\## Twotinos



Objects occupying Neptune's 2:1 resonance are sometimes called:



Twotinos



Their distribution also provides information about Neptune's migration history.



\## Nice Model



The \[\[Solar System Formation|Nice model]] is a family of models describing early migration of the giant planets.



It is named after Nice, France, where the model was developed.



In these scenarios, the giant planets originally occupied a more compact configuration.



\## Giant Planet Migration



In Nice-type models, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune interact with a large disk of leftover planetesimals.



These gravitational interactions change the planets' orbits.



Jupiter generally moves slightly inward while the outer giant planets move outward.



\## Jupiter Migration



Jupiter tends to scatter small bodies outward.



Through conservation of angular momentum, this can cause Jupiter to move inward.



Its migration is smaller in distance than that proposed for Neptune in many models.



\## Saturn Migration



Saturn can move outward as it interacts with planetesimals and the other giant planets.



Changes in the Jupiter-Saturn orbital relationship can have major dynamical consequences.



\## Uranus and Neptune Migration



Uranus and Neptune may have formed closer to the Sun than their current orbits.



Interactions with planetesimals could then drive them outward.



\## Planetary Resonance Crossing



As giant planets migrate, they can cross or leave orbital resonances with one another.



Such events may destabilize small-body populations and sometimes planetary orbits.



\## Dynamical Instability



Some versions of the Nice model include an episode in which the giant planets undergo strong mutual interactions.



One or more ice giants can experience close encounters with Jupiter or Saturn.



The final system settles into a more stable arrangement.



\## Possible Additional Giant Planet



Some models produce the present Solar System more naturally if an additional ice giant originally existed.



This extra planet may have been ejected during instability.



This is a model-based possibility rather than a directly observed former planet.



\## Late Heavy Bombardment Connection



Early versions of the Nice model were used to explain a proposed spike in lunar impacts known as the:



Late Heavy Bombardment



Modern interpretations of the lunar impact chronology are more complex.



A sharp bombardment spike is not universally accepted.



Therefore giant-planet migration does not have to be tied to one exact Late Heavy Bombardment scenario.



\## Grand Tack Hypothesis



The:



Grand Tack hypothesis



is a model involving large-scale migration of Jupiter in the gas disk.



It proposes that Jupiter initially moved inward toward the Sun.



Later, after Saturn grew and migrated into resonance with Jupiter, the direction of migration reversed.



Jupiter and Saturn then moved outward.



\## Why It Is Called a Tack



The name comes from sailing.



A sailing vessel changes direction during a maneuver called a tack.



In the model, Jupiter changes migration direction in a somewhat analogous way.



\## Jupiter's Inward Migration



In the Grand Tack model, Jupiter may have moved inward to approximately the region of:



1.5 astronomical units



before reversing.



The exact path depends on the specific simulation.



\## Mars Problem



A major motivation for the Grand Tack hypothesis is the unusually small mass of \[\[Mars]].



Simple terrestrial planet formation simulations often produce a Mars that is too massive.



Jupiter's inward migration could truncate the inner planet-forming disk near Mars's region.



This could leave less material available for Mars.



\## Asteroid Belt Mixing



Jupiter's migration could also remove and later repopulate portions of the \[\[Asteroid Belt]].



This may help explain why the belt contains objects with diverse compositions.



\## Jupiter-Saturn Resonance



The outward migration of Jupiter and Saturn in the Grand Tack model occurs after their interaction becomes favorable for reversing the net disk torque.



Resonant configurations between the two planets are central to the model.



\## Is the Grand Tack Proven?



No.



It is an influential hypothesis that explains several Solar System features, but it is not uniquely established.



Other formation histories may reproduce some of the same observations.



\## Migration of Terrestrial Planets



Migration is not limited to giant planets.



Rocky planetary embryos can migrate through a gas disk.



This can affect:



Their growth



Their final positions



Resonant chains



Collision histories



\## Super-Earth Migration



Many models propose that super-Earths form over a range of distances and migrate inward.



They may accumulate near the inner edge of the protoplanetary disk.



This can produce compact systems.



\## Why the Solar System Lacks Super-Earths



Super-Earths are very common around other stars, yet the Solar System has none.



Possible explanations involve:



Jupiter's formation



Migration



Disk structure



Early instability



Different initial conditions



This remains an open question.



\## Migration Toward Disk Inner Edge



A disk's inner boundary can sometimes halt inward migration.



Multiple planets may accumulate there and enter resonant configurations.



After disk dispersal, some systems become unstable.



\## Migrating Planets and Accretion



A migrating planet encounters fresh material.



Migration can therefore increase the amount of:



Gas



Pebbles



Planetesimals



available for accretion.



This links migration closely with \[\[Planet Formation]].



\## Pebble Accretion and Migration



A planetary core can grow through pebble accretion while simultaneously undergoing Type I migration.



The relative timescales of:



Growth



Migration



Disk dispersal



strongly affect whether the core becomes a rocky planet, ice giant, or gas giant.



\## Migration Maps



Researchers calculate regions of disks where planets of different masses are expected to migrate:



Inward



Outward



or remain near equilibrium



These diagrams are sometimes called:



Migration maps



As a disk evolves, the migration zones themselves move.



\## Zero-Torque Radius



A location where positive and negative disk torques approximately cancel is called a:



Zero-torque radius



A migrating planet can become temporarily trapped there.



\## Multiple Planet Interaction With Disk



When multiple planets share a disk, each affects the gas environment of the others.



Their gaps and spiral waves can overlap.



This can produce migration behavior very different from that of a single isolated planet.



\## Common Gap



Two giant planets in resonance may open a partially shared gap.



The balance of torques on the pair can produce joint migration.



This is relevant to models involving Jupiter and Saturn.



\## Outward Migration of Giant Planet Pairs



Under suitable conditions, a pair of resonant giant planets can migrate outward.



This provides a possible mechanism for the outward phase of the Grand Tack.



\## Disk Cavities



Some disks have central cavities.



Migration can stop near a cavity edge because the torque from the inner disk becomes weak or disappears.



\## Migration and Atmospheric Composition



A giant planet migrating across snow lines can accrete gas and solids with changing chemical composition.



Researchers therefore study atmospheric abundance ratios as potential clues to migration history.



Examples include:



Carbon-to-oxygen ratio



Nitrogen abundance



Sulfur abundance



Metal enrichment



Interpretation is complex because many processes can produce similar chemical signatures.



\## Migration and Water Delivery



Migration can mix material from different regions of a planetary system.



This may help transport water-rich bodies from colder outer regions toward rocky planets.



Such mixing may have contributed to water delivery to early Earth.



\## Migration and Asteroid Distribution



Migrating giant planets can:



Excite asteroid eccentricities



Shift resonances



Remove objects



Capture objects



Mix different populations



The present asteroid belt therefore preserves evidence of early dynamical evolution.



\## Migration and Trojans



Some planets possess Trojan objects that share their orbit near stable \[\[Lagrange Points]].



Planetary migration can influence the capture and survival of these populations.



\## Jupiter Trojans



Jupiter's Trojan asteroids occupy regions around its L4 and L5 points.



Some models suggest they were captured during giant-planet migration or instability.



\## Neptune Trojans



Neptune also possesses Trojan objects.



Their orbital properties can constrain models of Neptune's migration.



\## Migration and Centaurs



\[\[Centaurs]] are small bodies orbiting among the giant planets.



Their current trajectories are dynamically unstable over long timescales.



Many originate from outer Solar System reservoirs affected by giant-planet migration.



\## Scattered Disc



The \[\[Scattered Disc]] contains objects on eccentric and inclined orbits beyond Neptune.



Neptune's gravitational scattering played a major role in producing this population.



\## Oort Cloud Formation



Planetary scattering during Solar System formation also contributed to the creation of the \[\[Oort Cloud]].



The giant planets sent icy bodies onto extremely distant orbits.



Stellar and galactic perturbations then helped populate the cloud.



\## Migration and Planetary Defense



Migration itself is mainly an early-system process, but the orbital architecture it creates determines where small bodies remain.



This indirectly affects long-term populations of:



Asteroids



Comets



Near-Earth Objects



\## Post-Disk Migration



Planetary orbital changes continue after the gas disk disappears.



Mechanisms include:



Planetesimal scattering



Planet-planet scattering



Secular interactions



Tidal evolution



Stellar interactions



\## Secular Interactions



Secular interactions are long-term gravitational effects between planets.



They gradually change orbital:



Eccentricity



Orientation



Inclination



without requiring close encounters.



They can reshape a planetary system over millions or billions of years.



\## Secular Resonance



A secular resonance occurs when characteristic rates of orbital precession become commensurate.



Such resonances can strongly alter small-body eccentricities and inclinations.



\## ν6 Resonance



The:



ν6 secular resonance



near the inner asteroid belt is strongly influenced by Saturn.



It can increase asteroid eccentricities and help deliver objects toward near-Earth space.



The position of such resonances would have changed as planets migrated.



\## Migration and Stability



A migrating planet may cross resonances with another planet.



The outcome can be:



Stable resonance capture



Eccentricity growth



Scattering



Collision



Ejection



Small differences in initial conditions can therefore lead to very different planetary systems.



\## Planet Ejection



Dynamical instability can eject planets completely from their systems.



These planets become:



Rogue planets



Their existence is a natural possible outcome of planetary migration and scattering.



\## Stellar Cluster Effects



Young planetary systems often form inside stellar clusters.



Nearby stars can gravitationally perturb wide planetary orbits and outer planetesimal disks.



This can alter migration outcomes and help reshape distant small-body reservoirs.



\## Binary Stars



In binary-star systems, a companion star can strongly affect planet migration and long-term orbital stability.



Planets can orbit:



One star



or:



Both stars



Migration in these environments can be significantly more complicated.



\## Circumbinary Planet Migration



Planets forming in disks around both stars of a binary can migrate toward the inner edge of the circumbinary disk.



Several observed circumbinary planets orbit close to the theoretical stability boundary.



Migration may help explain this pattern.



\## Tidal Migration



Tidal forces can continue changing planetary or satellite orbits long after the protoplanetary disk disappears.



This is different from gas-disk migration.



\## Tides and Close-In Planets



A planet very close to its star raises tides on the star, while the star raises tides on the planet.



Over long periods, orbital energy and angular momentum are exchanged.



The planet may migrate:



Inward



or:



Outward



depending on the system's rotation and orbital configuration.



\## Orbital Decay



Some very close planets may slowly spiral toward their stars.



This process is called:



Orbital decay



Measuring it can provide information about tidal dissipation inside stars.



\## WASP-12 b



The hot Jupiter:



WASP-12 b



is a well-known system where observations indicate orbital decay.



It provides evidence that tidal migration can be measured directly in some exoplanet systems.



\## Tidal Migration of Moons



Moons also undergo migration because of tides.



Earth's \[\[Moon]] is moving outward.



\[\[Phobos]] is moving inward toward Mars.



These are forms of tidal orbital migration rather than disk migration.



\## Earth's Moon



The Moon currently recedes from Earth by approximately:



3.8 centimeters per year



because tidal interactions transfer angular momentum from Earth's rotation into the Moon's orbit.



\## Phobos



\[\[Phobos]] orbits Mars faster than Mars rotates.



Tidal interactions therefore remove orbital energy from Phobos.



It slowly moves inward toward Mars.



\## Deimos



\[\[Deimos]] orbits beyond Mars's synchronous orbit.



Tidal interaction causes it to move slowly outward.



\## Migration in Giant Planet Moon Systems



Moons around Jupiter and Saturn can also move due to tidal interactions.



Their migration changes orbital resonances and can affect tidal heating.



\## Enceladus



The orbital relationship between \[\[Enceladus]] and other Saturnian moons helps maintain tidal heating.



Long-term satellite migration influences these resonances.



\## Io, Europa and Ganymede



\[\[Io]], \[\[Europa]], and \[\[Ganymede]] occupy the Laplace resonance.



Long-term tidal evolution helped shape and continues to modify their orbital configuration.



\## Migration and Habitability



Migration can strongly influence planetary habitability.



A planet may form outside the habitable zone and later move into it.



Alternatively, it may form in a temperate region and migrate too close to its star.



Migration also affects:



Water delivery



Atmospheric composition



Impact rates



Orbital stability



Tidal locking



\## Habitable Zone Migration



The stellar habitable zone itself changes as a star evolves.



Planetary migration and stellar evolution therefore both influence whether a planet spends long periods under conditions compatible with surface liquid water.



\## Giant Planet Migration and Rocky Planets



Migrating giant planets can strongly affect nearby rocky worlds.



They may:



Scatter them



Destroy them through collisions



Eject them



Deliver material



Alter asteroid populations



A giant planet's migration history can determine whether terrestrial planets survive.



\## Hot Jupiter Systems and Small Planets



Some hot Jupiter systems may have lost inner rocky planets during giant-planet migration.



However, the outcome depends strongly on migration mechanism and timing.



Not every hot Jupiter necessarily destroys every smaller planet.



\## Migration and Planet Formation Models



Any successful theory of planet formation must explain both:



How planets acquire their masses



and:



How they reach their final orbits



Formation and migration therefore cannot be treated as completely separate processes.

\## Magnetized Disks



Real protoplanetary disks contain magnetic fields.



Magnetic turbulence and disk winds can change gas distribution and migration torques.

\## Disk Turbulence



Turbulence can cause migration torques to fluctuate.

\## Stochastic Migration



If turbulent density fluctuations repeatedly push a planet in changing directions, its migration can become partly random.



This is called:



Stochastic migration



\## Migration Versus In-Situ Formation



Some compact exoplanet systems may have formed largely near their current positions.



Others may have undergone major migration.



Distinguishing between:



In-situ formation



and:



Migration



is a central challenge in exoplanet science.



\## Common Misconceptions



\### Planets Always Form Where They Are Found



Incorrect. Many planets can move significantly after they form.



\### Migration Always Means Moving Toward the Star



Incorrect. Planets can migrate inward or outward.



\### Only Giant Planets Migrate



Incorrect. Low-mass planets and planetary embryos also experience migration.



\### Hot Jupiters Must Have Formed Next to Their Stars



Not necessarily. Many probably formed farther out and migrated inward.



\### Type II Migration Means a Planet Is Permanently Locked to the Gas Flow



This is an oversimplification. Modern simulations show that gap-opening planet migration can differ from classical disk viscous flow.



\### Jupiter Definitely Followed the Grand Tack Path



Incorrect. The Grand Tack is a hypothesis, not an established historical fact.



\### Neptune Has Always Been at Its Present Distance



Modern models strongly suggest significant orbital evolution, and outward migration is important for explaining Kuiper Belt structure.



\### Migration Stops All Planet Formation



Incorrect. Planets can continue accreting gas and solids while they migrate.



\### Migration Happens Only During the First Few Million Years



Gas-driven migration primarily occurs while a disk exists, but planetesimal-driven, gravitational, and tidal migration can continue much later.



\## Important Terminology



\*\*Planetary Migration:\*\* Long-term change in a planet's orbital distance or configuration.



\*\*Type I Migration:\*\* Disk-driven migration of lower-mass planets that do not open deep gaps.



\*\*Type II Migration:\*\* Traditional term for migration of massive gap-opening planets.



\*\*Type III Migration:\*\* Potential rapid migration involving strong co-orbital mass exchange.



\*\*Torque:\*\* Process that changes angular momentum.



\*\*Planet Trap:\*\* Disk location where migration slows or stops.



\*\*Resonant Capture:\*\* Process by which migrating planets become locked in an orbital resonance.



\*\*Planetesimal-Driven Migration:\*\* Orbital change caused by repeated gravitational scattering of smaller solid bodies.



\*\*High-Eccentricity Migration:\*\* Process involving highly eccentric planetary orbits followed by tidal orbital shrinking.



\*\*Resonance Sweeping:\*\* Movement of orbital resonances through small-body populations as planets migrate.



\*\*Orbital Decay:\*\* Gradual shrinking of an orbit, often caused by tides.



\## Simplified Disk Migration



A planet forms inside a gas disk. Its gravity creates spiral density waves. Gas inside and outside the planet's orbit exerts different gravitational torques. If the net torque is negative, the planet loses angular momentum and migrates inward. If the net torque is positive, the planet can migrate outward.



As the disk changes, the direction and speed of migration can also change.



\## Simplified Neptune Migration



Neptune interacts gravitationally with a massive population of outer Solar System planetesimals. Repeated scattering exchanges angular momentum between Neptune and the small bodies. Neptune gradually moves outward.



As Neptune migrates, its resonances also move outward. Some Kuiper Belt objects become trapped in those resonances, while others are scattered into new orbits.



The modern structure of the Kuiper Belt preserves evidence of this process.



\## Simplified Hot Jupiter Pathway



A giant planet forms far enough from its star for a massive core and gas envelope to develop. The planet then interacts with its protoplanetary disk and migrates inward.



Alternatively, later planet-planet interactions can give the planet a highly eccentric orbit. Repeated close approaches to the star produce tidal dissipation, shrinking and circularizing the orbit.



The result can be a giant planet orbiting extremely close to its star.



\## Scientific Importance



Planetary migration explains why the location where a planet forms may be very different from the location where it is eventually observed.



It is essential for understanding the origin of hot Jupiters, resonant systems, compact super-Earth systems, Kuiper Belt structure, giant-planet evolution, asteroid populations, and the possible early movement of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.



Migration also connects orbital evolution with planetary chemistry because a moving planet can collect material from multiple disk environments.



\## Open Questions



Scientists are still trying to determine how quickly planets migrate in realistic magnetized disks, how often migration reverses direction, and which disk structures create long-lived planet traps.



It remains uncertain exactly how far Jupiter and Saturn migrated during Solar System formation, whether the Grand Tack occurred, when giant-planet instability happened, and whether an additional giant planet was once present.



Researchers are also investigating whether most super-Earths formed near their current stars or migrated inward, how frequently hot Jupiters form through disk migration versus high-eccentricity migration, and how migration affects the survival and habitability of rocky planets.



\## Summary



Planetary migration is the movement of planets away from the orbital locations where they originally formed.



During the gas-disk phase, planets exchange angular momentum with surrounding gas. Low-mass planets can undergo Type I migration, while massive planets can open gaps and undergo migration traditionally described as Type II.



Migration can occur inward or outward depending on the balance of disk torques.



After the gas disappears, planets can continue moving through interactions with planetesimals, other planets, and tidal forces.



Migration helps explain hot Jupiters and compact exoplanet systems. In the Solar System, Neptune's outward migration is particularly important for understanding the Kuiper Belt and Pluto's 3:2 resonance.



Models such as the Nice model and Grand Tack hypothesis propose substantial early movement of the giant planets, although the precise history remains uncertain.



Planetary migration is therefore a central link between \[\[Planet Formation]], \[\[Orbital Dynamics]], the architecture of planetary systems, and the diversity of \[\[Exoplanets]].
