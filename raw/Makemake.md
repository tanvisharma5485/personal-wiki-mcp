\# Makemake



\## Overview



Makemake is a dwarf planet and one of the largest known \[\[Trans-Neptunian Objects]] in the outer Solar System. It orbits beyond \[\[Neptune]] in the region commonly associated with the \[\[Kuiper Belt]] and is notable for its bright surface, methane-rich ices, extremely low temperatures, and at least one known moon.



Makemake is smaller than \[\[Pluto]] but large enough for its own gravity to have shaped it into an approximately rounded body. It is one of the five dwarf planets formally recognized by the International Astronomical Union, together with Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, and Eris.



Its surface chemistry and orbital history make it especially useful for studying volatile retention, radiation processing, dwarf-planet evolution, and the composition of the primordial outer Solar System.



\## Discovery, Name and Classification



Makemake was discovered in 2005 by a team led by Michael Brown, Chad Trujillo, and David Rabinowitz during surveys searching for large bodies beyond Neptune. It initially carried the provisional designation 2005 FY9.



Its discovery occurred during a period when several large trans-Neptunian bodies were being identified. The discoveries of Makemake, Haumea, and especially Eris contributed to the debate over how the term "planet" should be defined.



In 2006, the International Astronomical Union introduced the dwarf-planet category. Makemake was formally recognized as a dwarf planet in 2008.



The name Makemake comes from the creator deity Makemake in the mythology of the Rapa Nui people of Easter Island.



A dwarf planet must orbit the Sun, have sufficient gravity to achieve an approximately equilibrium shape, not be a satellite, and not have cleared its orbital neighborhood. Makemake satisfies these general criteria.



\## Orbit, Size and Physical Characteristics



Makemake travels around the Sun at an average distance of roughly 46 astronomical units. Its orbit is moderately eccentric and inclined relative to the main planetary plane. It takes approximately 305 Earth years to complete one revolution around the Sun.



Because its orbit lies well beyond Neptune, sunlight reaching Makemake is only a tiny fraction of that received by Earth. Surface temperatures are therefore only a few tens of kelvins.



Makemake is approximately 1,400 kilometers across, although exact dimensions continue to be refined through stellar occultations, thermal observations, and other measurements. It is larger than many trans-Neptunian objects but substantially smaller than Pluto.



Its mass is more difficult to determine accurately than its size because its satellite was discovered only relatively recently and its orbit still requires precise characterization. Observations of the moon's orbit can eventually improve estimates of Makemake's mass and density.



Makemake rotates once in roughly 22 to 23 hours. This is dramatically slower than the approximately 3.9-hour rotation of \[\[Haumea]].



Its surface has a relatively high albedo, meaning it reflects a significant fraction of the sunlight falling on it. The high reflectivity is consistent with abundant volatile frost on the surface.



\## Surface Composition and Volatile Ices



Makemake's surface is dominated by frozen volatile compounds, with methane ice being particularly important. Spectroscopy has detected strong methane absorption features, making methane one of the best-established components of its surface.



Other compounds associated with methane chemistry, including ethane and related hydrocarbons, may also occur. These can form when sunlight and energetic particles chemically process methane over long periods.



Nitrogen ice may be present in smaller quantities, although Makemake appears much less nitrogen-rich at the surface than Pluto. This difference is important for understanding why large trans-Neptunian bodies develop different surface and atmospheric conditions despite forming in broadly similar cold environments.



Methane itself is volatile, but Makemake is sufficiently cold and massive to retain substantial amounts over geological timescales. Volatile retention depends on several factors acting together: surface temperature, gravity, molecular mass, orbital distance, and the age of the body.



Its surface also appears reddish or brownish rather than pure white. Radiation acting on methane and other carbon-bearing materials can create increasingly complex organic compounds. These radiation-processed substances are often broadly described as tholin-like materials.



Tholins are not one specific chemical compound. They are complex organic-rich mixtures produced when simple carbon- and nitrogen-bearing substances are exposed to ultraviolet radiation or energetic particles. Similar processes are thought to contribute to reddish surfaces on Pluto, \[\[Sedna]], \[\[Centaurs]], and other distant bodies.



Makemake's surface is therefore shaped by competition between fresh volatile frost and long-term radiation chemistry. Frost deposition can brighten or renew a surface, while irradiation gradually modifies the chemistry and color of exposed material.



\## Atmosphere and Seasonal Volatile Behavior



Before detailed occultation measurements, scientists considered whether Makemake might possess a substantial Pluto-like atmosphere because of its methane-rich surface.



Stellar occultation observations showed that Makemake does not currently possess a significant global atmosphere comparable with Pluto's. Any atmosphere present today must be extremely tenuous.



This difference between Pluto and Makemake is one of the most important comparisons among dwarf planets. Pluto contains extensive nitrogen ice, and nitrogen sublimation supports a thin global atmosphere. Makemake appears to have much less accessible nitrogen, making it more difficult to sustain a similar atmosphere.



However, the absence of a dense global atmosphere does not mean that no gas can ever exist around Makemake. Localized or temporary atmospheres could form if volatile-rich regions receive sufficient solar heating.



As Makemake travels around the Sun, changing illumination and seasonal conditions may cause methane, nitrogen, or other volatile materials to migrate between different regions of the surface. Sublimation can move material into gas, while colder areas can trap the gas again as frost.



Makemake's atmosphere and volatile cycle may therefore be highly localized rather than globally uniform.



The dwarf planet's long orbital period means that its seasons operate over timescales far longer than human lifetimes. Long-term monitoring is needed to understand whether its surface frost distribution changes measurably.



\## Makemake's Moon



In 2015, observations with the Hubble Space Telescope revealed a small moon orbiting Makemake. The satellite has been provisionally designated:



S/2015 (136472) 1



and is often informally called:



MK2



The discovery was important because satellites provide one of the best methods for measuring the mass of a distant dwarf planet. Once the satellite's orbit is known precisely, Keplerian orbital dynamics can be used to calculate Makemake's gravitational mass.



MK2 is much darker than Makemake itself. This difference may indicate that the satellite cannot retain bright volatile frost as effectively as the larger dwarf planet.



Its lower gravity could allow methane and other volatile substances to escape more readily, leaving behind a darker radiation-processed surface.



The contrast between Makemake and MK2 therefore provides a natural experiment in volatile retention. Two objects in the same region receive similar sunlight and radiation, but their different gravity can produce very different surface appearances.



The origin of MK2 is not yet completely established. It may have formed after a collision, could represent material produced during early dwarf-planet formation, or could potentially have been captured. Determining its orbit, composition, and density can help distinguish between these possibilities.



\## Internal Structure and Geological Evolution



Makemake's interior has never been directly observed, but its size makes some degree of internal evolution likely.



A plausible internal structure could contain a rocky interior surrounded by water-rich ice and additional volatile compounds near the surface. If sufficient heating occurred early in its history, Makemake may have undergone \[\[Planetary Differentiation]], with denser rock moving inward and ice-rich material remaining above it.



Possible internal heat sources include radioactive decay within rocky material, energy released during accretion, and residual heat from early Solar System formation.



Whether Makemake ever developed a subsurface liquid-water layer is unknown. Large icy bodies can potentially preserve internal oceans if enough heat remains and if thick ice provides sufficient insulation. Ammonia or dissolved salts could also lower the freezing point of water.



There is currently no direct evidence for an ocean beneath Makemake.



\[\[Cryovolcanism]] is also possible in principle on sufficiently evolved icy dwarf planets, but no detailed geological images exist to confirm it on Makemake. Unlike Pluto, Makemake has never been visited by a spacecraft, so scientists cannot currently resolve mountains, faults, glaciers, craters, or possible cryovolcanoes.



Its surface could contain ancient impact craters alongside areas altered by volatile frost transport. Stellar occultations and telescopic measurements reveal its global properties but cannot yet provide a geological map.



\## Formation and Outer Solar System Evolution



Makemake formed from icy planetesimals in the distant protoplanetary disk during \[\[Planet Formation]]. Its original building materials probably included water ice, rock, methane-bearing compounds, and other volatile-rich substances available in the cold outer Solar System.



The modern orbit of Makemake must also be considered in the context of \[\[Planetary Migration]]. Neptune is believed to have migrated outward during the early Solar System, dramatically restructuring the trans-Neptunian region.



As Neptune moved, its resonances swept through the primordial planetesimal disk. Some bodies were trapped into resonances, some were scattered, and others remained in relatively stable populations.



Makemake belongs to the dynamically excited trans-Neptunian population rather than the very low-inclination cold classical population. Its inclination and orbital history suggest that it experienced substantial dynamical evolution.



The presence of large dwarf planets such as Pluto, Eris, Haumea, and Makemake shows that the primordial outer Solar System contained enough material for some planetesimals to grow to planetary scales.



The present \[\[Kuiper Belt]] contains far less mass than the original planetesimal disk probably contained. Much of the original material was scattered inward, scattered outward, placed into the \[\[Oort Cloud]], accreted by planets, or ejected from the Solar System.



Makemake is therefore one of the surviving large remnants of that much more massive ancient population.



\## Comparison With Other Dwarf Planets



Makemake and \[\[Pluto]] are both methane-bearing dwarf planets, but their present environments differ significantly. Pluto has nitrogen glaciers, a thin global atmosphere, extensive geological activity, five known moons, and complex seasonal volatile transport. Makemake has no detected substantial global atmosphere, appears less nitrogen-rich, and has one known moon.



Makemake and \[\[Haumea]] also provide a strong contrast. Haumea rotates extremely rapidly, has an elongated shape, two known moons, a ring, a water-ice-dominated surface, and a recognized collisional family. Makemake rotates much more slowly and has a surface dominated by methane-related volatile chemistry.



Eris is more massive than Makemake and travels on a much more eccentric and distant orbit. Its surface is extremely reflective and contains methane frost. Eris may develop atmospheric effects near perihelion, while much of its atmosphere is expected to freeze onto the surface when it is far from the Sun.



\[\[Sedna]] is dynamically very different. Sedna has a perihelion far beyond Neptune and is classified as a detached object, whereas Makemake belongs much closer to the conventional Kuiper Belt region.



These comparisons demonstrate that dwarf planets cannot be understood as a single uniform category. Objects with similar size and formation era can evolve differently because of composition, mass, orbital history, collisions, rotation, and volatile retention.



Makemake is particularly valuable for studying the transition between bodies capable of retaining significant volatile frost and those capable of sustaining a substantial atmosphere.



\## Observation and Exploration



Because Makemake is extremely distant, almost everything known about it comes from remote observations.



Photometry measures its brightness and rotational changes. Spectroscopy reveals methane and other surface materials. Infrared measurements estimate its temperature and size. Stellar occultations constrain its dimensions and atmosphere, while observations of MK2 help determine the system's mass.



A stellar occultation occurs when Makemake passes in front of a distant star. Observers located along the occultation path measure the exact times at which the star disappears and reappears.



By combining measurements from multiple locations, astronomers can constrain the body's size and shape and search for an atmosphere, rings, or nearby material.



No spacecraft has yet visited Makemake.



A future mission could directly determine its geology, crater distribution, topography, surface composition, moon properties, gravitational field, density, and any local atmosphere or exosphere.



Such a mission would also allow direct comparison with \[\[New Horizons]] observations of Pluto and Arrokoth.



Reaching Makemake would be difficult because it lies more than forty astronomical units from the Sun. A spacecraft would face long travel times, weak sunlight, large communication delays, low data rates, and demanding trajectory requirements.



Radioisotope power would likely be valuable for a mission operating at such distances.



Until a spacecraft arrives, large telescopes and observatories such as the \[\[James Webb Space Telescope]] can improve measurements of its infrared surface chemistry.



\## Scientific Importance, Misconceptions and Open Questions



Makemake is especially important for understanding why apparently similar trans-Neptunian dwarf planets develop different atmospheric and surface states.



Its abundant methane but lack of a substantial global atmosphere helps scientists test models of volatile retention. The comparison between bright Makemake and dark MK2 provides further information about how body size and gravity control the survival of volatile frost.



Makemake also preserves material from the early outer Solar System. Studying its composition therefore contributes to understanding the chemical conditions in which icy planetesimals formed.



Several misconceptions should be avoided. Makemake is not a moon of Neptune; it independently orbits the Sun. It is not simply an asteroid but a recognized dwarf planet. Its methane surface does not imply liquid methane oceans—the surface is far too cold, and methane exists primarily as ice. The absence of a substantial atmosphere today does not prove that Makemake never had one or can never develop temporary localized gas. Its reddish surface also does not mean it is warm; the color reflects composition and radiation chemistry.



Major open questions include Makemake's exact mass and density, the detailed orbit and origin of MK2, the abundance of nitrogen ice, the extent of seasonal volatile transport, and whether localized atmospheric regions can form.



Scientists also want to know whether Makemake differentiated internally, whether it ever possessed a subsurface ocean, whether cryovolcanism occurred, and what its unresolved surface geology looks like.



Future observations of its moon and future stellar occultations can substantially improve models of its shape and mass, while eventual spacecraft exploration would reveal whether Makemake is geologically ancient and inactive or much more complex than its distant telescopic appearance suggests.



\## Summary



Makemake is a methane-rich dwarf planet in the trans-Neptunian region beyond Neptune. It was discovered in 2005 and became formally recognized as a dwarf planet in 2008.



It is roughly 1,400 kilometers across and requires about 305 years to orbit the Sun. Its extremely cold, relatively bright surface contains abundant methane ice and radiation-processed organic-rich compounds.



Unlike Pluto, Makemake does not currently possess a substantial global atmosphere. Its apparent shortage of accessible nitrogen may be one reason for this difference, although localized or seasonal gas may still be possible.



Makemake has at least one moon, provisionally designated S/2015 (136472) 1 and commonly called MK2. The moon is much darker than Makemake, possibly because its weaker gravity cannot retain bright volatile frost as effectively.



Makemake probably formed from icy planetesimals in the primordial outer Solar System and later experienced the dynamical effects of Neptune's migration.



Although its detailed geology and interior remain unknown, it may be differentiated and could potentially have experienced internal water-rich evolution.



Makemake therefore provides an important comparison with \[\[Pluto]], \[\[Haumea]], \[\[Sedna]], and other \[\[Trans-Neptunian Objects]] and helps scientists understand volatile retention, dwarf-planet formation, surface chemistry, and the evolution of the \[\[Kuiper Belt]].
