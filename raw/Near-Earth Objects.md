\# Near-Earth Objects



\## Overview



Near-Earth Objects, commonly abbreviated as NEOs, are asteroids and comets whose orbits bring them into the neighborhood of Earth's orbit.



NEOs are scientifically important because they:



\- Preserve material from the early Solar System

\- Provide information about asteroid and comet evolution

\- Can become targets for spacecraft exploration

\- May contain useful resources

\- Can potentially collide with Earth

\- Are therefore central to planetary-defense research



Most known Near-Earth Objects do not pose a significant impact threat to Earth.



Astronomers continuously discover, track, and calculate the orbits of NEOs to determine whether any could become hazardous.



\## Basic Definition



A Near-Earth Object is a natural Solar System body whose orbit approaches Earth's orbital region.



NEOs primarily include:



\- Near-Earth Asteroids

\- Near-Earth Comets



The majority of known NEOs are asteroids.



\## Near-Earth Asteroid



A Near-Earth Asteroid, or NEA, is an asteroid whose orbit brings it relatively close to Earth's orbit.



NEAs are divided into several important orbital groups:



\- Atira asteroids

\- Aten asteroids

\- Apollo asteroids

\- Amor asteroids



\## Near-Earth Comet



A Near-Earth Comet is a comet whose orbit brings it relatively close to Earth.



Near-Earth comets are much less numerous than Near-Earth Asteroids among known NEO populations.



Because comets can have highly eccentric orbits, some can approach Earth at very high relative velocities.



\## Formal Orbital Definition



A commonly used definition classifies an object as a Near-Earth Object when its perihelion distance is less than approximately:



1.3 AU



Perihelion is the point in an object's orbit closest to the Sun.



Therefore:



q < 1.3 AU



is commonly used as the basic NEO criterion.



\## Astronomical Unit



One astronomical unit, or AU, is the average distance between Earth and the Sun.



1 AU ≈ 149.6 million kilometers.



Earth's average orbital distance from the Sun is therefore approximately:



1 AU



\## NEO Does Not Mean Dangerous



The term Near-Earth Object does not automatically mean that the object is on a collision course with Earth.



It only describes an orbital category.



Most NEOs pass Earth safely.



An object's actual risk depends on:



\- Orbit

\- Size

\- Velocity

\- Future gravitational interactions

\- Orbital uncertainty



\## Potentially Hazardous Asteroids



A Potentially Hazardous Asteroid, commonly abbreviated PHA, is a Near-Earth Asteroid that satisfies criteria related to both its size and how closely its orbit can approach Earth's orbit.



A commonly used criterion includes:



Minimum Orbit Intersection Distance with Earth of approximately 0.05 AU or less



and



Absolute magnitude H of approximately 22 or brighter.



Absolute magnitude is used as a proxy for size because exact asteroid diameters are often initially unknown.



\## Potentially Hazardous Does Not Mean Impact Is Expected



A PHA is not necessarily expected to collide with Earth.



The classification means that its size and orbit make it important enough to monitor carefully.



Many PHAs have well-determined orbits showing that they pose no significant threat for the foreseeable future.



\## Minimum Orbit Intersection Distance



Minimum Orbit Intersection Distance, or MOID, measures the minimum possible separation between the osculating orbital paths of two objects.



For Earth and an asteroid, this is called Earth MOID.



A small MOID does not mean that the two objects will actually occupy the crossing point at the same time.



Therefore, MOID alone does not predict an impact.



\## NEO Orbital Groups



Near-Earth Asteroids are commonly classified into several groups based on their orbital properties.



The principal groups are:



\- Atiras

\- Atens

\- Apollos

\- Amors



\## Atira Asteroids



Atira asteroids have orbits contained largely inside Earth's orbit.



Their aphelion distances are smaller than Earth's perihelion distance.



They are sometimes called Interior-Earth Objects.



Because they remain close to the Sun in Earth's sky, they can be difficult to discover using conventional nighttime surveys.



\## Aten Asteroids



Aten asteroids have semimajor axes smaller than 1 AU but can cross Earth's orbit.



Their orbital periods are generally shorter than one Earth year.



Some Aten asteroids can therefore make relatively close approaches to Earth.



\## Apollo Asteroids



Apollo asteroids have semimajor axes greater than 1 AU and perihelion distances that bring them inside Earth's orbital region.



They are Earth-crossing asteroids.



The Apollo group contains many known Near-Earth Asteroids.



\## Amor Asteroids



Amor asteroids approach Earth's orbit from outside but generally do not currently cross Earth's orbit.



Their perihelia lie outside Earth's orbit but relatively nearby.



Some can experience gravitational changes that alter their orbits over long timescales.



\## Orbital Elements



Scientists describe NEO orbits using orbital elements.



Important parameters include:



\- Semimajor axis

\- Eccentricity

\- Inclination

\- Longitude of ascending node

\- Argument of perihelion

\- Mean anomaly



These parameters allow astronomers to calculate an object's trajectory around the Sun.



\## Semimajor Axis



The semimajor axis describes the overall size of an elliptical orbit.



For Earth:



a ≈ 1 AU



Objects with larger semimajor axes generally have longer orbital periods.



\## Eccentricity



Eccentricity describes how elongated an orbit is.



An eccentricity of:



0



represents a circular orbit.



Values closer to:



1



represent increasingly elongated elliptical orbits.



Many NEOs have more eccentric orbits than Earth.



\## Inclination



Inclination describes the angle between an object's orbital plane and a reference plane.



NEOs can have:



\- Low inclinations

\- Moderate inclinations

\- High inclinations



Orbital inclination strongly affects encounter geometry.



\## Perihelion



Perihelion is the point in an object's orbit closest to the Sun.



It is represented by:



q



The perihelion distance is especially important for determining whether an object qualifies as an NEO.



\## Aphelion



Aphelion is the point in an object's orbit farthest from the Sun.



It is represented by:



Q



\## Where Do Near-Earth Asteroids Come From?



Most Near-Earth Asteroids are believed to originate in the main asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.



Their orbits can gradually change through:



\- Gravitational resonances

\- Collisions

\- Planetary encounters

\- Thermal forces



Eventually, some are transported into near-Earth space.



\## Main Asteroid Belt



The main asteroid belt lies primarily between:



Mars and Jupiter



It contains large numbers of rocky and metallic bodies.



Most asteroids remain safely within the belt.



Only a small fraction evolve onto near-Earth trajectories.



\## Jupiter's Role



Jupiter's powerful gravity creates resonances within the asteroid belt.



Objects entering certain resonances can experience increasing orbital eccentricity.



Eventually, their orbits may cross those of the terrestrial planets.



\## Kirkwood Gaps



The asteroid belt contains regions with relatively few asteroids known as Kirkwood gaps.



Many correspond to orbital resonances with Jupiter.



These resonances can destabilize asteroid orbits.



\## 3:1 Resonance



The 3:1 mean-motion resonance with Jupiter is an important pathway for transporting asteroids from the main belt toward near-Earth space.



An asteroid in this region completes approximately three orbits for every one orbit of Jupiter.



The resonance can increase orbital eccentricity.



\## ν6 Secular Resonance



The ν6 secular resonance is another major dynamical pathway.



It can increase asteroid orbital eccentricities and send objects toward the inner Solar System.



It is particularly important near the inner edge of the main asteroid belt.



\## Yarkovsky Effect



The Yarkovsky effect is a small force caused by uneven thermal radiation from a rotating asteroid.



An asteroid absorbs sunlight and later emits thermal infrared radiation.



Because the emission is not perfectly symmetrical, it creates a tiny thrust.



Over millions of years, this can gradually change an asteroid's semimajor axis.



\## Importance of the Yarkovsky Effect



Although extremely weak at any moment, the Yarkovsky effect can move small asteroids into orbital resonances.



Once inside a strong resonance, planetary gravitational effects can dramatically alter their orbits.



Therefore, thermal physics can indirectly help transport asteroids into near-Earth space.



\## YORP Effect



The YORP effect is related to uneven absorption and re-emission of sunlight.



It can change an asteroid's:



\- Rotation rate

\- Spin-axis orientation



Over long timescales, YORP can cause an asteroid to rotate faster or slower.



Rapid rotation can sometimes contribute to structural changes or fragmentation.



\## Asteroid Collisions



Collisions in the asteroid belt can produce fragments.



Some fragments enter dynamically unstable regions.



These fragments may eventually become NEOs.



\## Lifetime of NEOs



Near-Earth orbits are generally not permanently stable over the age of the Solar System.



Typical NEO dynamical lifetimes are much shorter than the age of the Solar System.



Eventually an NEO may:



\- Collide with the Sun

\- Collide with a planet

\- Be ejected from the inner Solar System

\- Be scattered into another orbit



The population is continuously replenished from source regions.



\## Close Approach



A close approach occurs when an NEO passes relatively near Earth.



Close approaches happen regularly.



A close approach does not necessarily indicate danger.



Astronomers calculate the distance and uncertainty of each encounter.



\## Lunar Distance



Close approaches are sometimes described using lunar distance.



One lunar distance is the average distance between Earth and the Moon.



1 LD ≈ 384,400 km



Some small asteroids pass inside the Moon's orbit.



This still does not necessarily mean they will strike Earth.



\## Earth's Size Compared With Space



Earth is extremely small compared with the enormous volume through which NEOs travel.



Therefore, even objects whose orbits cross Earth's orbital path usually miss the planet.



Timing is crucial.



Both objects must reach the same location simultaneously for a collision to occur.



\## Impact Probability



Impact probability is calculated using observations and orbital models.



Scientists consider:



\- Current orbit

\- Measurement uncertainty

\- Future planetary perturbations

\- Non-gravitational effects



As more observations become available, orbital uncertainty generally decreases.



\## Orbit Determination



When an asteroid is first discovered, astronomers measure its position against background stars.



Multiple observations taken over time allow its orbit to be estimated.



Additional observations improve the calculation.



\## Astrometry



Astrometry is the precise measurement of astronomical positions and motions.



Astrometric observations are essential for calculating NEO trajectories.



\## Observation Arc



The observation arc is the period between the first and last observations used to determine an object's orbit.



A longer observation arc generally improves orbital accuracy.



\## Precovery



Sometimes an asteroid is discovered in older archived images taken before its official discovery.



Finding these earlier observations is called precovery.



Precovery can greatly extend the observation arc and improve orbital predictions.



\## Radar Observations



Radar has historically been extremely valuable for some close-approaching NEOs.



Radar measurements can provide precise information about:



\- Distance

\- Velocity

\- Size

\- Shape

\- Rotation

\- Surface properties



Radar observations can significantly improve orbital calculations.



\## Optical Telescopes



Most NEOs are discovered using optical survey telescopes.



Astronomers repeatedly photograph large areas of the sky.



Moving points of light are identified against background stars.



\## Infrared Observations



Infrared telescopes are particularly useful for measuring asteroid thermal emission.



They can help estimate:



\- Diameter

\- Surface temperature

\- Reflectivity



This is important because visible brightness alone does not directly determine size.



\## Albedo



Albedo is a measure of how much light an object reflects.



A bright, reflective asteroid can appear similar in visible brightness to a larger but darker asteroid.



Therefore, diameter cannot always be determined accurately from visible brightness alone.



\## Absolute Magnitude



Asteroid absolute magnitude is represented by:



H



It describes how bright an asteroid would appear under standardized viewing conditions.



Absolute magnitude can be combined with an assumed or measured albedo to estimate diameter.



\## Discovery Bias



NEO surveys have observational biases.



Large bright objects are easier to discover.



Small dark objects are more difficult.



Objects approaching from the direction of the Sun are particularly difficult to detect using conventional ground-based nighttime telescopes.



\## Objects From the Sun's Direction



Some dangerous objects could approach Earth from directions close to the Sun in the sky.



Ground-based optical telescopes have difficulty observing these regions because of sunlight.



Space-based infrared surveys can help reduce this observational gap.



\## Near-Earth Object Surveys



Modern NEO surveys repeatedly scan the sky to discover moving objects.



Important goals include:



\- Finding new NEOs

\- Measuring positions

\- Determining orbits

\- Identifying close approaches

\- Detecting potentially hazardous objects



\## Planetary Defense



Planetary defense refers to efforts to detect, track, characterize, and potentially mitigate asteroids or comets that could threaten Earth.



Major components include:



\- Discovery

\- Tracking

\- Orbit prediction

\- Physical characterization

\- Impact probability assessment

\- Deflection planning

\- Emergency preparedness



\## Planetary Defense Coordination Office



NASA maintains a Planetary Defense Coordination Office.



Its responsibilities include coordinating efforts related to:



\- Detection of potentially hazardous objects

\- Tracking

\- Characterization

\- Impact-warning information

\- Mitigation planning



\## International Cooperation



Planetary defense is an international problem.



An impact could affect multiple countries or produce global consequences.



International organizations coordinate:



\- Observations

\- Orbit calculations

\- Risk communication

\- Emergency planning



\## Minor Planet Center



The Minor Planet Center collects and distributes observational data for small Solar System bodies.



It plays a central role in:



\- Recording observations

\- Assigning designations

\- Maintaining orbital information

\- Supporting asteroid and comet research



\## Impact Monitoring



Specialized systems continuously calculate whether known NEOs could potentially impact Earth in the future.



Possible trajectories are repeatedly updated as new observations become available.



\## Torino Scale



The Torino Scale communicates the impact hazard associated with a Near-Earth Object.



It combines:



\- Impact probability

\- Potential impact energy



The scale ranges from:



0 to 10



Level 0 indicates essentially no hazard.



Higher levels represent increasingly serious situations.



\## Palermo Technical Impact Hazard Scale



The Palermo Scale is a more technical risk measure used by specialists.



It compares a particular object's impact risk with the average background risk from objects of similar size or larger.



\## Newly Discovered Objects and Temporary Risk



A newly discovered asteroid may initially show a small impact probability.



This does not necessarily mean the threat is increasing.



The apparent risk can result from uncertainty in its poorly known orbit.



As additional observations improve the orbit, Earth is often excluded from the range of possible future positions.



The object's risk rating can then fall to zero.



\## Risk Corridor



When an impact has not yet been ruled out, orbital uncertainty may produce a range of possible impact locations across Earth's surface.



This can sometimes be represented as a risk corridor.



As observations improve, the corridor changes or disappears if impact is excluded.



\## Impact Velocity



NEOs can strike Earth at velocities of many kilometers per second.



Impact velocity depends on:



\- Object orbit

\- Earth's orbital velocity

\- Encounter direction

\- Gravitational acceleration by Earth



High velocity means even relatively small objects can carry enormous kinetic energy.



\## Kinetic Energy



The kinetic energy of an object is:



E = 1/2 mv²



where:



E = kinetic energy



m = mass



v = velocity



Because velocity is squared, high-speed impacts release enormous amounts of energy.



\## Atmospheric Entry



Small NEOs entering Earth's atmosphere usually:



\- Heat

\- Ablate

\- Fragment

\- Vaporize



Larger objects can penetrate deeper.



\## Airburst



An airburst occurs when an incoming object releases much of its energy in the atmosphere.



It does not need to strike the ground to cause damage.



Shock waves from an airburst can damage structures over large areas.



\## Chelyabinsk



On February 15, 2013, an approximately 20-meter asteroid entered Earth's atmosphere over Russia.



It produced an extremely bright fireball and powerful airburst.



The shock wave:



\- Damaged buildings

\- Broke windows

\- Injured many people



Most injuries resulted from broken glass.



\## Importance of Chelyabinsk



The Chelyabinsk event demonstrated that relatively small asteroids can cause significant local damage.



It also highlighted the difficulty of detecting objects approaching from near the Sun's direction.



\## Tunguska



On June 30, 1908, a much larger atmospheric explosion occurred over Siberia.



The Tunguska event flattened a huge area of forest.



The most widely accepted explanation is an asteroid or cometary body that exploded in the atmosphere.



\## Larger Impacts



As impactor size increases, potential consequences become more severe.



Possible effects include:



\- Blast waves

\- Thermal radiation

\- Crater formation

\- Earthquakes

\- Tsunamis

\- Atmospheric dust

\- Climate effects



\## Kilometer-Scale Objects



Kilometer-scale asteroid impacts are rare but potentially capable of causing global consequences.



For this reason, finding large NEOs has been a major objective of planetary-defense surveys.



\## Chicxulub Impactor



Approximately 66 million years ago, an asteroid roughly 10 kilometers across struck Earth near the present-day Yucatán Peninsula.



The event created the Chicxulub crater.



It contributed to a global mass extinction that eliminated the non-avian dinosaurs and many other species.



\## Impact Frequency



Small objects strike Earth much more frequently than large objects.



Tiny particles enter the atmosphere continuously.



Meter-scale objects enter less frequently.



Tens-of-meters objects are rarer.



Kilometer-scale impacts are extremely rare on human timescales.



\## Why Large Objects Are Easier to Find



Large asteroids generally reflect more sunlight and can be detected at greater distances.



Therefore, surveys are more complete for large NEOs than for small ones.



Many small NEOs remain undiscovered.



\## Asteroid Deflection



If a hazardous asteroid were discovered sufficiently early, it might be possible to change its orbit.



The objective would normally not be to destroy the asteroid.



Instead, a very small velocity change applied years before the predicted encounter could cause the asteroid to miss Earth.



\## Why Early Detection Matters



A tiny orbital change accumulates over time.



Therefore:



Small deflection + long warning time = potentially large change in future position



This makes early discovery one of the most important components of planetary defense.



\## Kinetic Impactor



A kinetic impactor is a spacecraft intentionally crashed into an asteroid.



Momentum transferred during the collision changes the asteroid's velocity slightly.



Given enough time, this can alter its future trajectory substantially.



\## DART Mission



NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test, or DART, was the first full-scale demonstration of asteroid deflection technology.



DART targeted:



Dimorphos



Dimorphos is a small moon orbiting the asteroid:



Didymos



DART struck Dimorphos on September 26, 2022.



\## Result of DART



The impact changed the orbital period of Dimorphos around Didymos.



The mission demonstrated that a kinetic impactor can measurably alter the motion of an asteroid.



This was an important milestone for planetary defense.



\## Momentum Enhancement



When DART struck Dimorphos, material was ejected from the asteroid.



The escaping ejecta provided additional momentum.



Therefore, the orbital change was greater than would have resulted from the spacecraft's momentum alone.



\## Hera Mission



ESA's Hera mission is designed to study the Didymos-Dimorphos system after the DART impact.



Its observations are intended to improve understanding of:



\- Impact crater

\- Dimorphos mass

\- Internal structure

\- Momentum transfer

\- Binary asteroid dynamics



These measurements help improve future deflection models.



\## Gravity Tractor



A gravity tractor is a proposed asteroid-deflection method.



A spacecraft would hover near an asteroid.



The spacecraft's gravity would exert a tiny continuous pull on the asteroid.



Over a long period, this could gradually change its orbit.



Advantages include precise control.



The major limitation is that it requires substantial warning time.



\## Nuclear Deflection



For certain extreme scenarios with limited warning time or very large objects, nuclear devices have been studied as a possible mitigation technique.



The objective could be to alter the object's velocity through energy deposition rather than simply fragment it.



This remains a contingency concept rather than a routinely used planetary-defense method.



\## Why Fragmentation Can Be Complicated



Breaking an asteroid into pieces does not automatically remove the threat.



Some fragments could remain on Earth-impacting trajectories.



Therefore, controlled orbital deflection with sufficient warning time is generally preferable when feasible.



\## Civil Defense



If an object is too small for spacecraft deflection or discovered too late, civil-defense measures may be more appropriate.



These can include:



\- Evacuation

\- Shelter guidance

\- Window safety

\- Emergency response

\- Impact-zone prediction



\## Asteroid Physical Properties



Successful deflection depends on understanding the asteroid.



Important properties include:



\- Size

\- Mass

\- Shape

\- Density

\- Rotation

\- Composition

\- Internal structure

\- Surface strength



\## Rubble-Pile Asteroids



Many small asteroids are not solid monolithic rocks.



Instead, they may consist of collections of:



\- Rocks

\- Boulders

\- Dust

\- Regolith



held together by weak gravity.



These are called rubble-pile asteroids.



\## Why Internal Structure Matters



A solid asteroid and a rubble-pile asteroid can respond differently to:



\- Spacecraft impacts

\- Explosions

\- Rotation

\- Tidal forces



Therefore, asteroid characterization is essential for mitigation planning.



\## Binary Asteroids



Some NEOs consist of two gravitationally bound bodies.



Others have small moons.



Binary systems are scientifically important for measuring asteroid:



\- Mass

\- Density

\- Internal structure

\- Formation history



Didymos and Dimorphos are an example.



\## Contact Binaries



Some asteroids consist of two lobes touching each other.



These are called contact binaries.



They may form through:



\- Gentle collisions

\- Binary orbital evolution

\- Rotational processes



\## Asteroid Rotation



Asteroids rotate around their axes.



Rotation periods range widely.



Some small asteroids rotate extremely rapidly.



Rotation affects:



\- Surface stability

\- Thermal forces

\- Spacecraft operations

\- Deflection behavior



\## Spin Barrier



Many rubble-pile asteroids larger than a few hundred meters cannot rotate much faster than roughly one rotation every couple of hours without beginning to lose material.



This approximate limit is sometimes called the rubble-pile spin barrier.



Cohesive strength can allow smaller asteroids to rotate faster.



\## Asteroid Shape



Small asteroids are often irregular because their gravity is too weak to make them spherical.



Possible shapes include:



\- Elongated

\- Bilobed

\- Top-shaped

\- Irregular



\## Top-Shaped Asteroids



Some rapidly rotating asteroids develop broad equatorial ridges.



Examples include:



\- Bennu

\- Ryugu



Their shapes may result from rotation and movement of surface material.



\## Asteroid Regolith



Regolith is loose material covering an asteroid's surface.



It can contain:



\- Dust

\- Pebbles

\- Rocks

\- Boulders



Asteroid regolith behaves differently from soil on Earth because asteroid gravity is extremely weak.



\## Bennu



101955 Bennu is a Near-Earth Asteroid.



It is approximately half a kilometer across.



Bennu is a carbon-rich rubble-pile asteroid.



NASA's OSIRIS-REx spacecraft studied Bennu in detail and collected a surface sample.



\## OSIRIS-REx



OSIRIS-REx launched in 2016.



It reached Bennu in 2018.



The spacecraft:



\- Mapped Bennu

\- Measured its orbit

\- Studied its composition

\- Investigated the Yarkovsky effect

\- Collected surface material



The sample returned to Earth in 2023.



\## Importance of Bennu Samples



Bennu samples provide information about:



\- Primitive asteroid material

\- Carbon chemistry

\- Water-bearing minerals

\- Early Solar System history

\- Organic compounds



They also help improve understanding of potentially hazardous asteroids.



\## Bennu Impact Risk



Bennu makes periodic close approaches to Earth.



Scientists have calculated its orbit far into the future.



There is a very small possibility of impact during future centuries, which makes Bennu an important object for precise orbital research.



This does not mean an impact is expected.



\## Apophis



99942 Apophis is a well-known Near-Earth Asteroid.



After its discovery in 2004, early observations temporarily indicated a concerning probability of a future Earth impact.



Additional observations dramatically improved its orbit and eliminated the previously predicted impact possibilities.



\## Apophis 2029 Flyby



Apophis will make an exceptionally close but safe approach to Earth on:



April 13, 2029



It will pass closer than many geostationary satellites.



The encounter provides an unusual opportunity to study how Earth's gravity affects an asteroid.



\## Apophis and Earth Impact



Apophis is not expected to strike Earth during its 2029 close approach.



Modern orbital calculations have ruled out the previously discussed major impact scenarios for the foreseeable future.



\## Gravitational Keyhole



A gravitational keyhole is a small region in space during a close planetary encounter.



If an asteroid passes through a particular keyhole, the planet's gravity can alter its orbit so that it returns on a future collision trajectory.



Keyholes are important in long-term impact calculations.



\## Apophis and Keyholes



Earlier concerns about Apophis involved possible gravitational keyholes during close encounters.



Improved observations eventually ruled out those dangerous trajectories.



\## 433 Eros



433 Eros is a large Near-Earth Asteroid belonging to the Amor group.



NASA's NEAR Shoemaker spacecraft became the first spacecraft to orbit an asteroid.



It studied Eros extensively and eventually landed on its surface in 2001.



\## Itokawa



25143 Itokawa is a small Near-Earth Asteroid.



Japan's Hayabusa spacecraft visited Itokawa and returned samples to Earth.



Observations showed that Itokawa is a rubble-pile asteroid.



\## Hayabusa



The Hayabusa mission demonstrated technologies for:



\- Asteroid rendezvous

\- Surface sampling

\- Sample return



Its samples confirmed links between certain asteroids and ordinary chondrite meteorites.



\## Ryugu



162173 Ryugu is a carbon-rich Near-Earth Asteroid.



Japan's Hayabusa2 spacecraft explored Ryugu and returned samples to Earth.



Ryugu is a rubble-pile asteroid with a distinctive top-like shape.



\## Hayabusa2



Hayabusa2:



\- Orbited Ryugu

\- Deployed small surface vehicles

\- Collected samples

\- Created an artificial impact crater

\- Returned asteroid material to Earth



The samples provide information about primitive carbon-rich Solar System material.



\## Near-Earth Comets



Some comets can enter near-Earth space.



Compared with asteroids, long-period comets can be especially challenging because they may:



\- Approach at high velocity

\- Have highly eccentric orbits

\- Be discovered with shorter warning times



However, large long-period comet impacts are rare.



\## NEOs and Meteorites



Small fragments from Near-Earth Asteroids can enter Earth's atmosphere.



If material survives atmospheric passage and reaches the ground, it becomes a meteorite.



Therefore, meteorites provide physical samples of some asteroid populations.



\## NEOs and Meteor Showers



Meteor showers are generally produced by much smaller debris particles than the objects normally emphasized in planetary-defense NEO programs.



Comets and some asteroid-like bodies can produce these debris streams.



\## NEOs and the Moon



The Moon's cratered surface demonstrates the long history of impacts throughout the Solar System.



Unlike Earth, the Moon lacks:



\- Strong erosion

\- Oceans

\- Plate tectonics



Therefore, ancient impact craters remain much better preserved.



\## Earth's Impact Record



Earth has also experienced impacts throughout its history.



However, many old craters have been erased or altered by:



\- Plate tectonics

\- Weathering

\- Erosion

\- Sedimentation

\- Oceans

\- Volcanism



\## NEO Composition



Near-Earth Asteroids can have different compositions.



Broad types include:



\- Carbon-rich

\- Silicate-rich

\- Metal-rich



Composition depends strongly on the object's parent body and formation history.



\## C-Type Asteroids



C-type asteroids are carbon-rich and generally dark.



They can contain primitive material and hydrated minerals.



Bennu and Ryugu are examples of carbon-rich asteroids.



\## S-Type Asteroids



S-type asteroids are rich in silicate minerals and contain varying amounts of metal.



They are generally brighter than many carbonaceous asteroids.



\## M-Type Asteroids



M-type asteroids are traditionally associated with metal-rich compositions, although modern observations show that the category is more compositionally diverse than originally assumed.



\## Spectroscopy



Astronomers use spectroscopy to study asteroid composition.



The reflected-light spectrum can reveal information about surface minerals.



Comparisons with meteorites help connect asteroid populations to physical samples.



\## Density



Asteroid density helps reveal internal composition and porosity.



A low-density asteroid may contain:



\- Large void spaces

\- High porosity

\- Rubble-pile structure



\## Porosity



Porosity describes empty space within an object.



A rubble-pile asteroid can contain substantial internal voids.



Porosity strongly influences:



\- Density

\- Strength

\- Impact response



\## NEO Resources



Near-Earth Asteroids may contain useful resources.



Potential materials include:



\- Water

\- Iron

\- Nickel

\- Silicates

\- Platinum-group elements



Water could potentially be used for:



\- Drinking

\- Oxygen production

\- Hydrogen production

\- Rocket propellant



\## Asteroid Mining



Asteroid mining has been proposed as a future space industry.



However, major challenges remain:



\- Transportation

\- Extraction technology

\- Economics

\- Legal frameworks

\- Processing

\- Mission risk



No large-scale commercial asteroid-mining industry currently exists.



\## NEOs as Exploration Targets



NEOs can be attractive spacecraft targets because some require less energy to reach than more distant Solar System bodies.



Missions to NEOs can test technologies for:



\- Navigation

\- Sampling

\- Resource utilization

\- Planetary defense

\- Human exploration



\## NEOs and Solar System Formation



Asteroids are remnants of the planet-building process.



They preserve evidence about:



\- Protoplanetary disk

\- Planetesimal formation

\- Collisions

\- Differentiation

\- Water distribution

\- Organic chemistry



\## NEOs and Origin of Earth's Water



Water-rich asteroids may have contributed substantially to Earth's water during early Solar System history.



Scientists compare isotopic signatures in:



\- Meteorites

\- Asteroid samples

\- Comets

\- Earth water



to investigate this question.



\## NEOs and Organic Molecules



Carbon-rich asteroids contain organic compounds.



Samples from primitive asteroids help scientists investigate chemical processes that occurred before life developed on Earth.



This does not mean that asteroid samples demonstrate extraterrestrial life.



\## NEOs Versus Main-Belt Asteroids



Near-Earth Asteroids:



\- Have orbits approaching Earth's orbital region

\- Are dynamically temporary populations

\- Often originated in the main belt



Main-belt asteroids:



\- Primarily orbit between Mars and Jupiter

\- Usually remain far from Earth

\- Represent the main source population for many NEAs



\## NEOs Versus Potentially Hazardous Objects



Every potentially hazardous asteroid is a Near-Earth Asteroid.



Not every Near-Earth Asteroid is potentially hazardous.



The potentially hazardous classification requires additional orbital and size-related criteria.



\## NEOs Versus Meteoroids



NEOs usually refer to larger cataloged asteroids and comets in near-Earth space.



Meteoroids are smaller natural solid bodies.



The terminology boundary is partly conventional.



\## NEOs Versus Comets



NEO describes orbital proximity to Earth.



Asteroid and comet describe physical and observational characteristics.



Therefore, both asteroids and comets can qualify as NEOs.



\## NEOs Versus PHAs



NEO:



Object whose orbit enters the defined near-Earth region.



PHA:



Near-Earth Asteroid satisfying additional criteria indicating that its size and orbital approach make it worthy of particular monitoring.



\## NEOs Versus Impacting Objects



An NEO is not necessarily an impactor.



An impactor is an object whose calculated trajectory actually intersects Earth at the correct time.



Most NEOs never impact Earth.



\## Common Misconceptions



\### Every Near-Earth Object Is Dangerous



Incorrect.



Most known NEOs pose no significant threat.



\### Near-Earth Means the Object Is Currently Near Earth



Incorrect.



NEO is an orbital classification.



An object may currently be far away.



\### A Potentially Hazardous Asteroid Will Eventually Hit Earth



Incorrect.



Potentially hazardous means the object satisfies monitoring criteria.



It does not mean an impact is predicted.



\### A Small MOID Means an Impact Will Occur



Incorrect.



MOID compares orbital paths.



The objects must also reach the intersection region at the same time.



\### Scientists Cannot Predict Asteroid Orbits



Incorrect.



Well-observed asteroid trajectories can be calculated with extraordinary precision.



Uncertainty remains larger for newly discovered objects with short observation arcs.



\### Nuclear Weapons Are the Only Asteroid Defense



Incorrect.



Possible strategies include:



\- Kinetic impact

\- Gravity tractor

\- Other slow-push methods

\- Nuclear options for particular extreme scenarios



\### Scientists Would Need to Destroy a Dangerous Asteroid



Usually not.



With sufficient warning, changing its velocity slightly can cause it to miss Earth.



\### DART Destroyed an Asteroid



Incorrect.



DART intentionally struck Dimorphos and altered its orbit around Didymos.



The purpose was controlled deflection testing, not destruction.



\### Apophis Will Hit Earth in 2029



Incorrect.



Its April 13, 2029 approach will be extremely close but safe.



\### Bennu Is Expected to Hit Earth



Incorrect.



Bennu has a very small calculated possibility of impact in future centuries, but an impact is not expected.



\### Asteroids Only Come From the Asteroid Belt



The main belt is the dominant source of many NEAs, but Solar System small-body dynamics are more complex.



\### We Have Discovered Every Dangerous Asteroid



Incorrect.



Large NEO discovery is relatively complete compared with smaller objects, but many small NEOs remain undiscovered.



\## Important Terminology



\### NEO



Near-Earth Object.



An asteroid or comet whose orbit approaches Earth's orbital region.



\### NEA



Near-Earth Asteroid.



An asteroid classified as a Near-Earth Object.



\### NEC



Near-Earth Comet.



A comet with a near-Earth orbit.



\### PHA



Potentially Hazardous Asteroid.



An NEA meeting particular size and orbital-approach criteria.



\### MOID



Minimum Orbit Intersection Distance.



A measure of the minimum separation between two orbital paths.



\### Perihelion



The point in an orbit closest to the Sun.



\### Aphelion



The point in an orbit farthest from the Sun.



\### Semimajor Axis



A measure of the overall size of an elliptical orbit.



\### Eccentricity



A measure of orbital elongation.



\### Inclination



The tilt of an orbit relative to a reference plane.



\### Yarkovsky Effect



Gradual orbital change caused by anisotropic thermal radiation from a rotating body.



\### YORP Effect



Change in asteroid rotation caused by uneven absorption and re-emission of solar radiation.



\### Gravitational Keyhole



A small region during a close encounter that could place an asteroid onto a future resonant impact trajectory.



\### Kinetic Impactor



A spacecraft deliberately collided with an asteroid to change its velocity.



\### Rubble Pile



An asteroid composed of gravitationally bound fragments rather than one solid monolithic rock.



\### Planetary Defense



Detection, tracking, characterization and mitigation of potentially hazardous natural objects.



\## Open Scientific Questions



Scientists continue investigating:



\- How many small NEOs remain undiscovered?

\- What is the precise size distribution of the NEO population?

\- How frequently do Tunguska-scale impacts occur?

\- How accurately can small-object airbursts be predicted?

\- How does asteroid internal structure affect deflection?

\- How common are rubble-pile NEOs?

\- How common are binary NEOs?

\- How efficiently does the Yarkovsky effect transport asteroids?

\- Which main-belt regions supply particular NEO populations?

\- How rapidly do NEO orbits evolve?

\- How can objects approaching from the Sun's direction be detected earlier?

\- Which deflection techniques work best for different asteroid types?

\- How much warning time is needed for different mitigation strategies?

\- How accurately can asteroid mass and strength be estimated remotely?

\- What resources exist on different NEO classes?

\- What do primitive NEOs reveal about Earth's water and organic materials?



\## Why Near-Earth Objects Matter



Near-Earth Objects connect several major areas of planetary science.



They provide information about the formation of the Solar System while simultaneously representing accessible targets for exploration and a natural hazard that can be scientifically monitored.



Their study combines:



\- Astronomy

\- Orbital mechanics

\- Planetary geology

\- Spacecraft engineering

\- Impact physics

\- Geochemistry

\- Planetary defense



Unlike many natural disasters, a dangerous asteroid impact could potentially be predicted years or decades in advance.



With sufficient warning, technology may be able to alter the object's orbit.



This makes NEO detection one of the most practical applications of planetary science.



\## Summary



Near-Earth Objects are asteroids and comets whose orbits bring them into Earth's orbital neighborhood.



Most known NEOs are asteroids, and most pose no significant danger to Earth.



Near-Earth Asteroids are commonly divided into Atira, Aten, Apollo and Amor orbital groups.



Many originate in the main asteroid belt and are transported toward near-Earth space through gravitational resonances, collisions and thermal effects such as the Yarkovsky effect.



Astronomers discover and track NEOs using optical, infrared and other observations.



Repeated measurements allow increasingly accurate orbital predictions.



Potentially Hazardous Asteroids are a subset of Near-Earth Asteroids whose sizes and orbital approaches make them particularly important to monitor.



Planetary-defense programs search for hazardous objects and study possible mitigation strategies.



NASA's DART mission demonstrated that deliberately striking an asteroid can measurably change its motion.



Near-Earth Asteroids such as Bennu, Ryugu, Itokawa, Eros and Apophis have also become important scientific targets.



Samples returned from asteroids provide direct information about primitive Solar System material, water-bearing minerals and organic chemistry.



Near-Earth Objects are therefore important not only because of impact risk but also because they preserve information about planetary formation, provide accessible exploration targets and offer opportunities to develop technologies capable of protecting Earth.
