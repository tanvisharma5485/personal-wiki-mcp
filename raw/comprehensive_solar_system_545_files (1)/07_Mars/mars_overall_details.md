# Mars

## Overview

Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun and the second-smallest planet in the Solar System after Mercury.

It is a terrestrial or rocky planet with a solid surface, a thin atmosphere, polar ice caps, volcanoes, canyons, impact craters, dunes, dust storms, and extensive evidence of ancient liquid water.

Mars is commonly called the Red Planet because iron-bearing minerals in its rocks and soil have oxidized, producing iron oxides similar to rust.

Billions of years ago, Mars was very different from today. Geological evidence shows that ancient Mars contained rivers, lakes, deltas, groundwater systems, and possibly large bodies of surface water.

Today, Mars is cold, dry, dusty, and exposed to significant radiation.

Most known Martian water is currently stored as ice or trapped inside minerals.

Mars is one of the most important worlds in astrobiology because scientists are investigating whether ancient Mars once contained environments suitable for microbial life.

## Why Mars Is Called the Red Planet

Mars appears red because its surface contains abundant iron oxides.

Iron-bearing minerals in Martian rocks reacted with oxygen-bearing substances over geological time.

This produced oxidized iron compounds similar to rust.

Fine iron-rich dust was spread across much of the planet by winds and dust storms.

Mars is therefore not red because it is hot or covered by lava.

Its color primarily comes from oxidized iron minerals.

## Distance From the Sun

Mars orbits the Sun at an average distance of about 228 million kilometers.

This is approximately 1.52 astronomical units.

One astronomical unit is approximately the average distance between Earth and the Sun.

Mars is therefore about one and a half times farther from the Sun than Earth.

## Distance Between Earth and Mars

The distance between Earth and Mars changes continuously because both planets orbit the Sun.

At favorable close approaches, Mars can be roughly 55 to 60 million kilometers from Earth.

At large separations, Earth and Mars can be more than 400 million kilometers apart.

The changing distance affects:

Spacecraft travel time

Communication delay

Mission launch opportunities

Mars's brightness in Earth's sky

## Opposition

Mars is especially favorable for observation near opposition.

Opposition occurs when Earth is positioned approximately between Mars and the Sun.

During opposition, Mars generally appears:

Brighter

Larger through telescopes

Visible for much of the night

Some oppositions bring Mars closer to Earth than others because Mars has an elliptical orbit.

## Sunlight Travel Time

Sunlight takes roughly 12 to 13 minutes to reach Mars when Mars is near its average distance from the Sun.

The exact time changes throughout Mars's orbit.

## Sunlight on Mars

Mars receives less sunlight than Earth.

At its average distance, Mars receives roughly 43% of the solar energy per unit area that Earth receives.

This lower solar energy contributes to Mars's generally cold climate.

However, atmospheric thickness, surface properties, dust, seasons, and orbital position also influence temperature.

## Size of Mars

Mars has a diameter of approximately 6,792 kilometers.

Earth's diameter is approximately 12,742 kilometers.

Mars is therefore a little more than half as wide as Earth.

## Mars Compared With Earth in Size

Mars's diameter is about 53% of Earth's diameter.

Its surface area is approximately 28% of Earth's total surface area.

Interestingly, the total surface area of Mars is roughly comparable to the total land area of Earth because most of Earth is covered by oceans.

## Mass

Mars has a mass of approximately:

6.42 × 10²³ kilograms

This is around 10.7% of Earth's mass.

Mars is therefore far less massive than Earth.

## Density

Mars has a mean density of approximately 3.93 g/cm³.

Earth's average density is approximately 5.51 g/cm³.

Mars is less dense because it contains a smaller proportion of dense metallic material and has a different internal structure.

## Gravity

Mars's surface gravity is approximately:

3.71 m/s²

This is about:

38% of Earth's gravity.

A person weighing 100 kg in terms of Earth-based scale force would experience roughly 38% of that weight on Mars, although their mass would remain unchanged.

## Effects of Low Gravity

Mars's lower gravity affects:

Human movement

Atmospheric retention

Dust transport

Mountain height

Spacecraft landing

Long-term human physiology

Potential future colonization

The long-term effects of Martian gravity on human health are still unknown because no person has lived in 0.38 g for extended periods.

## Escape Velocity

Mars's escape velocity is approximately:

5.0 km/s

Earth's escape velocity is approximately:

11.2 km/s

Mars's weaker gravity makes it easier for gases to escape into space over geological time.

This contributed to the loss of much of Mars's early atmosphere.

## Shape

Mars is approximately spherical but slightly flattened at its poles because of rotation.

Its equatorial radius is slightly larger than its polar radius.

## Rotation

Mars rotates once approximately every 24 hours and 37 minutes relative to distant stars.

A solar day on Mars is approximately:

24 hours 39 minutes 35 seconds

This solar day is called a sol.

## Sol

The term sol is used for a Martian solar day.

One sol is slightly longer than one Earth day.

Mars missions commonly count their operations using sols.

For example:

Sol 1

Sol 100

Sol 1000

This allows mission teams to track local Martian days.

## Mars Day Compared With Earth

Earth day:

About 24 hours

Mars sol:

About 24 hours 39 minutes

The similarity between Earth and Mars day lengths is useful when considering future human exploration.

## Orbit

Mars takes approximately:

687 Earth days

to complete one orbit around the Sun.

This equals approximately:

1.88 Earth years.

## Martian Year

One Martian year lasts nearly twice as long as an Earth year.

Because Mars experiences seasons, each season also lasts significantly longer than comparable Earth seasons.

## Orbital Eccentricity

Mars has a more elliptical orbit than Earth.

Its orbital eccentricity is approximately:

0.093

Earth's orbital eccentricity is approximately:

0.017

Mars therefore experiences a greater variation in distance from the Sun during its orbit.

This affects seasonal temperatures.

## Perihelion

Perihelion is the point where Mars is closest to the Sun.

Mars receives more solar energy near perihelion.

Currently, southern summer occurs relatively near perihelion.

This contributes to stronger seasonal differences in the southern hemisphere.

## Aphelion

Aphelion is the point where Mars is farthest from the Sun.

Mars receives less solar energy near aphelion.

## Axial Tilt

Mars has an axial tilt of approximately:

25.2 degrees

Earth's axial tilt is approximately:

23.4 degrees

Because their axial tilts are similar, Mars experiences recognizable seasons similar in principle to Earth's.

## Seasons

Mars experiences:

Spring

Summer

Autumn

Winter

However, Martian seasons last longer because the planet takes 687 Earth days to orbit the Sun.

The seasons are also unequal in length because Mars's orbit is more elliptical than Earth's.

## Northern and Southern Seasons

Mars's southern hemisphere experiences more extreme seasons than the northern hemisphere.

Southern summer occurs when Mars is relatively close to the Sun.

Therefore, southern summers are:

Shorter

Warmer

More intense

Southern winters are:

Longer

Colder

Northern seasons are generally less extreme.

## Formation of Mars

Mars formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago from material in the protoplanetary disk surrounding the young Sun.

Dust grains collided and accumulated into larger bodies.

These bodies eventually formed planetary embryos.

Mars may represent a planetary embryo whose growth stopped relatively early compared with Earth and Venus.

## Why Mars Is Smaller Than Earth

One major question in planetary science is why Mars contains much less mass than Earth or Venus.

Possible explanations involve the distribution of material in the early Solar System and gravitational effects from Jupiter.

The Grand Tack model suggests Jupiter's early migration may have limited the amount of material available for Mars to accumulate.

However, the exact formation history remains under investigation.

## Age of Mars

Mars formed roughly 4.5 billion years ago.

Much of its ancient crust is billions of years old.

Some regions preserve geological records from the earliest history of the Solar System.

## Geological History

Mars's geological history is commonly divided into broad periods.

### Noachian Period

The Noachian represents ancient Mars.

It was characterized by:

Heavy impact cratering

Valley networks

Surface water activity

Clay mineral formation

Possible lakes

More active erosion

Potentially thicker atmosphere

### Hesperian Period

The Hesperian involved:

Large volcanic activity

Formation of extensive lava plains

Major outflow channels

Increasingly dry conditions

Sulfate mineral formation

### Amazonian Period

The Amazonian extends toward the present day.

It is characterized by:

Ice-related processes

Wind erosion

## Internal Structure

Mars is differentiated into:

Crust

Mantle

Core

Its internal structure formed when heavier materials sank and lighter materials rose early in its history.

## Crust

Mars has a solid rocky crust.

The crust varies significantly in thickness.

The southern highlands generally contain ancient thick crust.

The northern lowlands contain younger and generally lower-elevation terrain.

## Mantle

Below the crust lies a silicate mantle.

The mantle played an important role in:

Volcanism

Heat transport

Tectonic activity

Planetary evolution

Mars is smaller than Earth, so it cooled faster.

This contributed to the decline of large-scale internal activity.

## Core

Mars has a metallic core consisting primarily of iron mixed with substantial lighter elements such as sulfur.

Data from NASA's InSight mission greatly improved estimates of the core's size and properties.

Mars's core is at least partially liquid.

## InSight and Mars's Interior

NASA's InSight lander measured seismic waves from marsquakes and impacts.

These measurements provided the first detailed seismic investigation of another planet's interior.

InSight helped scientists estimate:

Crust thickness

Mantle structure

Core size

Core properties

Marsquake activity

## Marsquakes

Mars experiences earthquakes called marsquakes.

These can be caused by:

Tectonic stresses

Cooling and contraction

Fault movement

Meteorite impacts

Mars is less tectonically active than Earth but is not completely geologically dead.

## No Plate Tectonics Like Earth

Mars does not currently have Earth-style global plate tectonics.

Earth's lithosphere is divided into moving plates that continuously interact.

Mars appears to have developed a largely stagnant outer shell.

The absence of active global plate tectonics helped enormous volcanoes remain over persistent magma sources.

## Why Mars Has Giant Volcanoes

Mars's low gravity and lack of plate tectonics allowed volcanoes to grow enormous.

On Earth, moving tectonic plates carry crust over mantle hotspots.

On Mars, crust could remain over the same volcanic source for extremely long periods.

This allowed repeated lava eruptions to build huge shield volcanoes.

## Olympus Mons

Olympus Mons is the largest known volcano in the Solar System.

It rises roughly:

20 to 25 kilometers

above surrounding plains depending on how height is defined.

Its base is approximately:

600 kilometers across.

Olympus Mons is a shield volcano.

It formed through many layers of relatively fluid lava.

## Why Olympus Mons Is So Large

Olympus Mons became enormous because of:

Low Martian gravity

Long-lived volcanism

Lack of moving tectonic plates

Large lava flows

Stable volcanic hotspot activity

Tharsis contains several major volcanoes, including:

## Tharsis Montes

Three huge volcanoes form a roughly aligned chain:

Ascraeus Mons

## Elysium

The Elysium region is another important volcanic province.

## Valles Marineris

Valles Marineris is the largest major canyon system on Mars and one of the largest in the Solar System.

It extends roughly:

4,000 kilometers

across Mars.

In some locations it is:

Hundreds of kilometers wide

Several kilometers deep

It is far larger than Earth's Grand Canyon.

## Formation of Valles Marineris

Valles Marineris was not formed primarily by a single river like Earth's Grand Canyon.

Its origin is strongly associated with crustal stretching and tectonic fracturing related to the Tharsis volcanic region.

Later processes involving:

Landslides

Erosion

Water

Groundwater

Sedimentation

further modified the canyon system.

## Mars's Hemispheric Dichotomy

One of Mars's largest geological mysteries is the difference between its hemispheres.

The southern hemisphere contains:

Older

Higher

More heavily cratered

terrain.

The northern hemisphere contains:

Lower

Smoother

Generally younger

terrain.

This is called the:

Martian hemispheric dichotomy.

## Northern Lowlands

Mars's northern hemisphere contains vast low-elevation plains.

Some scientists have proposed that parts of the northern lowlands once contained a large ocean.

Evidence remains debated.

## Borealis Basin

The northern lowlands may represent an enormous impact structure called the Borealis basin.

If this interpretation is correct, it would be one of the largest impact basins in the Solar System.

An enormous early impact may have helped produce Mars's hemispheric dichotomy.

However, the full origin remains under investigation.

## Hellas Planitia

Hellas Planitia is a gigantic impact basin in Mars's southern hemisphere.

It is approximately:

2,300 kilometers wide.

Its floor lies several kilometers below surrounding terrain.

Because of its low elevation, atmospheric pressure there is higher than on much of Mars.

## Argyre Planitia

Argyre is another enormous ancient impact basin.

It lies in Mars's southern hemisphere.

Its formation strongly modified regional geology and drainage systems.

## Isidis Planitia

Isidis is another major impact basin.

It lies near the boundary between the southern highlands and northern lowlands.

The Perseverance rover's Jezero Crater landing site is near the western edge of the Isidis basin.

## Craters

Mars has enormous numbers of impact craters.

The southern highlands are particularly heavily cratered.

Crater density allows scientists to estimate relative surface ages.

Older surfaces generally contain more accumulated impact craters.

## Jezero Crater

Jezero Crater is one of the most important scientifically explored locations on Mars.

NASA's Perseverance rover landed there in 2021.

Jezero once contained:

A lake

A river delta

Sedimentary deposits

Water-altered minerals

These features make it an important location for investigating ancient habitability.

## Gale Crater

Gale Crater is the landing site of NASA's Curiosity rover.

It contains a central mountain called:

Mount Sharp

also known as:

Aeolis Mons.

Layers in Mount Sharp preserve a long record of environmental changes.

## Mount Sharp

Mount Sharp rises several kilometers above Gale Crater's floor.

Its sedimentary layers contain information about ancient:

Lakes

Climate

Water-rock interaction

Environmental change

Curiosity has climbed through these layers to reconstruct Mars's history.

## Water on Ancient Mars

Mars contains overwhelming geological evidence that liquid water existed on its surface in the past.

Evidence includes:

## Ancient Rivers

Networks of valleys occur throughout ancient Martian highlands.

Many resemble river drainage systems.

They indicate that liquid water flowed across Mars's surface billions of years ago.

## Ancient Lakes

Multiple Martian craters once contained lakes.

Important examples include:

Jezero Crater

Gale Crater

Other closed and open basin lakes

Lake sediments can preserve information about past environments and possibly ancient biosignatures.

## Jezero Delta

Jezero Crater contains a well-preserved ancient river delta.

A river once carried sediment into the crater lake.

As water slowed, sediment accumulated into a delta.

Deltas are important because fine sediments can preserve organic material and possible biosignatures.

## Ancient Ocean Hypothesis

Some scientists propose that a large ocean once covered portions of Mars's northern lowlands.

Possible evidence includes:

Potential shoreline-like features

Sedimentary deposits

Northern lowland topography

Climate and hydrological models

However, the existence, size, duration, and timing of a northern ocean remain debated.

## Outflow Channels

Mars contains enormous channels created by catastrophic floods.

These differ from smaller valley networks.

Some outflow channels may have formed when huge quantities of groundwater or subsurface ice were suddenly released.

## Groundwater

Ancient Mars likely had active groundwater systems.

Groundwater could have:

Moved through rock

Altered minerals

Fed lakes

Created springs

Produced deposits

Generated some large flood events

## Where Water Is Today

Most modern Martian water exists as:

Polar ice

Subsurface ice

Ground ice

Water bound in minerals

Small amounts of atmospheric vapor

Mars does not currently have stable large bodies of liquid surface water.

## Polar Ice Caps

Mars has permanent polar ice deposits at both poles.

They contain large amounts of:

Water ice

Seasonal carbon dioxide ice

The polar regions expand and contract seasonally.

## North Polar Cap

The north polar residual cap consists primarily of water ice.

During northern winter, carbon dioxide frost can temporarily cover a much larger region.

## South Polar Cap

The south polar region also contains water ice.

A persistent carbon dioxide ice deposit exists in portions of the southern residual cap.

## Polar Layered Deposits

Mars's polar regions contain layered deposits of:

Ice

Dust

Sediment

These layers preserve records of past climate variations.

Scientists study them as a type of Martian climate archive.

## Subsurface Ice

Large quantities of water ice exist beneath the Martian surface, especially at middle and high latitudes.

Some ice deposits occur only centimeters or meters below the surface.

Impact craters occasionally expose bright subsurface ice.

## Mid-Latitude Ice

Radar and imaging evidence suggests large buried ice deposits occur at mid-latitudes.

Some are associated with glacier-like landforms.

These deposits could be important resources for future human missions.

## Glaciers

Mars contains landforms interpreted as:

Buried glaciers

Debris-covered glaciers

Lobate debris aprons

Lineated valley fill

These indicate extensive ice movement in relatively recent geological history.

## Recurring Slope Lineae

Dark seasonal streaks called recurring slope lineae were once widely discussed as possible evidence of flowing salty liquid water.

Later studies showed that many may be better explained by dry granular flows or other processes.

Their exact mechanisms may vary by location.

A reliable wiki should not state that recurring slope lineae are confirmed liquid-water flows.

## Brines

Extremely salty liquid solutions called brines could theoretically remain liquid at lower temperatures than pure water.

Scientists investigate whether transient brines can occasionally form under specific Martian conditions.

However, stable surface liquid water is generally difficult because of low atmospheric pressure and low temperatures.

## Triple Point of Water and Mars

Much of Mars's surface pressure lies near or below conditions where liquid water is difficult to maintain.

Liquid water exposed at the surface can:

Freeze

Boil

Evaporate

Sublimate indirectly through ice

depending on local conditions.

## Atmosphere

Mars has a very thin atmosphere.

Its composition is approximately:

95% carbon dioxide

About 2.6% molecular nitrogen

About 1.9% argon

with traces of:

Oxygen

Carbon monoxide

Water vapor

Methane

Other gases

Exact percentages vary slightly with measurement and season.

## Atmospheric Pressure

Mars's average surface pressure is roughly:

6 to 7 millibars

Earth's sea-level pressure is approximately:

1,013 millibars.

Mars's atmosphere therefore has less than 1% of Earth's sea-level atmospheric pressure.

Pressure varies strongly with elevation and season.

## Why Mars Has a Thin Atmosphere

Mars lost much of its early atmosphere over billions of years.

Important contributing factors include:

Low gravity

Loss of global magnetic protection

Solar wind stripping

Ultraviolet radiation

Atmospheric escape

Impact erosion

Atmospheric chemistry

Some gases also became trapped in surface rocks and minerals.

## MAVEN and Atmospheric Loss

NASA's MAVEN spacecraft studies Mars's upper atmosphere and atmospheric escape.

Its observations show that the solar wind and solar radiation continue to remove particles from Mars's atmosphere.

This process was much stronger when the young Sun was more active.

## Global Magnetic Field

Mars does not currently possess a strong global intrinsic magnetic field like Earth.

However, portions of ancient Martian crust are strongly magnetized.

This indicates that early Mars once had an active global dynamo.

## Ancient Magnetic Field

Mars probably generated a global magnetic field early in its history through motion in its molten metallic core.

The dynamo later shut down.

Scientists continue investigating exactly when and why this happened.

## Crustal Magnetism

Ancient Martian rocks preserve remanent magnetization.

Some southern highland regions contain particularly strong magnetic anomalies.

These provide evidence about early Mars's magnetic history.

## Magnetosphere

Mars lacks a strong global magnetosphere.

Instead, solar wind interacts directly with its upper atmosphere and with localized crustal magnetic fields.

Mars also develops an induced magnetosphere due to interaction between the solar wind and ionized atmospheric particles.

## Radiation

Mars's surface receives substantially more ionizing radiation than Earth's surface.

Important sources include:

Galactic cosmic rays

Solar energetic particles

Mars has weaker radiation protection because:

Its atmosphere is thin

It lacks a strong global magnetic field

## Radiation and Humans

Radiation is one of the major challenges for future human Mars missions.

Long-term exposure can increase risks involving:

Cancer

Central nervous system damage

Cardiovascular effects

Radiation sickness during extreme solar events

Potential reproductive effects

Habitats may require significant shielding.

## Possible Radiation Shielding

Possible shielding strategies include:

Regolith covering

Water shielding

Hydrogen-rich materials

Underground habitats

Lava tubes

Storm shelters

## Temperature

Its average surface temperature is roughly:

−63°C

## Temperature Range

Temperatures can fall below:

−120°C

near polar regions during winter.

Under favorable daytime conditions near the equator, surface temperatures can occasionally rise above:

0°C.

However, air temperature remains extremely cold and the atmosphere is very thin.

## Why Mars Is Cold

Mars is cold because:

It is farther from the Sun than Earth

Its atmosphere is thin

It has a weak greenhouse effect compared with Earth

It loses heat rapidly at night

## Greenhouse Effect

Mars's atmosphere is mostly carbon dioxide.

However, because the atmosphere is extremely thin, it produces only a modest greenhouse effect.

This illustrates that greenhouse warming depends not only on gas type but also on atmospheric quantity, pressure, clouds, and other factors.

Important phenomena include:

## Winds

Martian winds can reach high speeds.

However, because the atmosphere is extremely thin, the force exerted by a Martian wind is much weaker than an equally fast wind on Earth.

## Dust

Fine dust covers much of Mars.

Martian dust influences:

Climate

Temperature

Atmospheric circulation

Spacecraft operations

Solar panels

Visibility

Human health risks during future missions

## Dust Storms

Mars experiences local, regional, and sometimes planet-encircling dust storms.

Large dust storms can cover enormous areas for weeks.

Dust absorbs sunlight and alters atmospheric heating.

This can dramatically modify global circulation.

## Global Dust Storms

Some Martian years experience planet-encircling dust events.

These are often informally called global dust storms.

They do not occur every Martian year.

## Opportunity and the 2018 Dust Storm

NASA's Opportunity rover stopped communicating during a major 2018 planet-encircling dust storm.

Dust blocked sunlight from reaching its solar panels.

After months of unsuccessful attempts to restore communication, the mission was declared complete in 2019.

## Dust Devils

Mars has numerous dust devils.

These rotating columns form when surface heating creates rising air.

Dust devils can grow much taller than typical terrestrial dust devils because of Mars's atmospheric structure.

## Dust Devils and Rovers

Dust devils can be both harmful and helpful.

They can deposit dust on spacecraft.

They can also remove dust from solar panels.

Unexpected cleaning events extended the operating lives of some solar-powered Mars rovers.

## Clouds

Mars has clouds.

They can contain:

Water ice

Carbon dioxide ice

Clouds occur at different altitudes and seasons.

Some high-altitude clouds can form shortly after sunset.

## Carbon Dioxide Cycle

During winter, carbon dioxide from the atmosphere freezes onto the polar caps.

During spring and summer, much of this frozen carbon dioxide sublimates back into the atmosphere.

This seasonal exchange causes atmospheric pressure to change significantly throughout the Martian year.

## Methane

Small amounts of methane have been reported in Mars's atmosphere by some observations.

Methane is scientifically interesting because it can potentially be produced by:

Geological processes

Water-rock reactions

Ultraviolet chemistry

Release from stored subsurface reservoirs

Biological processes

However, methane measurements on Mars are difficult and sometimes contradictory.

## Is Martian Methane Evidence of Life?

No.

Even if methane is present, methane alone is not evidence of biology.

Non-biological geological processes can produce methane.

The origin, distribution, and variability of reported Martian methane remain unresolved.

## Southern Highlands

The southern hemisphere contains much of Mars's ancient heavily cratered terrain.

## Northern Plains

Northern Mars is dominated by smoother low-elevation plains.

## Dunes

Mars contains enormous dune fields.

Martian dunes are shaped by wind and may contain:

Basaltic sand

Dust

Dark volcanic minerals

Modern orbital and rover observations have shown that dunes can still migrate.

## Yardangs

Yardangs are streamlined ridges carved by wind erosion.

Mars contains many spectacular examples.

Their shapes reveal prevailing wind directions and erosion history.

## Sedimentary Rocks

Sedimentary rocks occur in many Martian regions.

They can form through deposition involving:

Water

Wind

Volcanic ash

Chemical precipitation

Ancient sedimentary rocks are especially important in the search for past habitable environments.

## Clay Minerals

Clay minerals have been detected in ancient Martian terrains.

Clays generally form through prolonged interactions between rock and liquid water.

Their presence provides strong evidence of ancient aqueous environments.

## Sulfates

Sulfate minerals are widespread in some younger ancient terrains.

They often indicate water interacting with acidic or evaporative environments.

The transition from clay-rich to sulfate-rich deposits may record major changes in Mars's climate.

## Hematite

Hematite is an iron oxide mineral.

NASA's Opportunity rover landed in Meridiani Planum partly because orbital observations detected hematite.

Opportunity discovered small hematite-rich spherical objects nicknamed:

Blueberries

These provided evidence of past water-related processes.

## Organic Molecules

Curiosity has detected organic molecules in ancient Martian rocks.

Organic molecules contain carbon and are important ingredients for life.

However, organic molecules can form through non-biological processes.

Therefore:

Organic molecules do not automatically indicate life.

## Habitability

Habitability means whether an environment could support life.

Ancient Mars had environments that appear to have been potentially habitable.

Evidence includes:

Liquid water

Organic compounds

Chemical energy sources

Essential elements

Long-lived lake environments

## Gale Crater Habitability

Curiosity found that ancient Gale Crater contained a lake environment with conditions that could have supported microbial life.

The sediments contained important elements such as:

Carbon

Hydrogen

Oxygen

Nitrogen

Sulfur

Phosphorus

This demonstrates past habitability but not past life.

## Jezero Crater Habitability

Jezero Crater once contained a lake and river delta.

This environment could have:

Concentrated organic matter

Preserved sediments

Supported habitable conditions

Perseverance is collecting samples from these ancient environments.

## Has Life Been Found on Mars?

No confirmed evidence of past or present Martian life has been discovered.

Scientists have found:

Ancient habitable environments

Organic molecules

Water-related minerals

Possible methane signals

But none of these individually or collectively constitute confirmed evidence of life.

## Search for Biosignatures

A biosignature is a feature that could provide evidence of past or present life.

Potential Martian biosignatures could include:

Specific organic molecules

Distinctive isotopic patterns

Microscopic structures

Mineral patterns

Chemical disequilibrium

However, non-biological processes can mimic many possible biosignatures.

Multiple independent lines of evidence would be necessary for strong confirmation.

## Mars Meteorites

Rocks from Mars have reached Earth as meteorites.

Large impacts on Mars can eject rocks into space.

Some eventually intersect Earth's orbit and fall to the ground.

Scientists identify Martian meteorites using:

Chemical composition

Mineralogy

Trapped atmospheric gases

## ALH84001

ALH84001 is a famous Martian meteorite found in Antarctica.

In 1996, scientists reported microscopic features and chemical evidence that they suggested might potentially indicate ancient life.

The interpretation became highly controversial.

Most features can potentially be explained through non-biological processes.

ALH84001 therefore does not represent confirmed evidence of Martian life.

## Phobos

Phobos is the larger and inner moon of Mars.

It is irregularly shaped.

Phobos orbits extremely close to Mars.

Its orbital period is approximately:

7 hours 39 minutes.

Because Phobos orbits Mars faster than Mars rotates, it appears to rise in the west and set in the east when viewed from the Martian surface.

## Phobos Size

Phobos is approximately:

27 × 22 × 18 km

in overall dimensions.

It is far too small for gravity to make it spherical.

## Stickney Crater

The largest prominent crater on Phobos is:

Stickney

The impact that created Stickney was enormous relative to Phobos's size.

## Phobos's Future

Phobos is gradually spiraling inward toward Mars.

Tidal interactions are lowering its orbit.

Eventually, tens of millions of years from now, Phobos may:

Break apart due to tidal forces

or

Crash into Mars.

If it breaks apart, Mars could temporarily develop a ring system.

## Deimos

Deimos is Mars's smaller and more distant moon.

It is irregularly shaped and much smaller than Earth's Moon.

Deimos orbits Mars more slowly than Phobos.

## Origin of Phobos and Deimos

The origin of Mars's moons remains uncertain.

Traditional hypotheses suggested they could be captured asteroids because of their:

Small sizes

Dark surfaces

Irregular shapes

However, their relatively circular equatorial orbits are difficult to explain through simple capture.

Another hypothesis proposes that they formed from debris produced by a giant impact on Mars.

The precise origin remains under investigation.

## Mars Does Not Have Rings

Mars has no current ring system.

However, models suggest Phobos may eventually be disrupted and potentially produce rings around Mars in the distant future.

## Early Mars Missions

Mars exploration began in the early space age.

Many early Soviet and American attempts failed because interplanetary missions were technically extremely difficult.

## Mariner 4

NASA's Mariner 4 performed the first successful flyby of Mars in:

1965.

It returned the first close-up images of another planet's surface.

The images revealed heavily cratered terrain.

This initially reinforced the view of Mars as a Moon-like dead world.

## Mariner 9

Mariner 9 became the first spacecraft to orbit another planet when it entered Mars orbit in:

1971.

It discovered or revealed:

Olympus Mons

Valles Marineris

Giant volcanoes

Ancient channels

Dust storms

Its observations transformed scientific understanding of Mars.

## Viking Missions

NASA's Viking 1 and Viking 2 missions reached Mars in:

1976.

Each included:

An orbiter

A lander

The Viking landers were the first fully successful long-duration spacecraft to operate on Mars's surface.

## Viking Life-Detection Experiments

Viking carried experiments designed to search for signs of microbial activity in Martian soil.

The results were complex and controversial.

Most scientists concluded that the observations could be explained by unusual soil chemistry rather than biology.

Viking did not provide confirmed evidence of life.

## Mars Pathfinder

Mars Pathfinder landed in:

1997.

It deployed:

Sojourner

the first successful rover to operate on Mars.

Sojourner demonstrated mobile robotic exploration on another planet.

## Sojourner

Sojourner was a small rover that explored rocks near the Pathfinder lander.

Its success paved the way for larger future rovers.

## Mars Global Surveyor

Mars Global Surveyor entered orbit in:

1997

and conducted extensive mapping.

It studied:

Topography

Magnetic fields

Atmosphere

Surface changes

Geology

Its laser altimeter produced major improvements in Mars topographic maps.

## Mars Odyssey

NASA's 2001 Mars Odyssey orbiter detected large quantities of hydrogen near the Martian surface.

Much of this hydrogen was interpreted as evidence of water ice.

Mars Odyssey has also served as a communications relay for surface missions.

## Mars Express

The European Space Agency's Mars Express entered Mars orbit in:

2003.

It has studied:

## Beagle 2

Britain's Beagle 2 lander traveled to Mars with Mars Express.

## Spirit

NASA's Spirit rover landed in:

2004.

Spirit explored Gusev Crater.

It discovered evidence that some Martian environments had been altered by liquid water.

Spirit operated far beyond its planned mission duration.

## Opportunity

NASA's Opportunity rover also landed in:

2004.

Opportunity found extensive evidence of ancient water, including:

## Spacecraft Landing Challenges

Landing on Mars is extremely difficult.

## Seven Minutes of Terror

NASA sometimes uses the phrase:

Seven Minutes of Terror

to describe the intense entry, descent, and landing sequence for some Mars missions.

Because communication delay prevents real-time control, spacecraft must perform the landing autonomously.

## Communication Delay

Communication between Earth and Mars is not instantaneous.

Depending on planetary positions, one-way radio travel time is roughly:

3 to 22 minutes.

A command sent from Earth could therefore take many minutes to reach Mars.

The response then requires additional minutes to return.

## Solar Conjunction

Approximately every two years, Mars passes near the Sun in Earth's sky.

This is called:

Solar conjunction.

Radio signals passing close to the Sun can become disrupted.

Mission teams therefore reduce or suspend commanding during this period.

## Common Misconceptions

### Mars Is Hot Because It Is Red

Incorrect.

Mars is generally very cold.

Its red color comes mainly from iron oxides.

### Mars Has No Atmosphere

Incorrect.

Mars has an atmosphere, but it is extremely thin compared with Earth's.

### Mars Has Canals Built by Aliens

Incorrect.

Nineteenth-century observations of supposed canals were optical and interpretive errors.

There is no evidence of artificial canals.

### Mars Currently Has Large Rivers

Incorrect.

Mars has extensive ancient river channels, but no confirmed stable large flowing rivers exist on its surface today.

### Scientists Have Already Found Life on Mars

Incorrect.

No confirmed Martian life has been discovered.

### Organic Molecules Mean Life

Incorrect.

Organic compounds can form through non-biological processes.

### Methane Proves Microbes Exist

Incorrect.

Methane can have geological or chemical origins.

### Humans Could Walk Outside With Only an Oxygen Mask

Incorrect.

Mars's atmospheric pressure is far too low.

Humans would require a pressurized spacesuit.

### Mars Has Earth-Like Weather

Only partly.

Mars has winds, clouds, seasons, and storms, but its atmosphere is dramatically thinner and climate far colder.

### Mars Has Stronger Dust Storms Than Earth in Terms of Force

Martian winds can be fast, but the extremely thin atmosphere means their mechanical force is much lower than an equally fast Earth wind.

### Olympus Mons Is an Active Erupting Volcano Right Now

There is no confirmed observation of an ongoing eruption.

Some lava surfaces may be geologically young, so Mars may not be completely volcanically dead.

### Valles Marineris Was Carved Entirely by a River

Incorrect.

Large-scale tectonic stretching played a major role.

### Mars Has Only Carbon Dioxide Ice

Incorrect.

Mars contains enormous quantities of water ice as well as seasonal and permanent carbon dioxide ice.

### Phobos and Deimos Are Definitely Captured Asteroids

Not proven.

Capture is one hypothesis, while formation from impact-generated debris is another major possibility.

### Mars Is Earth's Twin

Mars shares some useful similarities with Earth but differs profoundly in:

Mass

Atmospheric pressure

Climate

Magnetic protection

Surface water

Radiation

Habitability

## Important Terminology

Terrestrial planet: A rocky planet with a solid surface.

Sol: One Martian solar day, approximately 24 hours 39 minutes.

Regolith: Loose fragmented material covering the surface.

Oxidation: Chemical process responsible for much of Mars's reddish iron oxide.

Dichotomy: Large difference between Mars's northern lowlands and southern highlands.

Tharsis: Enormous Martian volcanic province.

Olympus Mons: Largest known volcano in the Solar System.

Valles Marineris: Giant canyon system on Mars.

Outflow channel: Large channel created by catastrophic flooding.

Valley network: Branching ancient drainage system often associated with flowing water.

Delta: Sediment deposit formed where flowing water enters a standing body of water.

Perchlorate: Chlorine-containing salt found in Martian soil.

Biosignature: Evidence potentially indicating biological activity.

Habitability: Ability of an environment to support life.

Planetary protection: Procedures intended to prevent biological contamination between worlds.

ISRU: In-Situ Resource Utilization, meaning use of local planetary resources.

Marsquake: Seismic event occurring on Mars.

MAVEN: Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution mission.

MOXIE: Experiment that demonstrated oxygen production from Martian atmospheric carbon dioxide.

## Retrieval Note

Some Mars information changes with time and should be date-stamped in a RAG system.

Examples include:

Current rover and orbiter mission status

Mars Sample Return architecture

New Perseverance discoveries

New methane measurements

New geological interpretations

Potential evidence for present-day water

Future human mission schedules

Do not permanently store statements such as:

"Mission X is currently active"

without attaching a date.

Instead use:

"As of [date], Mission X is active."

Many fundamental Mars facts are relatively stable, including:

Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun.

Mars is a rocky terrestrial planet.

Mars has two moons, Phobos and Deimos.

Mars has a thin atmosphere dominated by carbon dioxide.

Mars has approximately 38% of Earth's surface gravity.

One Martian solar day is about 24 hours 39 minutes.

Mars takes about 687 Earth days to orbit the Sun.

Olympus Mons is the largest known volcano in the Solar System.

Valles Marineris is an enormous tectonic canyon system.

Mars contains strong geological evidence of ancient liquid water.

No confirmed evidence of Martian life has been found.

For scientific uncertainties, the wiki should preserve uncertainty.

Use:

"Ancient Mars may have possessed a northern ocean."

rather than:

"Ancient Mars definitely had a northern ocean."

Use:

"Some observations have reported methane in Mars's atmosphere."

rather than:

"Mars definitely has biologically produced methane."

Use:

"Phobos and Deimos may be captured objects or may have formed from impact-generated debris."

rather than stating one origin theory as proven.

This makes the Mars page more reliable for semantic search, embeddings, and RAG-based question answering.
