# Impact Melting

**Dataset ID:** 513  
**Category:** 30 Advanced Planetary Science

## Overview

Impact melting occurs when the enormous energy released during a hypervelocity collision heats part of the impactor and target above their melting temperatures.

Planetary impacts commonly occur at velocities of several to tens of kilometers per second. At these speeds, kinetic energy is converted rapidly into:

- Shock compression
- Heating
- Fracturing
- Excavation
- Melting
- Vaporization
- Motion of ejecta

Large impacts can generate enormous volumes of molten rock, producing impact-melt pools, sheets, veins, breccias, and glass.

Impact melting is important because it records the extreme pressure-temperature conditions of collisions and can help scientists determine the age, scale, and geological consequences of ancient impacts.

## Energy of an Impact

The kinetic energy of an impactor is:

**E = 1/2 mv²**

where:

- E = kinetic energy
- m = impactor mass
- v = impact velocity

Because velocity is squared, increasing impact velocity strongly increases the available energy.

A large, fast-moving asteroid can therefore release energy comparable to an enormous explosive event.

## Hypervelocity Impacts

At hypervelocity, ordinary mechanical deformation alone cannot absorb the impact energy.

A powerful shock wave travels through:

- The impactor
- The target

The shock wave rapidly raises:

- Pressure
- Temperature
- Density

Material near the impact point can become partially or completely molten.

## Shock Compression

During the contact-and-compression stage, material is compressed to extremely high pressures.

Compression increases internal energy.

When the shock wave passes and the material decompresses, much of this energy remains as heat.

If the temperature exceeds the melting point of the material, impact melt forms.

## Shock Heating

Shock heating is not uniform throughout an impact structure.

The highest temperatures generally occur close to the impact point.

Farther away:

- Shock pressure decreases
- Heating becomes weaker
- Material may fracture without melting

This produces different zones of impact modification.

## Melt Zone

The central region of a sufficiently energetic impact can contain extensively melted target material.

Around it may be zones containing:

- Partially melted rock
- Strongly shocked rock
- Fractured rock
- Weakly shocked material

These zones preserve information about the pressure and temperature distribution during the collision.

## Impact Melting vs Vaporization

Melting and vaporization are related but distinct processes.

**Melting** converts solid material into liquid.

**Vaporization** converts material into gas or vapor.

The highest-energy regions of a large impact may experience vaporization, while surrounding material may only melt.

## What Controls the Amount of Impact Melt?

The volume of melt depends on several factors:

- Impactor size
- Impact velocity
- Impact angle
- Impactor composition
- Target composition
- Target temperature
- Target porosity
- Surface gravity
- Impact energy

Among these, impact velocity and impactor size are especially important.

## Impactor Size

Larger impactors carry more mass and therefore generally deliver more kinetic energy.

As impactor size increases, impacts can produce:

- Larger craters
- Greater excavation
- More extensive shock metamorphism
- Larger volumes of impact melt

Basin-forming impacts can produce immense melt deposits.

## Impact Velocity

Higher velocity strongly increases impact energy.

Faster collisions generally produce:

- Higher shock pressures
- Higher temperatures
- More melting
- More vaporization

Two impactors of similar size can therefore generate very different melt volumes if their velocities differ substantially.

## Impact Angle

Impact angle affects how energy is transferred into the target.

Most impacts occur obliquely rather than vertically.

At extremely shallow angles, energy deposition and melt distribution may become strongly asymmetric.

For many ordinary impact angles, however, substantial melting can still occur.

## Target Composition

Different materials have different:

- Melting temperatures
- Heat capacities
- Densities
- Shock responses

Therefore, identical impacts into different targets can generate different amounts of melt.

## Target Temperature

A hot target requires less additional heating to reach its melting point than a cold target.

Consequently, the pre-impact thermal state of a planetary crust can influence melt production.

This is especially relevant when interpreting impacts on geologically hot early planets.

## Target Porosity

Porous material responds differently to shock compression.

Pore spaces can collapse during impact, consuming energy and producing additional localized heating.

Porosity therefore influences:

- Shock propagation
- Melt generation
- Crater dimensions
- Ejecta production

This is particularly important for asteroids and porous planetary regolith.

## Impact Melt

Impact melt is material melted directly by impact-generated shock heating.

It may consist primarily of:

- Target rock
- Small amounts of projectile material
- Mixed lithologies

After formation, the liquid can move through the evolving crater before cooling and solidifying.

## Impact-Melt Pools

Melt may collect in depressions within a crater.

These deposits are known as impact-melt pools.

They may occur:

- On crater floors
- Behind terraces
- Within topographic lows
- Among collapsed blocks

## Impact-Melt Sheets

Very large impacts can produce extensive bodies of molten material called **impact-melt sheets**.

These can cover substantial portions of large crater or basin floors.

Thick melt sheets cool slowly and may undergo internal differentiation or crystallization.

## Melt Veins

Molten material can enter fractures and form thin veins.

Impact-melt veins are commonly observed in meteorites and terrestrial impact rocks.

They provide microscopic evidence of high-temperature impact processing.

## Impact Glass

Impact melt that cools rapidly may form glass.

Impact glasses can preserve information about:

- Target composition
- Impact temperature
- Impact age
- Atmospheric interaction

Some impact glasses contain material melted and rapidly quenched during the collision.

## Impact Breccias

Impact breccias are rocks composed of broken fragments produced by an impact.

When these fragments are mixed with impact melt, they may form **melt-bearing breccias**.

Such rocks are common in terrestrial and lunar impact structures.

## Suevite

Suevite is a type of impact breccia containing fragments of rock and impact-generated melt.

It is found at several terrestrial impact structures.

Suevite can preserve evidence of:

- Shock metamorphism
- Melting
- Excavation
- Mixing of different target rocks

## Melt Movement

Impact melt does not necessarily remain where it first forms.

During crater excavation and collapse, molten material may:

- Flow across the crater floor
- Move into fractures
- Pool in depressions
- Mix with breccias
- Become incorporated into ejecta

Its final distribution therefore reflects both melt production and crater dynamics.

## Melt in Ejecta

Some impact melt can be ejected from the crater.

Molten droplets and fragments may cool rapidly while traveling through the atmosphere or through space.

These materials can be deposited far from the crater.

## Tektites

Tektites are natural glass objects associated with some terrestrial impacts.

They form from target material that is melted and ejected during an impact before cooling rapidly.

Tektites can be distributed across large geographic regions known as strewn fields.

## Microtektites

Very small glassy particles produced by impacts are called microtektites.

They can be preserved in sedimentary layers far from the source crater.

These deposits help scientists study ancient impact events.

## Impact Spherules

Large impacts can produce droplets of molten or vaporized material that cool into small spherical particles.

Ancient impact-spherule layers can preserve evidence of major collisions even when the original crater is poorly preserved or unknown.

## Cooling of Impact Melt

Once the impact event ends, molten material begins to cool.

Cooling rate depends on:

- Melt thickness
- Surface environment
- Surrounding rock temperature
- Crater size
- Melt composition

Thin melt deposits may cool quickly.

Thick basin-scale melt sheets can remain hot much longer.

## Crystallization

As an impact-melt sheet cools, minerals crystallize from the liquid.

Large melt bodies can develop complex internal textures.

In some cases, crystals and remaining liquid may separate, producing compositional variations within the melt sheet.

## Differentiation of Melt Sheets

Very large impact-melt bodies may undergo differentiation.

Processes can include:

- Crystal settling
- Density separation
- Chemical evolution of residual melt

This can make an impact-generated melt body resemble some features of igneous intrusions.

However, its origin remains fundamentally different from ordinary magmatism.

## Impact Melt vs Volcanic Rock

Impact melt and volcanic lava can look similar because both originate as molten rock.

Their origins are different.

**Impact melt:** produced rapidly by shock heating during a collision.

**Volcanic lava:** produced by melting within a planetary interior and erupted onto the surface.

Geological context and shock evidence help distinguish them.

## Impact Melt vs Magma

Magma generally forms through internal geological processes such as:

- Decompression melting
- Heating
- Addition of volatiles

Impact melt forms through sudden shock compression and heating.

Large impacts can nevertheless influence later magmatic activity by modifying planetary crust.

## Shocked Minerals

Impact-melt deposits are commonly associated with evidence of shock metamorphism.

Diagnostic features may include:

- Shocked quartz
- Planar deformation features
- High-pressure mineral phases
- Melt textures
- Shatter cones in surrounding rocks

These help confirm an impact origin.

## High-Pressure Minerals

Large impacts can temporarily generate pressures high enough to form mineral phases stable only under extreme conditions.

Their presence provides evidence of powerful shock compression.

Some high-pressure phases can become trapped within impact rocks after pressure decreases.

## Impact Melting on the Moon

The Moon preserves extensive impact-melt deposits because erosion is minimal.

Impact melt occurs in:

- Crater floors
- Basin deposits
- Ejecta
- Returned lunar samples

These materials are important for determining the timing of lunar impacts.

## Lunar Impact-Melt Samples

Apollo astronauts returned impact-melt rocks and breccias.

Radiometric dating of these materials has contributed significantly to understanding lunar impact history.

However, linking a particular sample to a specific impact event can sometimes be difficult because lunar regolith contains material transported and mixed by many impacts.

## Lunar Basins

Large lunar basins generated enormous quantities of impact melt.

Studying basin melt deposits helps scientists investigate:

- Basin formation
- Crustal composition
- Impact chronology
- Early lunar evolution

## Mercury

Mercury contains many large impact craters and basins with evidence of impact melting.

Its ancient surface preserves:

- Melt deposits
- Crater-floor materials
- Basin structures

Spacecraft imaging helps distinguish impact-related materials from later volcanic deposits.

## Mars

Martian impact craters may contain preserved impact-melt deposits.

These are scientifically important because impacts into water- or ice-bearing crust may create temporary hydrothermal environments.

## Impact Melting of Ice-Rich Targets

If an impact occurs in ice-rich material, energy can melt both:

- Rock
- Ice

The resulting system may contain liquid water, molten rock, vapor, and fragmented material.

This can produce complex post-impact environments.

## Impact-Generated Hydrothermal Systems

Large impacts leave behind:

- Hot melt
- Fractured crust
- Residual heat

If water or ice is available, fluid can circulate through the hot fractured rocks.

This creates an impact-generated hydrothermal system.

## Duration of Hydrothermal Activity

The lifetime of a hydrothermal system depends on factors such as:

- Crater size
- Melt volume
- Water availability
- Permeability
- Crustal temperature

Large impact structures can remain thermally active much longer than small craters.

## Astrobiological Importance

Impact-generated hydrothermal environments are important in astrobiology.

They can provide:

- Liquid water
- Heat
- Mineral surfaces
- Chemical gradients
- Fracture networks

Such environments may create temporary habitats for microorganisms where other conditions are suitable.

## Impact Melting on Icy Moons

On icy moons, large impacts can locally melt the icy crust.

This may create temporary subsurface or surface liquid reservoirs.

The behavior depends on:

- Ice thickness
- Surface temperature
- Impact energy
- Presence of salts
- Internal heat

## Europa

An impact into Europa's icy shell could:

- Melt surface and subsurface ice
- Fracture the shell
- Expose deeper material
- Create temporary liquid environments

The exact response depends strongly on the thickness and structure of Europa's ice shell.

## Ganymede and Callisto

Large craters and basins on Ganymede and Callisto preserve evidence of impacts into ice-rich crust.

Their morphology can reveal how ice responds to:

- Shock heating
- Melting
- Collapse
- Long-term relaxation

## Asteroid Impacts

Impact melting also occurs on asteroids.

Meteorites preserve evidence such as:

- Melt veins
- Shock-darkened regions
- Breccias
- High-pressure minerals

These features record collisions that occurred during asteroid evolution.

## Shock Darkening

Shock processes can darken some meteorites by redistributing opaque melt phases through fractures.

This can alter spectral properties without requiring the entire rock to melt.

Shock-darkened asteroid material is important when interpreting remote observations.

## Meteorites as Impact Records

Many meteorites preserve evidence of ancient collisions.

Scientists study:

- Shock stages
- Melt veins
- Breccias
- Recrystallization
- High-pressure minerals

to reconstruct the collisional history of their parent bodies.

## Impact Melt and Radiometric Dating

Impact melting can reset some isotopic dating systems.

When the melt cools and crystallizes, isotopic clocks may begin recording time from the impact event.

This allows impact rocks to potentially provide numerical ages for collisions.

## Why Dating Can Be Difficult

Impact structures often contain material with several different histories.

A sample may include:

- Old target-rock fragments
- Newly crystallized impact melt
- Later alteration products

Scientists must determine which component actually records the impact age.

## Impact Melt and Crater Chronology

Radiometric ages of impact melts are extremely valuable because they can calibrate crater-counting chronology.

The Moon provides the strongest example.

If a surface's age is known independently, its crater density can be used to improve chronology models.

## Giant Impact Basins

Basin-forming impacts generate vastly more melt than ordinary craters.

They may create:

- Thick melt sheets
- Extensive melt pools
- Large volumes of breccia
- Regional thermal anomalies

These deposits can influence the basin's later geological evolution.

## Crustal Effects

Large melt-producing impacts can:

- Thin crust
- Fracture crust
- Redistribute material
- Expose deep rocks
- Produce long-lived thermal anomalies

These effects may extend far beyond the immediate melt zone.

## Can Impacts Trigger Volcanism?

Large impacts can substantially disturb planetary crust and mantle.

They may create fractures and alter local pressure conditions.

However, distinguishing volcanism triggered by an impact from unrelated later volcanism is difficult.

Temporal and geological evidence is required.

## Chicxulub Impact Melt

The Chicxulub impact structure on Earth contains extensive evidence of impact melting.

The event generated:

- Melt rocks
- Shocked minerals
- Breccias
- High-temperature deposits

Drilling and geophysical studies of Chicxulub have provided major insights into large-crater formation and peak-ring processes.

## Sudbury Structure

The Sudbury impact structure in Canada contains an enormous melt body associated with an ancient impact.

Its melt sheet underwent extensive crystallization and differentiation.

Sudbury provides an important terrestrial example of how large impact-melt systems evolve.

## Vredefort Structure

The Vredefort impact structure in South Africa is deeply eroded but preserves evidence of extreme shock conditions.

It demonstrates that impact-generated geological signatures can survive even after much of the original crater morphology has disappeared.

## Detecting Impact Melt Remotely

Spacecraft can investigate potential impact-melt deposits using:

- High-resolution imaging
- Spectroscopy
- Thermal observations
- Topography

Melt deposits may display:

- Smooth surfaces
- Flow-like textures
- Ponded morphology
- Distinct compositions

## Challenges in Identification

Impact melt can sometimes resemble:

- Volcanic lava
- Smooth sediment
- Landslide deposits

Scientists therefore combine morphology, geological context, composition, and crater relationships.

## Impact Melt as a Geological Archive

Impact-melt deposits can preserve information about:

- Target composition
- Impact conditions
- Shock pressure
- Thermal history
- Crater age
- Crustal structure

They are therefore among the most valuable materials within impact structures.

## Common Misconceptions

### Every impact produces a large amount of melt

Incorrect.

Small or low-energy impacts may produce little melt. Melt volume increases strongly with impact energy and velocity.

### Impact melt is the melted projectile only

Incorrect.

Most impact melt in large craters is derived from target material, although projectile material may become mixed into it.

### Impact melt and lava are the same thing

Incorrect.

Both are molten rock, but their formation mechanisms are fundamentally different.

### All impact melt stays on the crater floor

Incorrect.

Melt can be ejected, injected into fractures, mixed with breccia, or pooled at different locations.

### Melting is the most extreme possible impact effect

Incorrect.

Still higher shock temperatures can vaporize material.

### Impact melt cools immediately

Incorrect.

Small deposits may cool quickly, but enormous basin-scale melt sheets can retain heat for much longer periods.

## Scientific Importance

Impact melting helps scientists understand:

- Hypervelocity impact physics
- Shock metamorphism
- Crater and basin formation
- Planetary crust composition
- Collisional history
- Impact chronology
- Thermal evolution
- Hydrothermal systems
- Potential impact-generated habitats

## Open Scientific Questions

Important research questions include:

- How much melt is generated during the largest basin-forming impacts?
- How are impact-melt sheets distributed beneath giant basins?
- How strongly does target porosity influence melt production?
- How do rock-and-ice mixtures behave during extreme impacts?
- How long can large impact-generated hydrothermal systems remain active?
- How often do large impacts influence later volcanism?
- How reliably can impact melts be linked to specific ancient crater-forming events?
- How much projectile material survives within impact melt?
- How does impact melting differ on small asteroids, rocky planets, and icy moons?
