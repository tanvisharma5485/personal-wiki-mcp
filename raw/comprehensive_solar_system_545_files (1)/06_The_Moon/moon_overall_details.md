# Moon

## Overview

The Moon is Earth’s only natural satellite and the fifth-largest natural satellite in the Solar System. It is the brightest object in Earth’s night sky after the Sun and has played a major role in the evolution of Earth, its tides, climate stability, calendars, navigation, culture, mythology, and scientific exploration.

The Moon formed more than 4.4 billion years ago, relatively soon after the formation of the Solar System. The leading scientific explanation is the Giant Impact Hypothesis, which proposes that a Mars-sized protoplanet, often called Theia, collided with the young Earth. Material thrown into orbit eventually accumulated to form the Moon.

The Moon does not produce its own visible light. It appears bright because its surface reflects sunlight. Different portions of the illuminated half are visible from Earth during the lunar phases.

The Moon is tidally locked to Earth, meaning its rotational period is approximately equal to its orbital period. Because of this, nearly the same side of the Moon always faces Earth.

---

* Solar System position: Orbiting Earth, while Earth and Moon together orbit the Sun
* Age: Approximately 4.5 billion years
* Mean radius: About 1,737.4 km
* Diameter: About 3,474.8 km
* Circumference: About 10,921 km
* Surface area: About 37.9 million km²
* Volume: About 21.97 billion km³
* Mass: Approximately 7.342 × 10²² kg
* Mean density: About 3.34 g/cm³
* Surface gravity: About 1.62 m/s²
* Gravity compared with Earth: About 16.5% of Earth’s gravity
* Escape velocity: About 2.38 km/s
* Average distance from Earth: About 384,400 km
* Closest orbital distance from Earth: Roughly 363,000 km
* Farthest orbital distance from Earth: Roughly 405,000 km
* Orbital period relative to stars: About 27.3 Earth days
* Phase cycle relative to the Sun: About 29.5 Earth days
* Rotation period: About 27.3 Earth days
* Axial tilt relative to its orbital plane: About 6.7°
* Orbital inclination relative to Earth’s orbital plane: About 5.1°
* Average orbital speed around Earth: About 1.02 km/s
* Atmospheric pressure: Extremely low; effectively a vacuum
* Number of natural satellites: None confirmed
* Global magnetic field: No strong global magnetic field
* Known liquid surface oceans: None
* Surface water: Mostly frozen water in permanently shadowed polar regions

---

## Position in the Solar System

The Moon is gravitationally bound to Earth. Together, Earth and the Moon form a planet-satellite system that travels around the Sun.

Although commonly described as orbiting Earth, the Moon and Earth actually orbit a shared center of mass known as the barycenter.

Because Earth is far more massive than the Moon, this barycenter lies inside Earth, roughly 4,700 km from Earth’s center.

The Moon is unusually large relative to its parent planet compared with most other moons in the Solar System. Its diameter is more than one-quarter of Earth’s diameter.

---

## Size

The Moon has a diameter of approximately 3,475 km.

It is approximately:

* 27% as wide as Earth.
* About one-quarter of Earth's diameter.
* Much smaller than Earth in volume.
* Roughly 1/81 of Earth's mass.

About 49 Moons could theoretically fit inside Earth by volume.

Despite appearing similar in size to the Sun in Earth’s sky, the Moon is actually about 400 times smaller in diameter than the Sun.

The Sun is also about 400 times farther away from Earth than the Moon, which is why the Moon and Sun can appear almost the same angular size during a total solar eclipse.

---

## Mass

The Moon has a mass of approximately:

7.342 × 10²² kg.

Earth is about 81 times more massive than the Moon.

The Moon contains only about 1.2% of Earth's mass.

Its relatively low mass contributes to:

* Weak gravity.
* Lack of a substantial atmosphere.
* Low escape velocity.
* Difficulty retaining volatile gases over geological time.

---

## Gravity

Surface gravity on the Moon is approximately:

1.62 m/s².

This is about one-sixth of Earth's gravity.

A person who weighs 60 kg on Earth would have the same mass on the Moon but would weigh only about one-sixth as much.

Weak lunar gravity affects:

* Human movement.
* Falling objects.
* Dust behavior.
* Rocket launches.
* Atmospheric retention.
* Long-term human physiology.

Apollo astronauts could jump higher and carry equipment that would feel much heavier on Earth.

---

## Escape Velocity

The Moon's escape velocity is approximately:

2.38 km/s.

Earth's escape velocity is about 11.2 km/s.

Because the Moon's escape velocity is relatively low, spacecraft require much less energy to leave the Moon than to leave Earth.

This is one reason the Moon is considered potentially useful as a future staging location for deeper Solar System exploration.

---

## Distance From Earth

The average distance between Earth and the Moon is approximately:

384,400 km.

However, the Moon's orbit is elliptical.

At its closest point to Earth, called perigee, it can be around 363,000 km away.

At its farthest point, called apogee, it can be around 405,000 km away.

The exact distances vary because the lunar orbit is affected by the gravitational influence of Earth, the Sun, and other bodies.

---

## Lunar Distance as a Unit

The average Earth-Moon distance is sometimes called one lunar distance.

One lunar distance is approximately:

384,400 km.

Astronomers sometimes use lunar distances to describe how close asteroids and other near-Earth objects pass to our planet.

---

## Orbit

The Moon travels around Earth in an elliptical orbit.

One complete orbit relative to distant stars takes approximately:

27.3 Earth days.

This period is called the sidereal month.

However, because Earth is also moving around the Sun, the Moon requires approximately 29.5 days to return to the same phase.

This is called the synodic month.

---

## Sidereal Month

A sidereal month is the amount of time the Moon takes to complete one orbit around Earth relative to distant stars.

Duration:

Approximately 27.32 days.

---

## Synodic Month

A synodic month is the time between identical lunar phases, such as one full Moon to the next.

Duration:

Approximately 29.53 days.

This difference exists because Earth moves around the Sun while the Moon is orbiting Earth.

---

## Orbital Speed

The Moon travels around Earth at an average orbital velocity of approximately:

1.02 km/s.

This equals roughly:

3,680 km/h.

Its speed varies because its orbit is elliptical.

The Moon travels faster near perigee and slower near apogee.

---

## Orbital Inclination

The Moon's orbit is tilted by approximately 5.1° relative to Earth's orbital plane around the Sun.

Because of this inclination, solar and lunar eclipses do not happen every month.

Most months, the Moon passes slightly above or below the alignment needed for an eclipse.

---

## Lunar Nodes

The points where the Moon's orbit crosses Earth's orbital plane are called lunar nodes.

They are known as:

* Ascending node.
* Descending node.

Eclipses can occur when the Sun, Earth, and Moon align near these nodes.

---

## Rotation

The Moon rotates once approximately every 27.3 Earth days.

This is almost exactly the same amount of time it takes the Moon to orbit Earth.

As a result, the Moon is tidally locked.

---

## Tidal Locking

Tidal locking means that the Moon rotates once during each orbit around Earth.

Therefore, nearly the same hemisphere always faces Earth.

This does not mean the Moon does not rotate.

If the Moon did not rotate, observers on Earth would gradually see every side of it during one orbit.

Instead, its synchronized rotation keeps roughly the same face pointed toward Earth.

---

## Near Side

The hemisphere of the Moon that generally faces Earth is called the near side.

The near side contains:

* Many dark lunar maria.
* Numerous impact craters.
* Mountain ranges.
* Highlands.
* Basins.
* Volcanic plains.

Most early lunar observations and Apollo landing missions focused on the near side.

---

## Far Side

The hemisphere that generally faces away from Earth is called the far side.

It is sometimes incorrectly called the "dark side of the Moon."

The far side receives approximately the same amount of sunlight as the near side.

Its surface differs substantially from the near side.

The far side has:

* Much less visible mare basalt.
* More heavily cratered highlands.
* Thicker crust in many regions.
* The enormous South Pole-Aitken Basin.

The Soviet Luna 3 spacecraft photographed the far side for the first time in 1959.

---

## Lunar Libration

Although the same general hemisphere faces Earth, observers can see about 59% of the Moon's total surface over time.

This is possible because of an apparent rocking motion called libration.

Libration occurs because of:

* The Moon's elliptical orbit.
* Its axial tilt.
* Changes in the observer's position on Earth.

---

## Phases of the Moon

Lunar phases occur because the Moon orbits Earth while sunlight illuminates half of the Moon at all times.

The phases depend on how much of the illuminated hemisphere is visible from Earth.

The principal phases are:

* New Moon.
* Waxing Crescent.
* First Quarter.
* Waxing Gibbous.
* Full Moon.
* Waning Gibbous.
* Third Quarter or Last Quarter.
* Waning Crescent.

The entire phase cycle lasts approximately 29.5 days.

---

## New Moon

During a new Moon, the Moon is located approximately between Earth and the Sun.

The illuminated side primarily faces away from Earth.

Therefore, the Moon is difficult or impossible to see from Earth except during certain circumstances such as a solar eclipse.

---

## Waxing Crescent

After the new Moon, a thin illuminated crescent becomes visible.

"Waxing" means the visible illuminated portion is increasing.

---

## First Quarter

During the first-quarter phase, approximately half of the visible lunar disk is illuminated.

Despite the name, the Moon appears half illuminated because it has completed approximately one-quarter of its phase cycle.

---

## Waxing Gibbous

More than half of the Moon appears illuminated.

The illuminated portion continues increasing until full Moon.

---

## Full Moon

During a full Moon, Earth lies approximately between the Sun and Moon.

The hemisphere facing Earth is almost completely illuminated.

A lunar eclipse can occur during a full Moon if the alignment is sufficiently precise.

---

## Waning Gibbous

After the full Moon, the illuminated visible portion begins decreasing.

"Waning" means becoming smaller.

---

## Third Quarter

Approximately half of the lunar disk appears illuminated again.

The Moon has completed about three-quarters of its phase cycle.

---

## Waning Crescent

Only a thin crescent remains illuminated before the Moon returns to its new Moon phase.

---

## Earthshine

Sometimes the dark portion of a crescent Moon can faintly be seen.

This phenomenon is called Earthshine.

Sunlight reflects from Earth onto the Moon and then back toward Earth.

Earth is much brighter in the lunar sky than the Moon is in Earth's sky.

---

## Supermoon

A supermoon is a popular term used when a full Moon or new Moon occurs relatively close to lunar perigee.

A full supermoon may appear somewhat larger and brighter than an average full Moon.

The difference is real but usually modest to the unaided eye.

"Supermoon" is not a strict classical astronomical classification.

---

## Micromoon

A micromoon occurs when a full Moon or new Moon happens near lunar apogee.

The Moon therefore appears slightly smaller than average.

---

## Blue Moon

A blue Moon usually refers to:

* The second full Moon occurring within one calendar month.

Another traditional definition describes the third full Moon in a season containing four full Moons.

The Moon normally does not appear blue during a blue Moon.

---

## Blood Moon

"Blood Moon" is a popular term for the reddish appearance of the Moon during a total lunar eclipse.

Earth's atmosphere filters and refracts sunlight.

Shorter blue wavelengths are scattered more strongly while red and orange wavelengths pass through the atmosphere and reach the Moon.

---

## Solar Eclipse

A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun.

The Moon blocks part or all of the Sun from the perspective of observers on Earth.

Types include:

* Total solar eclipse.
* Partial solar eclipse.
* Annular solar eclipse.
* Hybrid solar eclipse.

---

## Total Solar Eclipse

During a total solar eclipse, the Moon completely covers the visible disk of the Sun for observers within a narrow region called the path of totality.

The Sun's corona becomes visible.

Total solar eclipses are possible because the Moon and Sun have very similar apparent angular sizes as seen from Earth.

---

## Annular Solar Eclipse

An annular eclipse occurs when the Moon passes in front of the Sun while appearing slightly smaller than the solar disk.

A bright ring of sunlight remains visible around the Moon.

This happens when the Moon is relatively far from Earth.

---

## Lunar Eclipse

A lunar eclipse occurs when Earth passes between the Sun and Moon and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.

Lunar eclipses occur only near the full Moon phase.

Types include:

* Total lunar eclipse.
* Partial lunar eclipse.
* Penumbral lunar eclipse.

---

## Umbra and Penumbra

Earth's shadow has different regions.

The umbra is the darker central part of the shadow.

The penumbra is the lighter outer part.

A total lunar eclipse occurs when the Moon moves fully into Earth's umbra.

---

## Why Eclipses Do Not Occur Every Month

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5.1° relative to Earth's orbital plane.

Therefore, during most new and full Moons, the Moon passes above or below the exact alignment required for an eclipse.

---

## Formation of the Moon

The leading scientific explanation for lunar formation is the Giant Impact Hypothesis.

According to this model, approximately 4.5 billion years ago, the young Earth collided with a large protoplanet.

This hypothetical body is commonly called Theia.

The collision ejected large quantities of rocky material into orbit around Earth.

This material formed a disk and eventually combined to produce the Moon.

---

## Evidence for the Giant Impact Hypothesis

Evidence includes:

* Similar isotopic compositions between Earth and lunar rocks.
* The Moon's relatively small metallic core.
* Depletion of volatile elements in lunar material.
* Computer simulations showing that giant impacts can produce Moon-forming debris disks.
* Similarities between lunar mantle material and Earth's mantle.

Modern models continue to investigate the exact details of the collision and subsequent mixing.

---

## Early Lunar Magma Ocean

The young Moon was probably largely molten.

This global or near-global layer of molten rock is called the lunar magma ocean.

As it cooled, minerals crystallized.

Dense minerals sank deeper into the Moon.

Lighter minerals, particularly plagioclase-rich material, floated upward and formed much of the early lunar crust.

---

## Lunar Differentiation

Like Earth, the Moon became internally differentiated.

It developed:

* Crust.
* Mantle.
* Core.

This happened because materials of different densities separated when the Moon was hot and partially molten.

---

## Internal Structure

The Moon has three major internal layers:

* Crust.
* Mantle.
* Core.

---

## Lunar Crust

The Moon's crust varies greatly in thickness.

The far-side crust is generally thicker than the near-side crust.

Much of the ancient crust consists of anorthosite, a light-colored rock rich in plagioclase feldspar.

This material forms much of the lunar highlands.

---

## Lunar Mantle

Beneath the crust is the mantle.

The lunar mantle is composed primarily of silicate minerals containing elements such as:

* Oxygen.
* Silicon.
* Magnesium.
* Iron.
* Calcium.
* Aluminum.

Parts of the mantle partially melted in the past, producing basaltic lava that reached the surface.

---

## Lunar Core

The Moon has a relatively small core compared with Earth.

It contains significant amounts of iron along with other elements.

Evidence suggests the core includes:

* A solid inner region.
* A liquid or partially molten outer region.
* A partially molten layer around parts of the core-mantle boundary.

The exact dimensions and composition continue to be studied.

---

## Surface

The lunar surface is ancient and heavily cratered.

Major surface features include:

* Impact craters.
* Maria.
* Highlands.
* Mountains.
* Valleys.
* Rilles.
* Basins.
* Regolith.
* Volcanic features.
* Lava plains.

Because the Moon has little atmosphere, liquid water erosion, and active plate tectonics, many ancient geological features remain preserved for billions of years.

---

## Lunar Highlands

The highlands are bright, elevated regions composed mostly of ancient crust.

They are:

* Older than most lunar maria.
* Heavily cratered.
* Rich in anorthositic material.

Large portions of the far side consist of lunar highlands.

---

## Lunar Maria

Dark areas visible from Earth are called lunar maria.

"Maria" is Latin for "seas."

Early astronomers mistakenly believed these areas might contain water.

They are actually enormous plains of solidified basaltic lava.

Major maria include:

* Mare Imbrium.
* Mare Tranquillitatis.
* Mare Serenitatis.
* Mare Crisium.
* Mare Nubium.
* Mare Fecunditatis.
* Mare Nectaris.
* Oceanus Procellarum.

---

## Why Lunar Maria Are Dark

Maria contain basalt rich in iron and other minerals.

Basalt reflects less sunlight than the lighter-colored anorthositic highlands.

Therefore, maria appear dark from Earth.

---

## Mare Tranquillitatis

Mare Tranquillitatis means "Sea of Tranquility."

Apollo 11 landed near this region on July 20, 1969.

Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin became the first humans to walk on the Moon there.

---

## Oceanus Procellarum

Oceanus Procellarum means "Ocean of Storms."

It is the largest major dark volcanic plain visible on the Moon's near side.

---

## Lunar Craters

The Moon has an enormous number of impact craters.

Because there is virtually no atmosphere to burn up incoming meteoroids, objects of many sizes can strike the surface.

Important craters include:

* Tycho.
* Copernicus.
* Kepler.
* Plato.
* Aristarchus.
* Clavius.

---

## Tycho Crater

Tycho is one of the most visually prominent lunar craters.

It has a large system of bright rays extending across the surface.

These rays consist of material ejected during the impact that created the crater.

---

## Copernicus Crater

Copernicus is a prominent young impact crater visible from Earth.

It has:

* Terraced walls.
* Central peaks.
* A prominent ejecta system.
* Bright rays.

---

## Impact Basins

Very large impacts produced enormous depressions called impact basins.

Some later filled with lava and became lunar maria.

Examples include:

* Imbrium Basin.
* Orientale Basin.
* Serenitatis Basin.
* Crisium Basin.
* South Pole-Aitken Basin.

---

## South Pole-Aitken Basin

The South Pole-Aitken Basin is one of the largest and oldest known impact structures in the Solar System.

It is located primarily on the Moon's far side.

It extends roughly 2,500 km across.

Scientists are especially interested in the basin because impacts may have excavated material from deep within the lunar crust or mantle.

---

## Lunar Mountains

The Moon contains mountain ranges created mainly by large impacts rather than plate tectonics.

Important lunar mountain ranges include:

* Montes Apenninus.
* Montes Caucasus.
* Montes Alpes.
* Montes Carpatus.
* Montes Jura.

---

## Mons Huygens

Mons Huygens is among the tallest named lunar mountains.

It rises more than 5 km above the surrounding terrain.

Unlike many mountains on Earth, lunar mountains commonly formed through impact processes rather than continental plate collisions.

---

## Lunar Valleys

Large valleys occur across the lunar surface.

One famous example is Vallis Alpes, or Alpine Valley.

Some valleys were produced by:

* Impact processes.
* Crustal fracturing.
* Volcanic activity.

---

## Lunar Rilles

Rilles are long, narrow depressions or channels on the Moon.

They may be:

* Straight.
* Curved.
* Sinuous.

Some sinuous rilles probably formed through flowing lava or collapsed lava tubes.

---

## Hadley Rille

Hadley Rille is a famous sinuous lunar channel.

Apollo 15 astronauts explored the region near it.

It likely formed through ancient volcanic processes involving flowing lava.

---

## Lava Tubes

Ancient lunar lava flows may have created underground lava tubes.

Some lava tubes could potentially contain large underground cavities.

Future lunar bases may use such structures for protection from:

* Radiation.
* Micrometeorites.
* Extreme temperature changes.

---

## Lunar Pits

Spacecraft have discovered several deep pits on the Moon.

Some may represent openings into collapsed lava tubes.

These structures are of significant interest for future exploration and habitation.

---

## Regolith

The lunar surface is covered by a layer of broken rock, dust, and fragments known as regolith.

Regolith forms primarily from billions of years of:

* Meteorite impacts.
* Micrometeorite bombardment.
* Solar wind exposure.
* Thermal breakdown of rocks.

---

## Lunar Dust

Fine lunar dust is extremely abrasive.

Unlike sand and dust on Earth, lunar particles are not rounded significantly by water or wind erosion.

Many grains have sharp edges.

Apollo astronauts reported that lunar dust:

* Stuck strongly to spacesuits.
* Damaged equipment.
* Scratched surfaces.
* Irritated eyes and respiratory systems.
* Entered spacecraft cabins.

Managing lunar dust is a major engineering challenge for future missions.

---

## Space Weathering

The lunar surface is constantly modified by space weathering.

Processes include:

* Solar wind bombardment.
* Micrometeorite impacts.
* Cosmic radiation.

Over time, these processes darken and alter exposed surface material.

---

## Moonquakes

The Moon experiences seismic activity known as moonquakes.

Apollo seismic instruments detected several types.

These include:

* Deep moonquakes.
* Shallow moonquakes.
* Thermal moonquakes.
* Meteorite impact vibrations.

Some shallow moonquakes can be relatively strong.

---

## Cause of Moonquakes

Moonquakes may result from:

* Earth's tidal gravitational forces.
* Cooling and contraction of the Moon.
* Temperature changes at the surface.
* Meteorite impacts.

---

## Lunar Shrinkage

The Moon has gradually cooled since its formation.

As its interior cooled, the Moon contracted slightly.

This contraction produced thrust faults and cliffs known as lobate scarps.

Some of these faults appear geologically young, indicating that the Moon is still undergoing limited tectonic activity.

---

## Atmosphere

The Moon does not have a dense atmosphere like Earth.

Instead, it possesses an extremely thin layer of atoms and molecules called an exosphere.

The particles are so sparse that they rarely collide with one another.

---

## Lunar Exosphere

Particles detected in the lunar exosphere include small amounts of:

* Helium.
* Neon.
* Argon.
* Hydrogen.
* Sodium.
* Potassium.

Sources include:

* Solar wind.
* Radioactive decay within lunar rocks.
* Micrometeorite impacts.
* Release of gases from lunar soil.

---

## Why the Moon Has Almost No Atmosphere

The Moon has weak gravity and low escape velocity.

Gas particles can therefore escape into space relatively easily.

Solar radiation and solar wind also help remove atmospheric particles.

---

## Sky on the Moon

Because the Moon has no substantial atmosphere, its sky appears black even during the lunar daytime.

Stars may be visible when conditions and viewing geometry allow, although bright sunlit surfaces can make them difficult to see without optical adjustment.

There is no blue sky because there is no dense atmosphere to scatter sunlight like Earth's atmosphere does.

---

## No Weather

The Moon has no conventional weather.

It has:

* No rain.
* No clouds.
* No normal wind.
* No snowstorms.
* No atmospheric storms.

However, its surface is affected by solar wind, radiation, micrometeorites, and extreme thermal cycles.

---

## Temperature

Lunar temperatures vary dramatically.

Near the equator:

Daytime surface temperatures can exceed approximately:

120°C.

At night, temperatures can fall below approximately:

−130°C.

Some permanently shadowed polar craters can reach temperatures below:

−200°C.

These are among the coldest naturally occurring locations in the Solar System.

---

## Lunar Day

A complete solar day on the Moon, from one sunrise to the next, lasts approximately:

29.5 Earth days.

Therefore, most locations experience roughly:

* About 14 Earth days of daylight.
* About 14 Earth days of darkness.

This creates major challenges for solar-powered missions.

---

## Water on the Moon

The Moon was once considered almost completely dry.

Modern spacecraft observations have demonstrated the presence of water and hydroxyl-bearing material.

The most important deposits appear to exist as water ice in permanently shadowed polar regions.

---

## Polar Water Ice

Deep craters near the lunar poles receive little or no direct sunlight.

These permanently shadowed regions can remain extremely cold for billions of years.

Water delivered by:

* Comets.
* Asteroids.
* Solar wind interactions.

may become trapped there as ice.

---

## Importance of Lunar Water

Lunar water could potentially be used for:

* Drinking water.
* Oxygen production.
* Hydrogen production.
* Rocket fuel.
* Agriculture.
* Industrial processes.

This makes polar regions important targets for future lunar exploration.

---

## Lunar South Pole

The lunar south pole has become a major exploration target.

It contains:

* Permanently shadowed craters.
* Potential water-ice deposits.
* Areas receiving relatively long periods of sunlight.
* Geologically ancient terrain.

Countries and space agencies are planning future missions in this region.

---

## Lunar North Pole

The north polar region also contains permanently shadowed areas where ice may exist.

However, the south polar region has generally received more attention for near-term exploration plans.

---

## Magnetic Field

The Moon does not currently possess a strong global magnetic field like Earth.

However, some lunar rocks retain magnetization.

This indicates that the Moon probably had a stronger magnetic field early in its history.

---

## Ancient Lunar Dynamo

Scientists believe motion within the Moon's molten metallic core may once have generated a magnetic field through a dynamo process.

Evidence from lunar samples suggests that this ancient field was significantly stronger than the Moon's present-day magnetism.

---

## Magnetic Anomalies

Certain regions of the lunar crust have stronger localized magnetic fields.

These are called magnetic anomalies.

They may partially shield the surface from solar wind.

Some are associated with unusual bright patterns called lunar swirls.

---

## Lunar Swirls

Lunar swirls are unusual bright and dark patterns on the surface.

One famous example is Reiner Gamma.

Their exact formation remains under investigation.

They appear to be associated with localized magnetic fields that affect how solar wind interacts with the surface.

---

## Radiation

The lunar surface is exposed to intense radiation because it lacks Earth's protective atmosphere and global magnetic field.

Sources include:

* Solar energetic particles.
* Solar wind.
* Galactic cosmic rays.

Radiation exposure is a major concern for long-duration human missions.

---

## Micrometeorites

Small meteoroids constantly strike the Moon.

Because there is almost no atmosphere, even tiny particles can reach the surface at extremely high speeds.

These impacts contribute to:

* Regolith formation.
* Surface erosion.
* Space weathering.
* Equipment hazards.

---

## Tides on Earth

The Moon's gravity is the primary cause of Earth's ocean tides.

Its gravitational pull produces tidal bulges in Earth's oceans.

As Earth rotates through these bulges, many coastal locations experience alternating high and low tides.

---

## Solar Contribution to Tides

The Sun also contributes to Earth's tides.

Although the Sun is much more massive than the Moon, it is also far farther away.

The Moon therefore has a stronger tide-generating effect on Earth.

---

## Spring Tides

Spring tides occur near:

* New Moon.
* Full Moon.

During these phases, the gravitational effects of the Sun and Moon reinforce each other.

This results in larger differences between high and low tides.

---

## Neap Tides

Neap tides occur near:

* First-quarter Moon.
* Third-quarter Moon.

The Sun and Moon pull approximately at right angles relative to Earth.

This produces smaller tidal differences.

---

## Effect on Earth's Rotation

Tidal interactions between Earth and the Moon gradually slow Earth's rotation.

As Earth loses rotational energy, the length of Earth's day slowly increases.

---

## Moon Moving Away From Earth

The Moon is gradually moving farther away from Earth.

Laser measurements show that its average distance increases by approximately:

3.8 cm per year.

This happens because tidal interactions transfer angular momentum from Earth's rotation to the Moon's orbit.

---

## Earth's Day in the Ancient Past

Because Earth's rotation has gradually slowed, days were shorter in the distant past.

Hundreds of millions of years ago, a day on Earth was noticeably shorter than 24 hours.

The Moon was also closer to Earth.

---

## Future of the Earth-Moon System

Over extremely long timescales, tidal evolution will continue changing Earth's rotation and the Moon's orbit.

However, the Sun's evolution will dramatically alter the Solar System long before a perfectly stable Earth-Moon double tidal lock is likely to be reached under current conditions.

---

## Stabilizing Earth's Axial Tilt

The Moon helps stabilize Earth's axial tilt.

Without the Moon, Earth's tilt could potentially vary more dramatically over long geological periods.

Greater variations could produce much stronger long-term climate changes.

Therefore, the Moon may have contributed indirectly to relatively stable conditions for life on Earth.

---

## Effects on Life

The Moon influences Earth through:

* Ocean tides.
* Coastal ecosystems.
* Biological rhythms.
* Nighttime illumination.
* Long-term rotational evolution.

Many organisms have evolved behaviors linked to lunar cycles.

Examples include changes in:

* Reproduction.
* Feeding.
* Migration.
* Spawning.
* Predator-prey activity.

---

## Moonlight

Moonlight is reflected sunlight.

The Moon reflects only a relatively small fraction of incoming sunlight.

Its average reflectivity, or Bond albedo, is low compared with many icy moons.

Despite this, the Moon appears extremely bright because it is close to Earth.

---

## Full Moon Brightness

A full Moon is dramatically brighter than a quarter Moon.

The brightness increase is not simply proportional to the illuminated area because surface scattering effects make the fully illuminated Moon particularly bright.

---

## Lunar Color

To the human eye, the Moon generally appears:

* Gray.
* White.
* Yellowish near the horizon.
* Orange or red under some atmospheric conditions.

Its actual surface contains subtle color variations depending on mineral composition.

---

## Why the Moon Looks Orange Near the Horizon

When the Moon is near Earth's horizon, its light passes through more of Earth's atmosphere.

Shorter blue wavelengths are scattered more strongly.

Longer red and orange wavelengths are transmitted more efficiently.

This can make the Moon appear yellow, orange, or red.

---

## Moon Illusion

The Moon often appears larger when it is near the horizon than when it is high in the sky.

This phenomenon is called the Moon illusion.

Measurements show that the Moon's angular diameter has not suddenly become much larger.

The effect is largely caused by human visual perception.

---

## Apparent Size

The Moon's apparent angular diameter in Earth's sky is approximately half a degree.

Its apparent size varies because its distance from Earth changes during its elliptical orbit.

---

## Surface Age

Some lunar surfaces are more than 4 billion years old.

The Moon preserves ancient geological records that have largely been erased on Earth by:

* Plate tectonics.
* Weathering.
* Erosion.
* Volcanism.

This makes the Moon valuable for studying the early history of the Solar System.

---

## Crater Counting

Scientists estimate the relative ages of lunar surfaces by counting impact craters.

Older surfaces generally contain more craters than younger surfaces.

The Moon provides an important reference for dating surfaces on other rocky bodies.

---

## Late Heavy Bombardment

Lunar rocks and impact basins provide evidence for a period of intense impacts early in Solar System history.

Traditionally, this period has been associated with the term Late Heavy Bombardment.

However, scientists continue to debate whether there was a sudden spike in impacts or a more gradual decline in bombardment.

---

## Lunar Volcanism

The Moon was volcanically active for long periods in its early history.

Large basaltic eruptions filled impact basins and created the lunar maria.

Evidence also exists for volcanic activity occurring later than the main period of mare formation.

---

## Basalt

Lunar basalt formed when molten rock from the Moon's interior erupted onto the surface and cooled.

Basaltic plains are particularly common on the near side.

---

## Pyroclastic Deposits

Some lunar regions contain deposits created by explosive volcanic activity.

These materials are known as pyroclastic deposits.

They may contain volcanic glass beads and relatively high concentrations of useful volatile elements.

---

## Orange Soil

Apollo 17 astronauts discovered orange-colored lunar soil near Shorty Crater.

The material consists largely of tiny volcanic glass beads.

Its discovery provided important evidence of ancient explosive lunar volcanism.

---

## KREEP

KREEP is a geochemical component found in certain lunar rocks.

The name refers to enrichment in:

* K = Potassium.
* REE = Rare-earth elements.
* P = Phosphorus.

KREEP-rich material is associated particularly with parts of the lunar near side.

It represents material left behind during late stages of lunar magma-ocean crystallization.

---

## Procellarum KREEP Terrane

A large geochemical region on the lunar near side is known as the Procellarum KREEP Terrane.

It contains elevated concentrations of heat-producing elements such as:

* Uranium.
* Thorium.
* Potassium.

This may have helped support long-lasting volcanic activity on the near side.

---

## Near-Side and Far-Side Differences

The near side contains far more large maria than the far side.

Possible reasons include differences in:

* Crust thickness.
* Heat-producing elements.
* Impact history.
* Internal thermal evolution.

The exact origin of this asymmetry remains an important scientific question.

## Lunar Laser Ranging

Apollo astronauts and Soviet lunar missions placed retroreflectors on the Moon.

## Common Misconceptions

### The Far Side Is Always Dark

False.

The far side receives sunlight just like the near side.

### The Moon Does Not Rotate

False.

The Moon rotates once during each orbit around Earth.

### Lunar Phases Are Caused by Earth's Shadow

False.

Ordinary lunar phases result from viewing different portions of the Moon's sunlit hemisphere.

Earth's shadow causes lunar eclipses, not ordinary phases.

### The Moon Produces Its Own Light

False.

The Moon reflects sunlight.

### There Is Absolutely No Water on the Moon

False.

Water and hydroxyl have been detected, with water ice particularly important in polar cold traps.

### There Is Absolutely No Atmosphere

Not strictly correct.

The Moon has an extremely thin exosphere, although for most practical purposes its surface is exposed to vacuum.

### Astronaut Footprints Will Last Forever

Not literally.

They may remain for extremely long periods but will eventually be degraded by micrometeorites and space weathering.

## Summary

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite, approximately 3,475 km in diameter and located an average of about 384,400 km from Earth. It formed around 4.5 billion years ago, most likely after a giant impact involving the young Earth.

Its surface contains ancient highlands, volcanic maria, enormous impact basins, mountains, rilles, lava tubes, craters, and a thick layer of regolith. The Moon has almost no atmosphere, weak gravity, extreme temperature variations, no conventional weather, and no strong global magnetic field.

Water ice exists in permanently shadowed regions near the poles, making these locations particularly important for future exploration.

The Moon is tidally locked to Earth and takes approximately 27.3 days to complete one orbit relative to distant stars, while its full phase cycle lasts approximately 29.5 days.

Its gravity drives much of Earth's tidal activity, gradually slows Earth's rotation, causes the Moon to recede from Earth, and helps stabilize Earth's axial orientation.

The Moon has been explored by robotic spacecraft from several countries and remains the only celestial body beyond Earth visited by humans. Twelve Apollo astronauts walked on its surface between 1969 and 1972.

Today, the Moon remains one of the most scientifically important locations in the Solar System and is expected to play a major role in future human space exploration, resource utilization, astronomy, planetary science, and preparation for missions deeper into space.
