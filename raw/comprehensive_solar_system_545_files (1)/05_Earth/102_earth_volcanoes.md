# Earth Volcanoes

**Dataset ID:** 102  
**Category:** 05 Earth

## Overview

[[Earth]] is one of the most volcanically active rocky worlds in the Solar System.

Earth's volcanism is powered primarily by internal heat left from planetary formation, heat generated by radioactive decay, and the movement and cooling of the mantle. Unlike most other known rocky worlds, Earth has an active global system of [[Plate Tectonics]], which strongly controls where and why much volcanism occurs.

Volcanoes are concentrated mainly at:

- divergent plate boundaries
- subduction zones
- continental rifts
- mantle hotspots

Earth also contains enormous submarine volcanic systems along the global mid-ocean ridge network.

Volcanism continually transfers heat, magma, gases, and chemical elements from Earth's interior toward the surface. It plays fundamental roles in crust formation, atmospheric evolution, ocean chemistry, climate, nutrient cycling, hydrothermal activity, and the long-term evolution of the biosphere.

Earth therefore provides the primary reference point for understanding [[Planetary Volcanism]] on other worlds.

## What Is a Volcano?

A volcano is a geological system through which magma, volcanic gases, fragmented material, or combinations of these reach or approach a planetary surface.

A volcanic system can include:

- magma-generation regions
- magma reservoirs
- dikes
- sills
- conduits
- vents
- craters
- calderas
- lava flows
- pyroclastic deposits

The visible volcanic mountain is therefore only one part of a much larger geological system.

## Magma and Lava

Molten or partially molten rock beneath Earth's surface is called magma.

Once magma erupts onto the surface, the molten material is called lava.

Magma commonly contains:

- liquid silicate melt
- crystals
- dissolved gases
- bubbles

Its composition strongly influences eruption behavior.

## Why Earth's Interior Is Hot

Earth's internal heat comes primarily from:

- heat retained from planetary accretion
- energy associated with differentiation and core formation
- radioactive decay of isotopes
- crystallization and cooling of the interior

This heat drives mantle convection and contributes to plate tectonics and magma generation.

## Partial Melting

Most of Earth's mantle is solid rather than completely molten.

Magma forms when portions of rock undergo partial melting.

Three particularly important mechanisms are:

- decompression melting
- flux melting
- heat-transfer melting

These mechanisms operate in different tectonic environments.

## Decompression Melting

Decompression melting occurs when hot mantle material rises toward the surface.

As pressure decreases, mantle rock can begin melting even without a large increase in temperature.

This process is especially important at:

- mid-ocean ridges
- continental rifts
- some mantle hotspots

## Flux Melting

Flux melting occurs when volatile substances such as water are added to hot mantle rock.

Water lowers the temperature at which mantle material begins to melt.

This process is particularly important above subduction zones.

Water and other volatiles released from the descending plate enter the overlying mantle and promote magma generation.

## Heat-Transfer Melting

Hot magma rising into cooler crust can transfer heat to surrounding rocks.

This can partially melt crustal material.

Heat-transfer melting contributes to the chemical diversity of magmas, especially in continental volcanic environments.

## Plate Tectonics and Volcanism

Earth's lithosphere is divided into tectonic plates.

These plates move relative to one another.

Their interactions create many of Earth's major volcanic environments.

Important settings include:

- divergent boundaries
- convergent boundaries
- continental rifts
- intraplate hotspots

Earth's volcanism therefore cannot be understood separately from [[Planetary Tectonics]].

## Divergent Plate Boundaries

At divergent boundaries, tectonic plates move apart.

Hot mantle rises to replace the separating material.

Pressure decreases as the mantle rises, causing decompression melting.

The resulting magma produces new crust.

The most extensive examples occur along mid-ocean ridges.

## Mid-Ocean Ridges

Earth's oceans contain a global network of mid-ocean ridges extending tens of thousands of kilometers.

These ridges represent one of the planet's largest volcanic systems.

Basaltic magma rises along spreading centers and forms new oceanic crust.

A large fraction of Earth's volcanic activity therefore occurs underwater rather than on continents.

## Seafloor Spreading

At mid-ocean ridges, newly formed magma cools and becomes oceanic crust.

As additional magma forms, older crust moves away from the ridge.

This process is called seafloor spreading.

It continually renews Earth's oceanic crust.

## Pillow Lava

When basaltic lava erupts underwater, rapid cooling can produce rounded structures called pillow lavas.

Pillow lavas provide clear geological evidence of submarine eruptions.

Ancient pillow lavas preserved on land can indicate that those rocks originally formed underwater.

## Subduction-Zone Volcanism

At convergent plate boundaries, one tectonic plate may descend beneath another.

This process is called subduction.

As the descending plate moves deeper, minerals release water and other volatile substances.

These volatiles enter the mantle wedge above the slab and promote partial melting.

The resulting magma can rise and feed volcanic arcs.

## Volcanic Arcs

Subduction commonly creates chains of volcanoes called volcanic arcs.

Examples include volcanic regions around the Pacific Ocean.

Depending on tectonic geometry, these may form:

- continental volcanic arcs
- island arcs

Subduction-zone volcanoes can produce both effusive and highly explosive eruptions.

## Pacific Ring of Fire

Many active volcanoes occur around the margins of the Pacific Ocean.

This broad zone is commonly called the Ring of Fire.

Its volcanism is associated mainly with subduction zones and other plate-boundary processes.

It should not be interpreted as one single continuous volcanic structure.

## Continental Rifting

Continental rifts form where Earth's crust is being stretched.

Extension thins the lithosphere and can allow hot mantle material to rise.

Decompression melting may then produce magma.

If rifting continues long enough, a continent can eventually split and a new ocean basin may develop.

## Hotspot Volcanism

Not all volcanoes occur at plate boundaries.

Some volcanic systems form within tectonic plates.

These are commonly associated with mantle hotspots or long-lived regions of mantle upwelling.

Examples include:

- Hawaii
- Yellowstone
- Réunion

The detailed origin and depth of individual hotspots can vary.

## Hawaii

The Hawaiian volcanic chain is a classic example of intraplate volcanism.

As the Pacific Plate moves over a long-lived magma-generating region, volcanoes form sequentially.

Older volcanoes move away from the active region while new ones develop.

This creates an age-progressive volcanic chain.

## Mantle Plumes

Some hotspots are explained by mantle plumes.

A mantle plume is an upwelling of unusually hot material from deep within the mantle.

As plume material rises, decompression can generate magma.

The exact structure and depth of mantle plumes remain active areas of geophysical research.

## Shield Volcanoes

Shield volcanoes are broad volcanoes built mainly from relatively fluid lava flows.

Their gentle slopes develop because low-viscosity lava can travel considerable distances.

Mauna Loa and other Hawaiian volcanoes are major examples.

Shield volcanism is also important when comparing Earth with [[Mars]], [[Venus]], and [[Io]].

## Stratovolcanoes

Stratovolcanoes, also called composite volcanoes, are steep volcanic structures composed of layers of lava, ash, and other volcanic deposits.

They are common in subduction-zone environments.

Examples include:

- Mount Fuji
- Mount St. Helens
- Mount Vesuvius

They can produce highly explosive eruptions.

## Cinder Cones

Cinder cones are relatively small volcanic structures built from fragmented volcanic material deposited around a vent.

They commonly have steep slopes and summit craters.

They may form rapidly compared with large shield volcanoes or stratovolcanoes.

## Lava Domes

Lava domes form when highly viscous lava accumulates near a vent.

Because the lava does not flow easily, it builds steep-sided structures.

Lava domes can become unstable and collapse, potentially producing dangerous pyroclastic flows.

## Calderas

A caldera is a large volcanic depression commonly produced when a magma reservoir is partially emptied and the overlying ground collapses.

Calderas can be much larger than ordinary volcanic craters.

Examples include:

- Yellowstone Caldera
- Toba
- Crater Lake

Caldera-forming eruptions can be among Earth's largest explosive volcanic events.

## Fissure Eruptions

Magma does not always erupt from a central volcanic cone.

It can emerge along elongated fractures called fissures.

Fissure eruptions can produce enormous quantities of lava.

Large-scale fissure eruptions have created some of Earth's major flood-basalt provinces.

## Flood Basalts

Flood basalts form when extremely large volumes of basaltic lava erupt over geologically short intervals.

Repeated flows can cover enormous areas.

Major examples include:

- Deccan Traps
- Siberian Traps
- Columbia River Basalt Group

Flood-basalt volcanism can strongly affect atmosphere, climate, ecosystems, and the carbon cycle.

## Large Igneous Provinces

A [[Large Igneous Province]] is a region containing exceptionally large volumes of igneous rock emplaced over a relatively limited geological interval.

Many are dominated by flood basalts.

Their formation may involve mantle upwelling, lithospheric extension, or other large-scale mantle processes.

## Magma Composition

Magma composition strongly affects volcanic behavior.

Important categories include:

- basaltic
- andesitic
- dacitic
- rhyolitic

These differ in silica content, temperature, viscosity, gas behavior, and eruption style.

## Basaltic Magma

Basaltic magma generally has relatively low silica content and low viscosity.

It can flow relatively easily.

Basaltic eruptions often produce:

- lava flows
- shield volcanoes
- fissure eruptions
- oceanic crust

Basalt is one of the most important volcanic rocks on Earth and other terrestrial worlds.

## Andesitic Magma

Andesitic magma has intermediate silica content and viscosity.

It is common in many subduction-zone volcanic systems.

It can produce both lava flows and explosive eruptions.

## Rhyolitic Magma

Rhyolitic magma is rich in silica and highly viscous.

Its high viscosity can trap volcanic gases.

Pressure can therefore build before eruption.

Some of Earth's largest explosive eruptions involve silica-rich magma.

## Magma Viscosity

Viscosity measures resistance to flow.

Magma viscosity is influenced strongly by:

- silica content
- temperature
- crystal abundance
- dissolved gases

Low-viscosity magma generally flows more easily.

High-viscosity magma can trap gases more effectively and increase explosive potential.

## Effusive Eruptions

Effusive eruptions are dominated by lava flowing onto the surface.

They are commonly associated with relatively low-viscosity magma.

They can produce:

- lava flows
- lava lakes
- shield volcanoes
- lava tubes
- volcanic plains

## Explosive Eruptions

Explosive eruptions occur when expanding gases fragment magma and surrounding rock.

They can produce:

- ash
- pumice
- volcanic bombs
- pyroclastic flows
- eruption columns

Explosivity depends on magma composition, viscosity, gas content, pressure, and interactions with external water.

## Volcanic Gases

Magma contains dissolved volatile compounds.

Important volcanic gases include:

- water vapor
- carbon dioxide
- sulfur dioxide
- hydrogen sulfide
- hydrogen chloride
- other gases

As magma rises and pressure decreases, gases can separate from the melt.

This process is called exsolution.

## Why Volcanic Gases Matter

Volcanic gases influence:

- eruption explosivity
- atmospheric chemistry
- climate
- acid deposition
- ocean chemistry
- long-term carbon cycling

Volcanic outgassing also played an important role in the evolution of Earth's early atmosphere.

## Pyroclastic Flows

Pyroclastic flows are fast-moving mixtures of hot gas, ash, and volcanic fragments.

They can move rapidly down volcanic slopes and across surrounding terrain.

They are among the most dangerous volcanic hazards.

## Volcanic Ash

Volcanic ash consists of small fragments of rock, minerals, and volcanic glass.

Ash can travel hundreds or thousands of kilometers depending on eruption strength and atmospheric conditions.

It can affect:

- aviation
- agriculture
- infrastructure
- water supplies
- respiratory health
- climate

## Lahars

Lahars are fast-moving mixtures of volcanic material and water.

They can form when ash and debris mix with:

- rainfall
- snowmelt
- glacier melt
- crater-lake water

Lahars can travel far from volcanic vents.

## Lava Flows

Lava flows are streams or sheets of molten rock moving across the surface.

Their behavior depends on:

- viscosity
- eruption rate
- temperature
- slope
- cooling rate

Basaltic lava can sometimes travel many kilometers from its vent.

## Pahoehoe and Aa

Basaltic lava can develop different surface textures.

Pahoehoe generally has smooth, ropy surfaces.

Aa commonly has rough, broken, blocky surfaces.

A lava flow can sometimes transition from pahoehoe-like behavior toward aa as it cools and changes dynamically.

## Lava Tubes

A [[Lava Tube]] forms when the surface of a lava flow solidifies while molten lava continues moving beneath it.

If lava later drains away, a hollow tunnel can remain.

Lava tubes are important both on Earth and when interpreting possible volcanic caves on the Moon and Mars.

## Hydrothermal Systems

Magma can heat groundwater.

This produces hydrothermal circulation.

Hydrothermal systems can create:

- hot springs
- geysers
- mineral deposits
- chemically unusual environments

They also play important roles in geochemistry and biology.

## Hydrothermal Vents

At mid-ocean ridges, seawater can circulate through hot crust.

The heated water reacts chemically with rocks and later emerges through hydrothermal vents.

These systems support ecosystems that do not depend directly on sunlight.

Instead, microorganisms can obtain energy through chemosynthesis.

## Volcanism and Life

Volcanism has both destructive and constructive relationships with life.

Eruptions can destroy ecosystems locally.

Over longer timescales, volcanic processes can:

- create new land
- supply nutrients
- support hydrothermal systems
- influence atmospheric composition
- participate in long-term carbon cycling

Volcanism has therefore been deeply connected with Earth's biosphere throughout geological history.

## Volcanism and the Early Atmosphere

Early Earth experienced extensive volcanic outgassing.

Volcanoes released gases from the interior into the atmosphere.

Important gases included:

- water vapor
- carbon dioxide
- nitrogen-bearing compounds
- sulfur gases

Volcanism was one contributor to the development and evolution of Earth's secondary atmosphere.

## Volcanism and the Carbon Cycle

Carbon moves among Earth's:

- atmosphere
- oceans
- crust
- mantle
- biosphere

Volcanoes return carbon dioxide from the solid Earth to the atmosphere and oceans.

Plate tectonics also carries carbon-bearing material into the mantle through subduction.

This creates part of Earth's long-term carbon cycle.

## Silicate Weathering Feedback

Atmospheric carbon dioxide dissolves in water and participates in chemical weathering of silicate rocks.

Carbon can eventually become stored in sediments and carbonate minerals.

Plate tectonics can carry some of this material into Earth's interior.

Volcanism later returns carbon dioxide.

This long-term cycle contributes to climate regulation over geological timescales.

## Volcanism and Climate

Volcanic eruptions can affect climate in different ways depending on scale and timescale.

Individual large explosive eruptions can inject sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere.

The sulfur dioxide forms sulfate aerosols that reflect sunlight.

This can temporarily cool Earth's surface.

## Short-Term Volcanic Cooling

Large explosive eruptions can cause temporary global cooling lasting months to several years.

The magnitude depends on:

- sulfur emissions
- eruption latitude
- injection altitude
- atmospheric circulation
- aerosol lifetime

Ash itself usually falls out more rapidly than stratospheric sulfate aerosols and is not the primary driver of long-lasting global volcanic cooling.

## Long-Term Volcanic Climate Effects

Over much longer timescales, volcanic carbon dioxide can contribute to greenhouse warming.

Large igneous provinces can release substantial quantities of carbon dioxide and sulfur gases.

Their environmental effects can involve both warming and cooling processes operating over different timescales.

## Volcanism and Mass Extinctions

Several major mass-extinction intervals are temporally associated with enormous volcanic events.

Examples include the Siberian Traps near the Permian-Triassic extinction and the Deccan Traps near the end-Cretaceous extinction.

Possible environmental effects include:

- greenhouse warming
- ocean acidification
- oxygen depletion
- sulfur-driven cooling
- acid rain
- ecosystem disruption

The role of volcanism can interact with other processes and must be evaluated from geological evidence.

## Volcanoes and Earthquakes

Volcanic activity can generate earthquakes as magma moves through the crust.

Earthquakes may result from:

- rock fracturing
- magma movement
- pressure changes
- fault movement

Monitoring seismicity is therefore one method used to assess volcanic unrest.

## Volcano Monitoring

Modern volcano monitoring combines multiple techniques.

These include:

- seismometers
- GPS
- satellite radar
- gas measurements
- thermal imaging
- visual observations
- gravity measurements
- satellite spectroscopy

No single measurement is sufficient for predicting every eruption.

Scientists combine several forms of evidence.

## Ground Deformation

Moving magma can cause the surface above a volcanic system to rise, sink, or deform.

GPS and satellite radar interferometry can measure very small changes in surface position.

These observations can reveal changes in subsurface magma systems.

## Gas Monitoring

Changes in volcanic gases can provide information about magma movement.

Scientists monitor gases such as:

- sulfur dioxide
- carbon dioxide
- hydrogen sulfide

Changes must be interpreted together with seismic, deformation, and thermal observations.

## Thermal Monitoring

Magma and hot volcanic gases can increase surface temperature.

Satellite and ground-based infrared instruments can detect thermal anomalies.

Thermal monitoring is also used to observe active lava flows and lava lakes.

## Volcanic Hazards

Major volcanic hazards include:

- lava flows
- ashfall
- pyroclastic flows
- lahars
- volcanic gases
- landslides
- tsunamis
- ballistic projectiles

Hazard type varies greatly among volcanoes.

## Volcanic Risk

Hazard and risk are not identical.

Hazard refers to potentially dangerous volcanic processes.

Risk depends on both the hazard and the exposure and vulnerability of people, infrastructure, and ecosystems.

A large volcano in an unpopulated region may pose lower human risk than a smaller volcano near a major city.

## Supereruptions

The informal term "supereruption" is commonly used for exceptionally large explosive eruptions.

Such events can produce enormous calderas and widespread deposits.

However, they are extremely rare.

Large caldera systems should not be assumed to be "overdue" based on simple average eruption intervals.

## Yellowstone

Yellowstone is a large volcanic and hydrothermal system associated with a continental hotspot.

It contains a major caldera and extensive geothermal activity.

Its present hydrothermal and seismic activity does not mean a giant eruption is imminent.

Monitoring data are required to assess changes in volcanic systems.

## Volcanoes Under the Oceans

Most people are familiar with terrestrial volcanoes on land, but enormous amounts of volcanic activity occur beneath Earth's oceans.

Mid-ocean ridges continually create new oceanic crust.

Submarine volcanism is therefore a fundamental part of Earth's geological activity.

## Volcanic Islands

Repeated submarine eruptions can build volcanic mountains high enough to emerge above sea level.

This creates volcanic islands.

Examples include the Hawaiian Islands.

Volcanic islands can later erode, subside, or develop coral reefs depending on environmental conditions.

## Volcanism and Crust Formation

Volcanism continuously creates new crust.

At mid-ocean ridges, basaltic magma forms oceanic crust.

Continental volcanic systems can also add material to continental crust.

Over geological time, magmatism contributes to crustal differentiation and chemical evolution.

## Volcanism and Planetary Differentiation

Earth's volcanic rocks provide samples derived directly or indirectly from the mantle.

Their compositions help scientists understand:

- mantle chemistry
- partial melting
- crust-mantle interactions
- volatile reservoirs
- planetary differentiation

Volcanism therefore provides a window into otherwise inaccessible parts of Earth's interior.

## Earth Compared With Venus

[[Venus]] is similar in size to Earth and contains extensive volcanic terrain.

However, Venus lacks a clearly established modern global plate-tectonic system.

Venusian volcanism is associated with:

- volcanic plains
- mantle upwellings
- rifts
- coronae
- large volcanoes

Evidence is consistent with geologically recent and likely ongoing activity.

Comparing Earth and Venus helps scientists understand why two similar-sized planets developed different tectonic systems.

## Earth Compared With Mars

[[Mars]] contains enormous shield volcanoes, including Olympus Mons.

Mars lacks modern Earth-like plate tectonics.

Because the Martian lithosphere did not move over magma sources in the same way as Earth's plates, individual volcanoes could remain above long-lived magma sources and grow to enormous sizes.

Mars also cooled more extensively because it is smaller than Earth.

## Earth Compared With Io

[[Io]] is the most volcanically active known world.

Its volcanism is powered mainly by extreme tidal heating rather than by an Earth-like plate-tectonic system.

Io demonstrates that strong volcanism can occur even on a small body when an external orbital energy source continuously generates internal heat.

## Earth Compared With the Moon

The [[Moon]] experienced extensive ancient basaltic volcanism that formed the lunar maria.

However, the Moon's small size caused it to cool more rapidly.

Earth retained much more internal heat and continues experiencing widespread volcanism today.

## Earth Compared With Mercury

[[Mercury]] preserves ancient volcanic plains and explosive volcanic deposits.

Its smaller size and unusual interior caused its major volcanic activity to decline much earlier.

Earth's continuing plate tectonics and greater retained heat sustain far more extensive present-day volcanism.

## Why Earth Does Not Have Olympus-Mons-Sized Volcanoes

Earth's plate tectonics continually moves lithosphere across many magma sources.

This limits how long a single volcanic structure may remain directly above a hotspot.

Erosion and gravitational deformation also modify volcanic mountains.

Mars lacks modern Earth-like plate motion and has lower gravity, allowing enormous structures such as Olympus Mons to develop.

## Volcanism and Planetary Habitability

Earth demonstrates that volcanism can contribute positively to long-term habitability.

Important effects include:

- atmospheric outgassing
- carbon cycling
- nutrient delivery
- creation of hydrothermal environments
- crust formation
- chemical energy sources

However, major eruptions can also produce severe environmental disruption.

Habitability therefore depends on the magnitude, frequency, and geological context of volcanic activity.

## Volcanism and the Origin of Life

Scientists investigate volcanic and hydrothermal environments as possible settings relevant to the origin of life.

Hydrothermal systems can provide:

- chemical gradients
- mineral catalysts
- heat
- reduced chemical compounds
- protected pore spaces

The exact environment in which life originated remains unknown.

Volcanic and hydrothermal settings represent important hypotheses rather than a confirmed single origin location.

## Common Misunderstandings

### Earth's Mantle Is a Global Ocean of Magma

Incorrect.

Most of Earth's mantle is solid.

It flows slowly over geological timescales and undergoes partial melting only in certain environments.

### Volcanoes Occur Only at Plate Boundaries

Incorrect.

Many occur at plate boundaries, but hotspot volcanism can occur within plates.

### Magma Comes From Earth's Core

Generally incorrect.

Most magma involved in surface volcanism originates through partial melting of the mantle or crust, not directly from the core.

### All Volcanoes Are Cone-Shaped Mountains

Incorrect.

Volcanism can produce shield volcanoes, fissures, lava plains, calderas, submarine ridges, lava domes, and many other landforms.

### All Eruptions Are Explosive

Incorrect.

Many eruptions are predominantly effusive.

### Volcanoes Cause Most Modern Global Warming

Incorrect.

Modern anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions greatly exceed volcanic carbon dioxide emissions.

Large eruptions can actually produce short-term cooling through sulfate aerosols.

### Yellowstone Is Overdue for an Eruption

Volcanoes do not follow simple schedules.

Average intervals between ancient eruptions cannot be used as countdown clocks.

### Volcanoes Are Only Destructive

Incorrect.

Volcanism also creates crust, islands, mineral deposits, soils, hydrothermal systems, and important chemical cycles.

## Evidence and How We Know

Earth's volcanism can be studied much more directly than volcanism on other worlds.

Evidence includes:

- direct eruption observations
- rock samples
- laboratory analysis
- seismic imaging
- GPS measurements
- satellite radar
- thermal observations
- gas measurements
- drilling
- geological mapping
- ocean-floor observations
- geochemical analysis

These methods allow scientists to connect observable eruptions with deeper geological processes.

## Scientific Importance

Earth is the primary reference world for understanding rocky-planet volcanism.

It demonstrates how internal heat, mantle convection, partial melting, plate tectonics, volatile cycling, atmosphere, oceans, and biology can interact.

Earth's volcanic system helps scientists interpret volcanic features on:

- Venus
- Mars
- Mercury
- the Moon
- Io
- other rocky worlds

It is also central to understanding [[Planetary Interiors]], [[Planetary Tectonics]], [[Planetary Atmospheres]], [[Planetary Evolution]], and [[Planetary Habitability]].

## Open Questions

Important research questions include:

- How exactly do mantle plumes originate?
- How is melt distributed beneath volcanic systems?
- What controls the timing of major eruptions?
- How does magma move through complex crustal networks?
- What determines whether magma erupts or solidifies underground?
- How did volcanism influence Earth's earliest atmosphere?
- What role did hydrothermal environments play in the origin of life?
- How do large igneous provinces affect climate and ecosystems?
- How has Earth's volcanic flux changed through geological time?
- How did plate tectonics and volcanism co-evolve?
- How accurately can volcanic unrest be translated into eruption forecasts?

## Summary

Earth is an actively volcanic planet whose geology is strongly controlled by plate tectonics.

Magma forms mainly through decompression melting, flux melting, and heat-transfer processes.

Volcanism occurs at mid-ocean ridges, subduction zones, continental rifts, and mantle hotspots.

Earth contains shield volcanoes, stratovolcanoes, lava domes, calderas, fissures, flood basalts, submarine volcanic systems, and numerous other volcanic landforms.

Volcanism creates new crust, transfers heat from the interior, releases gases, drives hydrothermal systems, participates in the carbon cycle, affects climate, and interacts with the biosphere.

Comparing Earth with Venus, Mars, Mercury, the Moon, and Io demonstrates that volcanic activity depends on planetary size, composition, internal heat, tectonic regime, volatile content, gravity, atmosphere, and external energy sources such as tidal heating.

Earth therefore provides the central reference point for comparative [[Planetary Volcanism]].
