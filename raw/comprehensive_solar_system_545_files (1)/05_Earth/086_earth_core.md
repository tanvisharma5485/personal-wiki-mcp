# Earth Core

**Dataset ID:** 086  
**Category:** 05 Earth

## Overview

Earth's core is the dense, metal-rich central region of the planet. It begins approximately 2,900 kilometers beneath Earth's surface at the core-mantle boundary and extends to Earth's center at a radius of about 6,371 kilometers.

The core is divided into two major regions:

**Liquid Outer Core → Solid Inner Core**

The outer core is predominantly liquid iron-rich alloy, while the inner core is solid despite its higher temperature because the enormous pressure at Earth's center raises the melting temperature of the material.

Earth's core is fundamental to the planet's thermal evolution, internal differentiation, magnetic field, and long-term geological history.

Unlike the deep interiors of most other planets, Earth's core can be studied in exceptional detail through global seismology. Earthquakes generate seismic waves that travel through the planet and reveal the locations, physical states, and properties of deep internal layers.

## Position Within Earth

Earth's major internal structure can be represented broadly as:

**Crust → Mantle → Outer Core → Inner Core**

The crust and mantle are dominated by silicate materials.

The core is primarily metallic.

The boundary between the mantle and core occurs at a depth of approximately 2,900 kilometers and represents one of the largest changes in physical properties anywhere inside Earth.

## Core-Mantle Boundary

The core-mantle boundary, commonly abbreviated CMB, separates Earth's rocky silicate mantle from its metallic outer core.

It lies approximately 2,900 kilometers below the surface.

Across this boundary:

- composition changes dramatically
- density increases
- seismic velocities change
- mechanical properties change
- heat is transferred from the core into the mantle

The CMB is therefore important for both core evolution and mantle dynamics.

## Total Size of Earth's Core

Earth's core extends from approximately 2,900 kilometers depth to the center of the planet.

Its total radius is approximately 3,480 kilometers.

The core therefore occupies slightly more than half of Earth's radius.

Because the core is much denser than the mantle, it contributes a large fraction of Earth's total mass.

## Outer Core

The outer core extends from the core-mantle boundary at approximately 2,900 kilometers depth to the inner-core boundary at approximately 5,150 kilometers depth.

It is roughly 2,260 kilometers thick.

The outer core is liquid.

Its motion is essential to the generation of Earth's global magnetic field.

## Inner Core

The inner core extends from approximately 5,150 kilometers depth to Earth's center at approximately 6,371 kilometers depth.

Its radius is about 1,220 kilometers.

The inner core is solid and consists primarily of iron-rich material.

Although its temperature is extremely high, the enormous pressure at Earth's center stabilizes the solid phase.

## Why the Outer Core Is Liquid

Whether iron-rich material is solid or liquid depends on both temperature and pressure.

Conditions in the outer core are sufficiently hot relative to the melting temperature of its composition for the material to remain liquid.

The presence of lighter elements in the core also affects melting behavior.

Seismic observations provide direct geophysical evidence that the outer core behaves as a liquid.

## Why the Inner Core Is Solid

Temperature increases toward Earth's center, but pressure also increases enormously.

High pressure raises the melting temperature of iron-rich material.

At the inner-core boundary, pressure becomes high enough that iron-rich alloy crystallizes despite the extreme temperature.

Therefore:

**Outer core = liquid**

**Inner core = solid**

This difference is primarily a consequence of the relationship among pressure, temperature, and composition.

## Composition of Earth's Core

Earth's core consists primarily of iron.

Nickel is also an important component.

However, the core cannot consist entirely of pure iron and nickel because its observed density is lower than would be expected for pure iron-rich metal under core conditions.

Scientists therefore infer that the core contains lighter elements.

Candidate light elements include:

- silicon
- oxygen
- sulfur
- carbon
- hydrogen

The exact proportions remain an active area of research.

## How Scientists Infer Core Composition

No sample has ever been collected directly from Earth's core.

Its composition is inferred using:

- seismic velocities
- seismic densities
- Earth's total mass
- moment of inertia
- meteorite chemistry
- high-pressure experiments
- theoretical mineral physics
- geochemical evidence
- planetary differentiation models

The strongest models must satisfy several independent constraints simultaneously.

## Iron Meteorites and Core Composition

Iron meteorites provide samples of metallic material from differentiated asteroid parent bodies.

They are not pieces of Earth's core.

However, they demonstrate how iron-rich metallic cores can form during planetary differentiation and provide useful information about metallic planetary materials.

Meteorites more broadly also help estimate the bulk chemical composition of the material from which Earth formed.

## Formation of Earth's Core

Earth's core formed early in planetary history through differentiation.

As the young Earth accreted, it was heated by:

- impacts
- gravitational energy
- radioactive decay
- differentiation itself

Large portions of the early planet became partially or extensively molten.

Dense metallic material separated from silicate material and migrated toward Earth's center.

This produced a metal-rich core surrounded by a silicate mantle.

## Metal-Silicate Differentiation

The separation of metallic and silicate material was both a physical and chemical process.

Dense metal moved inward under gravity.

At the same time, chemical elements partitioned differently between metallic and silicate phases.

Siderophile, or iron-loving, elements preferentially entered the metallic core under appropriate conditions.

Lithophile elements preferentially remained in silicate material.

As a result, core formation profoundly changed the chemistry of Earth's mantle.

## Magma Oceans

Early Earth probably experienced one or more extensive magma-ocean stages.

Large impacts and rapid accretion could melt substantial fractions of the silicate planet.

Metallic material descending through molten silicate could exchange chemical elements with the surrounding magma before entering the growing core.

Magma oceans therefore played an important role in models of Earth's core formation.

## Multi-Stage Core Formation

Earth's core probably did not form during one instantaneous event.

Earth grew through repeated accretion of smaller bodies.

Many impactors may already have been differentiated and contained their own metallic cores.

During collisions:

- impactor cores were delivered to Earth
- metallic material descended inward
- impacts generated additional melting
- metal and silicate interacted chemically
- Earth's core progressively grew

Core formation therefore occurred over multiple stages during planetary accretion.

## Hafnium-Tungsten Evidence

The hafnium-tungsten isotope system provides important constraints on the timing of Earth's early differentiation and core formation.

Hafnium behaves mainly as a lithophile element and tends to remain in silicate material.

Tungsten is more siderophile and can partition into metallic material.

Because radioactive hafnium-182 decayed to tungsten-182 early in Solar System history, tungsten isotopic compositions preserve information about the timing of metal-silicate separation.

The interpretation is model-dependent because Earth accreted and differentiated progressively rather than at one exact moment.

## Seismic Discovery of Earth's Core

One of the most powerful ways to study Earth's core is through seismic waves generated by earthquakes.

Two major types of body waves are:

- P waves
- S waves

Their behavior revealed that Earth's interior is layered.

## P Waves

P waves are compressional seismic waves.

They can travel through:

- solids
- liquids

When P waves encounter the core-mantle boundary, their velocity and direction change substantially.

This produces characteristic seismic patterns that reveal the existence and properties of the core.

## S Waves

S waves are shear waves.

They propagate through solids but cannot travel through ordinary liquids.

S waves traveling through Earth's mantle disappear when they reach the liquid outer core.

This produces an S-wave shadow zone.

The absence of S-wave transmission through the outer core is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that the outer core is liquid.

## Seismic Shadow Zones

Because seismic waves change speed and direction when they enter the core, some regions of Earth's surface receive particular types of seismic waves weakly or not at all after a large earthquake.

These regions are called seismic shadow zones.

Their geometry provided major early evidence for Earth's layered internal structure.

## Discovery of the Inner Core

The solid inner core was identified through subtle seismic observations.

In 1936, seismologist Inge Lehmann proposed that seismic waves reflected or refracted from a solid inner region could explain observations that could not be accounted for by a completely liquid core.

Subsequent seismology strongly confirmed the existence of the solid inner core.

## Inner-Core Boundary

The inner-core boundary, abbreviated ICB, separates the liquid outer core from the solid inner core.

It occurs at a depth of approximately 5,150 kilometers.

At this boundary, iron-rich liquid begins to crystallize because pressure and temperature conditions cross the melting relation of the core alloy.

## Pressure Inside the Core

Pressure increases dramatically with depth.

Near Earth's center, pressure reaches approximately 360 gigapascals.

That is millions of times atmospheric pressure at Earth's surface.

These enormous pressures strongly influence:

- material density
- melting temperatures
- crystal structures
- chemical behavior
- seismic velocities

Laboratory experiments and theoretical calculations are required to reproduce or estimate these conditions.

## Temperature of Earth's Core

Temperatures in Earth's core are estimated to reach several thousand degrees Celsius.

Near Earth's center, estimates are commonly around 5,000–6,000°C, though exact values remain uncertain.

The temperature cannot be measured directly.

Scientists infer it using:

- melting experiments
- seismic constraints
- mineral physics
- thermal models
- core evolution models

Exact estimates depend on core composition and material properties.

## Heat Sources in Earth's Core

Earth's core contains and releases heat from several sources.

These include:

- heat retained from planetary formation
- gravitational energy associated with differentiation
- secular cooling
- latent heat released during inner-core crystallization
- gravitational energy associated with compositional separation during crystallization

The relative importance of these energy sources has changed over geological time.

## Secular Cooling

Secular cooling refers to the gradual loss of Earth's internal heat over geological time.

As the core cools, heat flows across the core-mantle boundary into the mantle.

This cooling contributes energy that can drive convection in the liquid outer core.

## Inner-Core Growth

Earth's inner core grows as the planet gradually cools.

Iron-rich material crystallizes from the liquid outer core onto the inner core.

The inner core is therefore not simply an unchanged object left over from Earth's initial formation.

It is a product of later core cooling and crystallization.

Its exact age remains uncertain.

## Latent Heat

When liquid core material crystallizes, latent heat is released.

This heat contributes energy to the outer core.

Inner-core growth can therefore help sustain convection and magnetic-field generation.

## Compositional Convection

When iron-rich material crystallizes into the inner core, lighter elements can be preferentially excluded from the solid and released into the surrounding liquid.

The remaining liquid becomes locally more buoyant.

This compositional buoyancy can drive convection in the outer core.

It is an important energy source for Earth's modern dynamo.

## Outer-Core Convection

The liquid outer core is not static.

It undergoes complex fluid motion driven by thermal and compositional buoyancy.

Earth's rotation organizes some of this motion.

The interaction between moving electrically conducting fluid and planetary rotation is central to the geodynamo.

## Earth's Geodynamo

Earth's global magnetic field is generated primarily by motion within the liquid outer core.

This process is called the geodynamo.

The outer core contains electrically conducting iron-rich fluid.

As the fluid moves through an existing magnetic field, electrical currents are generated.

Those currents produce additional magnetic fields.

Under appropriate conditions, the system can maintain a self-sustaining dynamo.

## Requirements for a Dynamo

A planetary dynamo generally requires:

- an electrically conducting fluid
- sufficient fluid motion
- an energy source driving convection
- appropriate rotational organization

Earth's liquid outer core satisfies these conditions.

The existence of a metallic core alone is not sufficient.

Venus, for example, likely possesses a metallic core but does not currently maintain an Earth-like global intrinsic magnetic field.

## Earth's Magnetic Field

Earth's magnetic field extends far into space and creates a magnetosphere.

At the surface, the field resembles a tilted dipole to first order, although its real structure is much more complicated.

The field varies continuously because flow within the outer core changes with time.

## Secular Variation

Earth's magnetic field changes gradually over years to centuries.

This behavior is called secular variation.

It provides indirect information about flow in the outer core.

By tracking changes in the magnetic field, scientists can infer aspects of core dynamics that cannot be observed directly.

## Magnetic Reversals

Earth's magnetic field has reversed polarity many times during geological history.

During a reversal, the dominant magnetic polarity changes.

Records of these reversals are preserved in magnetic minerals in rocks, especially volcanic rocks and oceanic crust.

Magnetic reversals are natural outcomes of a complex dynamo and do not mean that Earth's core stops rotating.

## Paleomagnetism

Paleomagnetism is the study of ancient magnetic fields recorded in rocks.

It provides evidence for Earth's magnetic-field history over geological time.

Paleomagnetic observations help scientists investigate:

- dynamo longevity
- magnetic reversals
- plate tectonics
- continental motion

They therefore connect core dynamics with surface geology.

## Core-Mantle Heat Flow

Heat leaves the core across the core-mantle boundary.

The amount and spatial pattern of this heat flow influence core convection.

At the same time, mantle convection determines how efficiently heat is removed from the core.

Earth's core and mantle therefore evolve as a coupled thermal system.

## Mantle Control on Core Evolution

The mantle acts as a thermal boundary around the core.

If the mantle removes heat efficiently, stronger core cooling may occur.

If heat removal is inefficient, core convection can be reduced.

Large-scale mantle structures near the core-mantle boundary may therefore influence patterns of core heat flow and potentially aspects of the dynamo.

## The D'' Region

The lowermost several hundred kilometers of Earth's mantle are often called the D'' region.

This region lies immediately above the core-mantle boundary.

It contains complex seismic structures and strong thermal and chemical variations.

Interactions between the D'' region and the core are important for understanding deep mantle dynamics and heat transfer.

## Ultra-Low Velocity Zones

Some locations immediately above the core-mantle boundary contain ultra-low velocity zones, or ULVZs.

Seismic waves travel unusually slowly through these regions.

Their exact origins remain debated.

Possible explanations include:

- partial melting
- unusual chemical composition
- iron enrichment

ULVZs illustrate the complexity of the boundary between Earth's core and mantle.

## Inner-Core Structure

The inner core is not perfectly uniform.

Seismic waves travel through it differently depending on direction and location.

This phenomenon is known as seismic anisotropy.

It suggests that iron crystals or larger structures within the inner core possess preferred orientations.

## Inner-Core Anisotropy

Seismic anisotropy means that wave velocity depends on direction.

In parts of the inner core, seismic waves travel faster in some directions than others.

Possible explanations involve:

- alignment of iron crystals
- patterns of inner-core growth
- deformation
- convection or translation-like processes

The detailed structure remains an active research topic.

## Inner-Core Hemispheric Differences

Seismology suggests that the eastern and western hemispheres of the inner core may differ in seismic properties.

These differences could reflect variations in:

- crystal structure
- growth rate
- deformation
- thermal environment

The origin of this hemispheric asymmetry is not fully understood.

## Inner-Core Rotation

Researchers have investigated whether the inner core rotates at a slightly different rate from the mantle and crust.

Some seismic studies suggest differential rotation or changes in relative motion over time.

However, the magnitude and detailed behavior remain debated.

Claims about exact inner-core rotation rates should therefore be treated cautiously.

## Core Density

Earth's core is much denser than the mantle.

Density increases with depth because pressure compresses material.

The density contrast between mantle and core reflects both:

- composition
- compression

The outer core is approximately 10–12 g/cm³ across much of its depth, while inner-core density is greater.

Exact values vary with depth and model.

## PREM and Earth's Core

The Preliminary Reference Earth Model, or PREM, is a widely used one-dimensional model describing how properties such as:

- density
- seismic-wave velocity
- attenuation

vary with depth inside Earth.

PREM provides an important reference for interpreting Earth's core and mantle.

Real Earth structure, however, includes significant three-dimensional variations not represented by a simple radial model.

## Earth's Core and Moment of Inertia

Earth's measured moment of inertia demonstrates that mass is concentrated toward the center.

A uniform Earth would have a different normalized moment of inertia.

The observed value is consistent with a dense metallic core surrounded by a less-dense silicate mantle.

## High-Pressure Experiments

Scientists recreate core conditions using laboratory equipment.

Diamond-anvil cells can compress tiny samples to enormous pressures.

Laser heating can raise their temperatures to thousands of degrees.

These experiments investigate:

- iron melting
- iron alloys
- candidate light elements
- density
- sound velocity
- phase transitions

Experimental results are compared with seismic observations.

## Mineral Physics

Mineral physics studies how materials behave under extreme pressure and temperature.

For Earth's core, researchers investigate iron and iron alloys under deep-Earth conditions.

The goal is to determine which compositions and phases best match:

- observed density
- seismic velocities
- melting behavior
- thermal properties

## Computational Modeling

Quantum-mechanical calculations and large-scale numerical simulations complement laboratory experiments.

They help estimate material properties at conditions that are difficult to reproduce experimentally.

Computational methods are especially important for studying:

- iron alloys
- transport properties
- core crystallization
- inner-core structure

## Core Viscosity

The liquid outer core has a relatively low viscosity compared with solid mantle rock.

This allows large-scale fluid motion.

The exact effective viscosity and turbulence of core flow remain difficult to determine directly.

Core dynamics therefore require sophisticated fluid-dynamical and magnetohydrodynamic models.

## Magnetohydrodynamics

Magnetohydrodynamics, or MHD, describes the interaction between electrically conducting fluids and magnetic fields.

Earth's geodynamo is fundamentally an MHD process.

Numerical geodynamo models solve equations involving:

- fluid motion
- heat transport
- rotation
- electrical currents
- magnetic fields

These simulations help explain the behavior of Earth's magnetic field.

## Earth's Core Through Time

Earth's core has evolved continuously since planetary formation.

A simplified history is:

**Metal segregation → Early liquid core → Long-term cooling → Dynamo evolution → Inner-core nucleation → Continued inner-core growth**

The exact timing of these stages remains uncertain.

## Age of the Inner Core

The inner core is younger than Earth itself.

It formed only after the central core cooled enough for iron-rich material to begin crystallizing.

Published estimates for the age of the inner core vary substantially depending on assumptions about:

- core thermal conductivity
- core composition
- core-mantle heat flow
- dynamo history

It should therefore not be assigned a single universally certain age.

## The Core Before Inner-Core Formation

Earth's dynamo existed before the present solid inner core formed.

Earlier dynamo activity must therefore have been powered primarily by other mechanisms, especially thermal convection and core cooling.

The later onset of inner-core crystallization introduced additional latent-heat and compositional energy sources.

## Core and Plate Tectonics

Earth's core does not directly cause plate tectonics.

Plate tectonics occurs in the silicate mantle and lithosphere.

However, the mantle and core exchange heat.

Therefore Earth's long-term tectonic and core histories are indirectly coupled through planetary thermal evolution.

## Core and Volcanism

Magma responsible for ordinary volcanism forms mainly through partial melting of the mantle or crust, not by molten core material rising to the surface.

Earth's metallic outer core does not supply normal volcanic lava.

This distinction is important.

Volcanic activity and core evolution are connected through planetary heat flow but involve different materials and regions.

## Core and Habitability

Earth's core influences surface habitability indirectly.

The geodynamo generates a global magnetic field and magnetosphere.

The core also contributes to Earth's long-term thermal evolution.

However, habitability cannot be attributed to the core alone.

Atmosphere, water, climate, stellar environment, geology, chemistry, and biological processes are also essential.

## Earth's Core Compared With Mercury

Both Earth and Mercury possess:

- metallic cores
- liquid core material
- active magnetic dynamos

Mercury's core occupies a much larger fraction of its planetary radius.

Earth's silicate mantle is proportionally much thicker.

Earth's surface magnetic field is also considerably stronger.

## Earth's Core Compared With Venus

Venus is similar to Earth in size and bulk density and is expected to possess a substantial metallic core.

However, Venus does not currently have an Earth-like intrinsic global magnetic field.

The difference demonstrates that core presence alone does not guarantee dynamo activity.

The thermal history and convective state of the core and mantle are critical.

## Earth's Core Compared With Mars

Mars has a metallic core that remains at least substantially liquid, but it lacks a present global dynamo.

Ancient crustal magnetization indicates that Mars possessed a dynamo early in its history.

Earth's dynamo has persisted much longer.

Comparing Earth and Mars helps scientists investigate planetary cooling and dynamo longevity.

## Earth's Core Compared With the Moon

The Moon possesses a much smaller metallic core relative to its total size.

It has no present global dynamo.

Lunar rocks nevertheless preserve evidence for an ancient magnetic field.

Earth's larger size allowed it to retain internal heat much more effectively.

## Earth's Core Compared With Giant-Planet Cores

Earth's core should not be directly equated with the cores of Jupiter or Saturn.

Earth has a distinct iron-rich metallic core.

Giant planets contain enormous hydrogen-helium envelopes and deep heavy-element distributions that may be gradual rather than sharply bounded.

Jupiter's dilute core is fundamentally different from Earth's compact metallic core.

## Direct Sampling of Earth's Core

Humans have never drilled anywhere close to Earth's core.

The deepest boreholes penetrate only a tiny fraction of Earth's radius.

Our knowledge of the core is therefore indirect.

However, "indirect" does not mean speculative.

Seismology provides extremely strong observational constraints on the core's existence, dimensions, and physical state.

## Major Scientific Uncertainties

Important unresolved questions include:

- What are the exact abundances of light elements in the core?
- Which light elements dominate the outer core?
- How did core composition evolve during accretion?
- How efficiently did metal and silicate equilibrate during core formation?
- What is the exact temperature at the core-mantle boundary?
- What is Earth's central temperature?
- When did the inner core begin crystallizing?
- How rapidly is the inner core growing?
- How does inner-core anisotropy develop?
- Does the inner core rotate differently from the mantle?
- What causes hemispheric differences in inner-core structure?
- How heterogeneous is the core-mantle boundary?
- How does mantle structure influence core heat flow?
- What powered Earth's earliest dynamo?
- How has dynamo strength changed through geological time?
- How will the geodynamo evolve as Earth continues cooling?

## Common Misconceptions

### Earth's Core Is Completely Molten

Incorrect.

The outer core is liquid, but the inner core is solid.

### The Inner Core Is Cooler Than the Outer Core

Incorrect.

The inner core is generally hotter, but enormous pressure keeps it solid.

### Earth's Core Is Pure Iron

Incorrect.

It is iron-rich and contains nickel plus lighter elements.

### Scientists Have Drilled Into Earth's Core

Incorrect.

No drilling project has approached the core.

Its structure is determined primarily through geophysical evidence.

### S Waves Travel Through the Outer Core

Incorrect.

Ordinary S waves cannot propagate through the liquid outer core.

Their disappearance is major evidence for its liquid state.

### Earth's Magnetic Field Comes From a Permanent Bar Magnet

Incorrect.

The field is generated dynamically by moving electrically conducting fluid in the outer core.

### The Solid Inner Core Generates the Magnetic Field by Itself

Incorrect.

The geodynamo operates primarily in the liquid outer core.

Inner-core growth helps provide energy for convection.

### The Inner Core Has Existed Since Earth Formed

Incorrect.

The inner core formed later as the initially hotter core cooled enough to begin crystallizing.

### Earth's Core Causes Volcanoes Directly

Incorrect.

Ordinary magma forms primarily in the mantle and crust, not in the metallic core.

### Earth's Core Causes Plate Tectonics Directly

Incorrect.

Plate tectonics occurs within the lithosphere and mantle. Core-mantle heat exchange contributes to overall thermal evolution but is not a direct mechanical driver of individual plates.

### Earth's Core Is a Perfectly Uniform Sphere

Incorrect.

Both the core and its boundaries display complex structure and dynamic behavior.

## Why Earth's Core Matters

Earth's core provides the best-studied example of a planetary metallic core.

It allows scientists to investigate:

- planetary differentiation
- core formation
- metal-silicate chemistry
- high-pressure physics
- inner-core crystallization
- planetary cooling
- magnetic dynamos
- core-mantle coupling
- planetary evolution

Because Earth can be studied with dense seismic networks and direct surface measurements, it provides the primary physical framework used to interpret the less-accessible interiors of other planets.
