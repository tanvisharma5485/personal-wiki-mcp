\# Crater Erosion



\*\*Dataset ID:\*\* 307  

\*\*Category:\*\* 16 Planetary Surfaces



\## Overview



Crater erosion is the gradual destruction, weakening, or modification of impact-crater morphology after the crater has formed.



A fresh impact crater may initially have:



\- A sharp raised rim

\- Steep walls

\- A deep interior

\- Well-defined ejecta

\- Central peaks in larger craters

\- Rays around some young craters



Over time, geological and environmental processes can degrade these features.



The rate of crater erosion differs enormously between planetary bodies. On Earth, a crater can be strongly modified by water, wind, ice, vegetation, tectonics, and sedimentation. On the Moon, where these processes are largely absent, craters may remain recognizable for billions of years.



Crater erosion is therefore an important indicator of planetary surface history.



\## Crater Formation vs Crater Erosion



Crater formation occurs rapidly, often within seconds or minutes.



Crater erosion occurs afterward and may continue for:



\- Years

\- Thousands of years

\- Millions of years

\- Billions of years



Crater erosion should therefore be distinguished from the collapse that occurs during the original crater-formation process.



\## Fresh Craters



A relatively fresh crater commonly preserves:



\- Sharp rim crest

\- Steep interior walls

\- Clear ejecta deposits

\- High depth-to-diameter ratio

\- Central peak or peak structures where appropriate

\- Limited sediment filling

\- Few superposed craters



These characteristics gradually become less distinct as degradation progresses.



\## Degraded Craters



An old or heavily modified crater may show:



\- Rounded rim

\- Reduced depth

\- Eroded walls

\- Sediment-filled floor

\- Buried ejecta

\- Damaged central peak

\- Partial rim destruction

\- Superposed younger craters



In extreme cases, only subtle topographic or geophysical evidence remains.



\## Water Erosion



Liquid water is one of the most effective agents of crater erosion.



Rainfall and flowing water can:



\- Cut channels through crater rims

\- Erode crater walls

\- Transport ejecta

\- Carry sediment into crater interiors

\- Remove exposed impact deposits



This process is especially important on Earth and was important during wetter periods of Martian history.



\## River Erosion



Rivers can intersect impact structures and progressively modify them.



A river may:



\- Breach the crater rim

\- Drain a crater lake

\- Deposit sediment on the crater floor

\- Remove portions of ejecta



After long periods, the original circular morphology may become difficult to recognize.



\## Crater Lakes



Impact craters often create natural depressions where water can accumulate.



Crater lakes may form when:



\- Precipitation fills the depression

\- Rivers enter the crater

\- Groundwater rises

\- Ice melts within the basin



Sediment accumulating at the bottom can gradually bury the original crater floor.



\## Sediment Filling



Crater interiors are natural sediment traps.



Sediment can come from:



\- Rivers

\- Wind

\- Landslides

\- Glaciers

\- Volcanic ash

\- Lake deposition



As sediment accumulates, crater depth decreases.



Eventually, a deeply buried crater may no longer be visible from surface topography.



\## Wind Erosion



Wind can significantly modify craters on worlds with atmospheres.



Wind may:



\- Remove fine ejecta

\- Erode crater walls

\- Transport sand

\- Deposit dunes

\- Fill depressions with dust



Wind erosion is especially important on Mars.



\## Aeolian Modification on Mars



Mars has an active atmosphere capable of transporting dust and sand.



Martian craters may contain:



\- Sand dunes

\- Wind-eroded deposits

\- Dust layers

\- Yardang-like features



Wind can both erode and bury crater structures.



\## Glacial Erosion



Moving glaciers can strongly modify impact structures.



Glacial ice can:



\- Scrape crater rims

\- Deepen valleys

\- Transport rock

\- Deposit sediment

\- Remove surface expressions of craters



Glaciation has altered several terrestrial impact structures.



\## Ice-Related Modification on Mars



Mars contains abundant evidence of past and present ice.



Crater modification may involve:



\- Glacial flow

\- Ice-rich debris

\- Freeze-thaw-related processes

\- Sublimation

\- Ice loss



These processes can substantially alter crater interiors and rims.



\## Mass Wasting



Material on steep crater walls can move downslope under gravity.



Processes include:



\- Landslides

\- Rockfalls

\- Debris flows

\- Slumping



Mass wasting gradually reduces wall steepness and fills the crater floor.



\## Rim Degradation



Fresh crater rims are relatively sharp.



With time, they become rounded and lowered.



Rim degradation may result from:



\- Erosion

\- Mass wasting

\- Later impacts

\- Tectonic disruption

\- Regolith movement



Rim condition is therefore one indicator of crater preservation state.



\## Ejecta Degradation



Fresh ejecta deposits can extend far beyond the crater rim.



Over time, ejecta may be:



\- Eroded

\- Buried

\- Mixed into regolith

\- Covered by lava

\- Disrupted by later impacts

\- Altered by weathering



Ancient craters may therefore retain recognizable rims while losing obvious ejecta deposits.



\## Crater Ray Fading



Young craters on airless bodies may possess bright ray systems.



These rays fade over time because of:



\- Micrometeorite impacts

\- Space weathering

\- Regolith mixing



Ray preservation can therefore provide qualitative information about relative crater age.



\## Micrometeorite Bombardment



Even without an atmosphere or liquid water, airless surfaces are continually struck by tiny particles.



Over long periods, micrometeorite bombardment:



\- Breaks rocks into regolith

\- Softens small-scale topography

\- Mixes surface material

\- Degrades ejecta

\- Weakens crater rims



This process is often called impact gardening.



\## Impact Gardening



Impact gardening is the repeated churning and mixing of planetary regolith by small impacts.



It is especially important on:



\- Moon

\- Mercury

\- Asteroids



Over geological time, impact gardening can gradually soften ancient crater morphology.



\## Later Impacts



New impacts can destroy or modify older craters.



A younger crater may:



\- Remove part of an older rim

\- Cover older terrain with ejecta

\- Create secondary craters

\- Reshape the crater floor



On heavily cratered worlds, overlapping impacts become a major form of surface modification.



\## Crater Saturation



On extremely old surfaces, craters may become so numerous that new impacts increasingly destroy existing ones.



This condition is called \*\*crater saturation\*\*.



At saturation, crater production and crater destruction approach a statistical balance.



\## Volcanic Burial



Volcanic eruptions can bury impact craters beneath lava.



Lava may:



\- Fill crater floors

\- Cover ejecta

\- Bury small craters completely

\- Leave only portions of larger rims visible



Volcanic resurfacing has modified craters on the Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, and other worlds.



\## Ghost Craters



A crater that has been mostly buried but remains partially visible is sometimes called a \*\*ghost crater\*\*.



Possible surviving features include:



\- Circular rim segments

\- Low ridges

\- Subtle topographic depressions



Ghost craters provide evidence of an older surface beneath younger deposits.



\## Tectonic Modification



Faulting and crustal deformation can distort craters.



Tectonic activity may:



\- Offset crater rims

\- Fracture crater floors

\- Tilt crater structures

\- Stretch or compress crater terrain



This can make ancient craters difficult to identify.



\## Plate Tectonics on Earth



Earth's plate tectonics is one of the most powerful mechanisms for destroying ancient impact structures.



Oceanic crust is continually recycled through subduction.



As a result, almost no very ancient oceanic impact craters survive.



Continental impact structures may also be:



\- Faulted

\- Folded

\- Buried

\- Eroded



\## Weathering



Weathering breaks down exposed crater rocks without necessarily transporting them immediately.



Major forms include:



\- Physical weathering

\- Chemical weathering



Weathering weakens crater rims, ejecta, and exposed impact rocks.



\## Physical Weathering



Physical weathering breaks rock into smaller fragments.



Processes can include:



\- Thermal expansion and contraction

\- Freeze-thaw cycles

\- Salt crystallization

\- Mechanical fracturing



These processes make crater materials easier to erode.



\## Chemical Weathering



Chemical reactions alter minerals exposed to:



\- Water

\- Oxygen

\- Acids

\- Other reactive substances



Chemical weathering is particularly important on Earth.



It can alter impact melt, breccias, and shocked rocks.



\## Biological Effects



On Earth, biological processes can contribute indirectly to crater degradation.



Examples include:



\- Plant roots breaking rock

\- Soil formation

\- Organic chemical activity

\- Sediment stabilization and redistribution



Vegetation can also hide crater morphology from direct observation.



\## Marine Modification



Impact craters formed in or later submerged beneath oceans can be difficult to recognize.



Marine processes can:



\- Deposit thick sediments

\- Erode structures

\- Alter impact rocks

\- Hide crater morphology beneath the seafloor



Geophysical methods are often necessary to identify such structures.



\## Crater Erosion on Earth



Earth has one of the poorest preserved ancient crater records among rocky worlds.



Major causes include:



\- Rain

\- Rivers

\- Oceans

\- Glaciers

\- Wind

\- Sedimentation

\- Plate tectonics

\- Volcanism

\- Biological activity



Many terrestrial impact structures are therefore recognized through geology and geophysics rather than obvious crater shapes.



\## Crater Preservation on the Moon



The Moon has:



\- No substantial atmosphere

\- No rainfall

\- No rivers

\- No oceans

\- No active global plate tectonics



As a result, large lunar craters can survive for billions of years.



However, they are still modified by:



\- Later impacts

\- Impact gardening

\- Mass wasting

\- Volcanic flooding

\- Space weathering



\## Crater Erosion on Mercury



Mercury also preserves an extensive ancient crater record.



Its lack of atmosphere and liquid-water erosion promotes long-term preservation.



Nevertheless, craters can be altered by:



\- Later impacts

\- Volcanism

\- Tectonic deformation

\- Mass wasting

\- Space weathering



\## Crater Erosion on Mars



Mars occupies an intermediate position between Earth and the Moon.



Its craters can be modified by:



\- Wind

\- Dust deposition

\- Sand transport

\- Ancient rivers

\- Lakes

\- Glaciers

\- Ground ice

\- Mass wasting

\- Volcanism



Different Martian regions therefore preserve craters to very different degrees.



\## Crater Modification on Venus



Venus has a dense atmosphere and an active geological history.



Its impact craters may be modified by:



\- Volcanism

\- Tectonic deformation

\- Atmospheric processes



Its dense atmosphere also prevents many small impactors from forming craters in the first place.



\## Crater Degradation on Icy Worlds



On icy satellites, crater degradation can occur through processes uncommon on rocky planets.



These include:



\- Viscous relaxation

\- Ice tectonics

\- Sublimation

\- Cryovolcanism

\- Surface renewal



Ice can slowly flow under its own weight, causing craters to become shallower.



\## Viscous Relaxation



Viscous relaxation occurs when solid material slowly flows over geological time.



It is especially important in relatively warm ice.



A crater affected by relaxation may gradually lose:



\- Depth

\- Sharp walls

\- Central topography



Large craters are particularly susceptible.



\## Europa



Europa contains relatively few preserved impact craters.



Its icy surface has been strongly modified by geological activity.



Processes may include:



\- Ice tectonics

\- Surface deformation

\- Resurfacing

\- Possible cryovolcanism



The low crater density indicates a relatively young exposed surface.



\## Enceladus



Enceladus shows strong regional differences in crater preservation.



Older terrain contains numerous craters.



Younger active regions contain far fewer.



Near the south pole, tectonic and cryovolcanic activity continually modifies the surface.



\## Ganymede



Ganymede preserves both heavily cratered terrain and younger grooved terrain.



The contrast shows that tectonic resurfacing modified some areas while others retained older crater populations.



\## Callisto



Callisto's ancient surface is heavily cratered because widespread resurfacing has been limited.



Nevertheless, icy crater structures can still undergo long-term relaxation and impact modification.



\## Crater Degradation Classes



Planetary scientists may describe craters according to preservation state.



A simplified sequence is:



\### Fresh



Sharp rim, clear ejecta, high relief.



\### Slightly Degraded



Minor rim rounding and wall modification.



\### Moderately Degraded



Lower rim, smoother walls, partial infilling.



\### Heavily Degraded



Poorly defined rim, shallow interior, substantial burial or erosion.



\### Nearly Erased



Only subtle topographic, geological, or geophysical evidence remains.



\## Crater Depth as an Erosion Indicator



Crater depth can provide evidence about degradation.



A crater that is much shallower than expected for its diameter may have experienced:



\- Sediment filling

\- Erosion

\- Lava flooding

\- Ice relaxation

\- Wall collapse



However, original crater morphology must also be considered.



\## Remote Sensing of Eroded Craters



Spacecraft can detect degraded craters using:



\- High-resolution imaging

\- Topographic mapping

\- Radar

\- Spectroscopy

\- Gravity measurements



These methods can reveal structures that are difficult to recognize visually.



\## Detecting Buried Craters on Earth



Scientists use several techniques to identify deeply eroded or buried impact structures.



These include:



\- Seismic surveys

\- Gravity anomalies

\- Magnetic anomalies

\- Drilling

\- Geological mapping

\- Shocked mineral identification



\## Shocked Minerals



Even after surface morphology has been destroyed, impact-generated minerals may survive.



Examples include:



\- Shocked quartz

\- High-pressure mineral phases



These provide strong evidence for an impact origin.



\## Shatter Cones



Shatter cones are distinctive fracture structures produced by high-pressure shock waves.



They can survive long after a crater's original topography has been eroded.



They are important indicators of terrestrial impact structures.



\## Impact Breccias



Impact breccias contain broken rock fragments produced during impact.



They can help identify old crater structures even when surface morphology is poorly preserved.



\## Impact Melt



Impact-generated melt rocks may also survive erosion.



Radiometric dating of impact melt can sometimes determine the age of an impact event.



\## Erosion and Crater Counting



Crater erosion affects surface-age estimates.



If craters are removed faster than expected, simple crater counts may underestimate the true exposure age of the surface.



Scientists therefore consider:



\- Preservation state

\- Resurfacing

\- Burial

\- Erosion rates

\- Target properties



when interpreting crater statistics.



\## Erosion vs Resurfacing



These concepts overlap but are not identical.



\*\*Erosion\*\* removes or wears away existing material.



\*\*Resurfacing\*\* creates or exposes a newer surface, often covering or replacing older terrain.



Examples of resurfacing include:



\- Lava flows

\- Cryovolcanic deposits

\- New tectonic surfaces



Both processes can reduce visible crater density.



\## Why Crater Erosion Matters



Crater degradation can reveal information about processes that occurred after the original impact.



For example:



\- River-eroded craters can indicate past liquid water.

\- Glacially modified craters can indicate past ice.

\- Relaxed craters can reveal thermal properties of icy crust.

\- Lava-filled craters can reveal volcanic resurfacing.

\- Faulted craters can reveal tectonic activity.



Therefore, a damaged crater can sometimes provide more information about planetary evolution than a perfectly preserved one.



\## Common Misconceptions



\### Craters disappear only because of wind and rain



Incorrect.



Crater destruction can also result from tectonics, volcanism, sedimentation, later impacts, ice flow, and other processes.



\### Airless worlds experience no crater erosion



Incorrect.



They still experience impact gardening, mass wasting, later impacts, volcanic burial, and space weathering.



\### A shallow crater must have formed shallow



Incorrect.



It may originally have been deeper and later become filled, eroded, or relaxed.



\### If a crater is no longer visible, all evidence of the impact is gone



Incorrect.



Buried structures, shocked minerals, impact melt, gravity anomalies, and other evidence may remain.



\### Few visible craters always mean few impacts occurred



Incorrect.



Crater removal can erase evidence of earlier bombardment.



\## Scientific Importance



Studying crater erosion helps scientists reconstruct:



\- Surface ages

\- Geological activity

\- Climate history

\- Water history

\- Ice history

\- Volcanic resurfacing

\- Tectonic evolution

\- Regolith development

\- Crustal properties



\## Open Scientific Questions



Important research questions include:



\- How rapidly do craters degrade under different Martian climates?

\- How much ancient crater terrain is buried beneath planetary surfaces?

\- How quickly do large craters relax on icy moons?

\- How strongly does target composition control erosion rates?

\- How can heavily degraded craters be identified automatically?

\- How should erosion be incorporated into crater chronology models?

\- How many ancient impact structures have been erased from Earth's geological record?
