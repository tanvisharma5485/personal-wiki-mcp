\# Simple Vs Complex Craters



\*\*Dataset ID:\*\* 304  

\*\*Category:\*\* 16 Planetary Surfaces



\## Overview



Impact craters are commonly divided into \*\*simple craters\*\* and \*\*complex craters\*\* based on their size and final morphology.



Small craters are usually bowl-shaped and remain relatively stable after excavation. Larger craters undergo major gravitational collapse, causing their walls to slump inward and their floors to rise. This produces features such as central peaks, terraces, and peak rings.



The transition from simple to complex morphology depends mainly on:



\- Surface gravity

\- Target material

\- Crustal strength

\- Temperature

\- Ice content

\- Crater size



\## Simple Craters



Simple craters are relatively small impact structures.



They are generally characterized by:



\- Bowl-shaped interiors

\- Raised rims

\- Relatively steep walls

\- Smooth or brecciated floors

\- Ejecta blankets

\- No major central uplift



Their final shape remains fairly close to the transient cavity formed during excavation.



\## How Simple Craters Form



After impact:



\- Shock waves compress the target

\- Material is excavated

\- A transient cavity forms

\- Loose material slides down the walls

\- The cavity stabilizes



Because the crater is relatively small, gravity does not cause major structural collapse.



\## Simple Crater Depth



Simple craters are relatively deep compared with their diameter.



Their depth-to-diameter ratio is therefore larger than that of complex craters.



As simple craters age, their depth may decrease because of:



\- Erosion

\- Sedimentation

\- Wall collapse

\- Later impacts

\- Regolith movement



\## Raised Rim



Material near the crater edge is uplifted during crater formation.



This creates a raised rim.



The rim can include:



\- Uplifted target rock

\- Excavated material

\- Ejecta deposits



Fresh rims are usually sharper than old degraded rims.



\## Ejecta Blanket



Excavated material lands around the crater and forms an ejecta blanket.



Near the crater, ejecta can be thick and continuous.



Farther away, it becomes thinner and more discontinuous.



\## Complex Craters



Complex craters are larger impact structures that undergo significant gravitational collapse after excavation.



Typical features include:



\- Central peaks

\- Flat or uplifted floors

\- Terraced walls

\- Wider and shallower profiles

\- Large collapsed rims



Complex craters are not simply enlarged simple craters.



Their final morphology results from structural collapse of the transient crater.



\## Why Complex Craters Collapse



A very large transient crater is unstable.



Its walls are too steep and too high to remain intact.



Gravity therefore causes:



\- Wall slumping

\- Inward collapse

\- Faulting

\- Floor uplift

\- Central rebound



This modification stage transforms the transient crater into a complex crater.



\## Central Peaks



Many complex craters contain a central mountain or group of peaks.



These form when deeply compressed material beneath the crater rapidly rebounds upward.



Central peaks can expose rocks from deep below the original surface.



This makes them valuable for studying planetary crusts.



\## Terraced Walls



Large crater walls often collapse along curved faults.



Blocks of crust slide downward toward the crater floor.



This produces step-like terraces.



Terraced crater walls are common on:



\- Moon

\- Mercury

\- Mars



\## Flat Floors



Complex craters often have relatively broad and flat floors.



These can contain:



\- Impact melt

\- Brecciated material

\- Landslide deposits

\- Later sediment

\- Lava



The floor may also be uplifted during crater collapse.



\## Simple-to-Complex Transition



The diameter at which crater morphology changes from simple to complex is called the \*\*simple-to-complex transition diameter\*\*.



It differs from one planetary body to another.



The transition is strongly affected by gravity.



Higher-gravity bodies generally reach complex morphology at smaller crater diameters.



\## Why Gravity Matters



Gravity controls how easily crater walls collapse.



On a high-gravity world:



\- Walls fail more readily

\- Central uplift occurs at smaller crater sizes

\- Complex morphology begins sooner



On a low-gravity world:



\- Larger craters may remain simple for longer

\- Ejecta travels farther



\## Earth



Earth has relatively high surface gravity.



As a result, complex craters begin at comparatively small diameters.



On Earth, the transition from simple to complex morphology is roughly around a few kilometers, although exact values vary with target conditions.



Many ancient complex terrestrial craters have been altered by erosion and tectonics.



\## Moon



The Moon has lower gravity than Earth.



Therefore, simple craters can grow to larger diameters before collapsing into complex forms.



The transition occurs at roughly tens of kilometers, depending on local conditions and classification.



\## Mercury



Mercury displays excellent examples of both simple and complex craters.



Its low erosion allows:



\- Central peaks

\- Terraces

\- Ejecta

\- Peak rings



to remain well preserved.



\## Mars



Mars contains many complex craters.



Some have been modified by:



\- Wind

\- Sedimentation

\- Ice

\- Water-related processes

\- Volcanism



Martian complex craters can therefore preserve both impact and climatic history.



\## Icy Moons



Complex crater morphology on icy worlds can differ significantly from rocky planets.



Ice can deform over geological time.



Possible features include:



\- Central pits

\- Central domes

\- Relaxed crater floors

\- Shallow profiles

\- Modified peak structures



\## Peak-Ring Craters



At still larger sizes, the central peak can be replaced by a ring of mountains.



These are called \*\*peak-ring craters\*\*.



Peak-ring craters represent a transition between ordinary complex craters and giant impact basins.



\## Peak Rings



Peak rings form from extreme uplift and collapse of deeply shocked crust.



Their exact formation mechanism is still actively studied.



They can expose deep crustal material and therefore provide important geological information.



\## Multi-Ring Basins



The largest impact structures may develop multiple concentric rings.



These are called \*\*multi-ring basins\*\*.



They form when enormous transient cavities undergo large-scale structural collapse.



Examples occur on:



\- Moon

\- Mercury

\- Callisto



\## Crater Diameter and Morphology



A simplified progression is:



\### Small impacts



Produce simple bowl-shaped craters.



\### Larger impacts



Produce complex craters with central peaks and terraces.



\### Very large impacts



Produce peak-ring structures.



\### Giant impacts



Produce multi-ring basins.



\## Depth-to-Diameter Ratio



Simple and complex craters differ strongly in relative depth.



Simple craters are:



\- Narrower

\- Deeper relative to diameter



Complex craters are:



\- Wider

\- Shallower relative to diameter



This happens because gravitational collapse broadens and fills complex craters.



\## Target Strength



Crater morphology also depends on the mechanical strength of the target.



Targets may consist of:



\- Strong rock

\- Weak sediment

\- Ice

\- Regolith

\- Porous material



Weak targets may collapse differently from strong ones.



\## Target Layering



Layered crust can influence:



\- Wall collapse

\- Central uplift

\- Terrace formation

\- Crater depth



A crater may therefore reveal subsurface geological boundaries.



\## Temperature



Hotter crust is mechanically weaker.



This can affect:



\- Crater collapse

\- Central uplift

\- Relaxation

\- Final crater depth



Temperature is particularly important for icy moons.



\## Crater Degradation



Over time, both simple and complex craters can lose their original shape.



Processes include:



\- Erosion

\- Sedimentation

\- Lava flooding

\- Tectonic deformation

\- Mass wasting

\- Ice flow

\- Later impacts



A heavily degraded complex crater may lose its central peak or terraces.



\## Viscous Relaxation



On icy worlds, large craters may slowly become shallower because warm ice flows over long timescales.



This is called \*\*viscous relaxation\*\*.



It can make ancient complex craters difficult to recognize.



\## Central Peaks as Geological Windows



Central peaks are scientifically valuable because they can uplift material from several kilometers below the original surface.



Remote sensing of these peaks can reveal:



\- Deep crustal minerals

\- Buried geological layers

\- Crustal composition



\## Complex Craters and Impact Melt



Larger craters generate more impact melt.



Melt may accumulate:



\- On crater floors

\- Along terraces

\- Within depressions

\- Beneath ejecta



Large complex craters can contain extensive melt sheets.



\## Secondary Modification



After crater formation, later geological processes may alter morphology.



For example:



A complex crater can later become:



\- Sediment-filled

\- Lava-flooded

\- Eroded

\- Ice-filled

\- Partially buried



Therefore, morphology must be interpreted together with geological context.



\## Scientific Importance



The simple-to-complex transition provides information about:



\- Surface gravity

\- Crustal strength

\- Crustal temperature

\- Target composition

\- Impact mechanics

\- Planetary structure



Central peaks and peak rings also help scientists investigate deep crust.



\## Simple vs Complex Craters Summary



\### Simple Crater



Typical properties:



\- Smaller

\- Bowl shaped

\- Relatively deep

\- Raised rim

\- Ejecta blanket

\- No major central peak



\### Complex Crater



Typical properties:



\- Larger

\- Relatively shallow

\- Central peak

\- Terraced walls

\- Broad floor

\- Strong gravitational collapse



\### Peak-Ring Crater



Typical properties:



\- Very large

\- Ring-shaped central uplift

\- Extensive collapse

\- Large impact-melt deposits



\### Impact Basin



Typical properties:



\- Largest class

\- Enormous diameter

\- Multiple rings possible

\- Deep crustal deformation

\- Large-scale ejecta and melt



\## Common Misconceptions



\### Complex craters are simply bigger simple craters



Incorrect.



They undergo major gravitational collapse and structural reorganization.



\### Central peaks are pieces of the impactor



Incorrect.



They are mainly uplifted target material.



\### Every planet has the same simple-to-complex transition diameter



Incorrect.



The transition depends strongly on gravity and target properties.



\### Bigger craters are always proportionally deeper



Incorrect.



Large complex craters are actually shallower relative to their diameter because of collapse.



\### Peak rings are ordinary crater rims



Incorrect.



They form inside large craters through deep structural uplift and collapse.



\## Open Scientific Questions



Important research topics include:



\- Exact mechanism of peak-ring formation

\- How temporarily weakened rock flows during crater collapse

\- Effects of crustal layering

\- Differences between rock and ice targets

\- Influence of target temperature

\- Transition behavior on small bodies

\- How porosity changes crater collapse

\- How complex craters evolve over billions of years
