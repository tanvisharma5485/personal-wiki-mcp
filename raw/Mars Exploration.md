\# Mars Exploration



\## Overview



Mars is one of the most extensively explored worlds beyond Earth. Since the beginning of the Space Age, dozens of spacecraft have been launched toward \[\[Mars]] to investigate its surface, atmosphere, climate, geology, interior, magnetic history, water inventory, and potential habitability.



Mars exploration has progressed through several stages. Early flybys established the basic appearance of the planet, orbiters later mapped its surface globally, landers directly measured atmospheric and surface conditions, and increasingly sophisticated rovers began exploring individual geological environments in detail.



Modern missions investigate Mars as an evolving planetary system rather than simply photographing its surface. A major scientific objective is reconstructing how early Mars changed from a world containing rivers, lakes, groundwater, and possibly long-lived surface water into the cold and dry planet observed today.



Mars is also a major target in astrobiology. No mission has established that life exists or previously existed on Mars, but spacecraft have identified ancient environments that could once have been habitable. Current exploration therefore focuses heavily on understanding those environments, preserving scientifically valuable samples, and preparing for increasingly capable robotic and eventual human exploration.



\## Early Exploration: Mariner and the First Successful Encounters



Early attempts to reach Mars were difficult, and several spacecraft failed before successful exploration became possible.



NASA's Mariner 4 performed the first successful close flyby of Mars in July 1965. It returned the first close-range photographs of another planet and revealed a cratered surface. Its measurements also showed that the Martian atmosphere is much thinner than Earth's.



The limited area photographed by Mariner 4 initially encouraged an image of Mars as a largely Moon-like, geologically inactive world. Later missions demonstrated that this interpretation was incomplete.



Mariner 6 and Mariner 7 flew past Mars in 1969 and returned additional images and atmospheric measurements.



A major advance came with Mariner 9, which entered orbit around Mars in November 1971 and became the first spacecraft to orbit another planet.



When Mariner 9 arrived, a global dust storm obscured much of the surface. As the atmosphere cleared, the spacecraft revealed enormous geological structures including giant volcanoes, extensive canyon systems, ancient river valleys, channels, and diverse terrain.



Among the features mapped were Olympus Mons and Valles Marineris. These discoveries demonstrated that Mars had experienced extensive \[\[Planetary Volcanism]], \[\[Planetary Tectonics]], erosion, and surface modification.



Mariner 9 fundamentally changed the scientific picture of Mars from a simple cratered world to a planet with a complex geological and climatic history.



\## Viking Orbiters and Landers



NASA's Viking program represented the next major stage of Mars exploration.



Viking 1 and Viking 2 each consisted of an orbiter and a lander. Both missions reached Mars in 1976.



The orbiters mapped large portions of the planet and identified geological features associated with ancient water flow, volcanism, impacts, and atmospheric processes.



The landers performed the first fully successful long-duration surface operations on Mars.



They returned detailed photographs of rocky Martian landscapes and measured atmospheric pressure, temperature, winds, and chemical composition. Their experiments showed that the atmosphere is dominated by carbon dioxide and confirmed the extremely dry and cold conditions at the landing sites.



Viking also carried experiments designed to search for possible biological activity in Martian soil.



The results proved difficult to interpret. Some experiments produced chemical responses, but the overall evidence did not establish the presence of life. Later discoveries about reactive chemicals in Martian soil further complicated interpretation of the Viking results.



The Viking missions therefore should not be described as having discovered life on Mars.



Their greatest legacy was establishing a foundation for modern Mars surface science and demonstrating that spacecraft could successfully land and operate for extended periods on the planet.



\## Return to Mars and Global Mapping



After Viking, there was a long gap before sustained Mars exploration resumed.



Mars Global Surveyor entered orbit in 1997 and produced detailed global mapping of the planet. Its instruments studied topography, mineralogy, gravity, magnetic properties, atmosphere, and surface changes.



One of its most important contributions was evidence for ancient crustal magnetic fields. Mars does not possess a strong present global magnetic field like Earth, but magnetized regions of ancient crust indicate that the planet once operated a global dynamo.



This discovery became important for understanding \[\[Planetary Magnetism]] and \[\[Atmospheric Escape]]. As Mars's internal dynamo weakened, the atmosphere became more directly exposed to the \[\[Solar Wind]], contributing to long-term atmospheric loss.



Mars Odyssey arrived in 2001 and used gamma-ray and neutron measurements to investigate surface composition. It detected large quantities of hydrogen in the shallow subsurface at high latitudes, interpreted mainly as water ice.



Mars Express, launched by the European Space Agency, entered Mars orbit in 2003. It has investigated geology, mineralogy, atmosphere, plasma interactions, subsurface structure, and the planet's moons \[\[Phobos]] and \[\[Deimos]].



Its observations contributed to evidence for water-related minerals and the long-term geological evolution of Mars.



\## Spirit and Opportunity



NASA's Spirit and Opportunity rovers landed on opposite sides of Mars in January 2004.



Both were originally designed for missions lasting about 90 Martian days, but each operated far beyond its planned lifetime.



Spirit explored Gusev crater. Although the landing region initially appeared less obviously water-altered than expected, the rover eventually discovered minerals and geological evidence indicating past interaction with liquid water and hydrothermal environments.



Spirit became trapped in soft soil in 2009 and later stopped communicating, with its mission officially ending in 2011.



Opportunity landed in Meridiani Planum and discovered extensive evidence that liquid water had altered the region in the ancient past.



It identified sulfate-rich sedimentary rocks, hematite-rich spherical concretions informally called blueberries, and geological structures indicating past water activity.



Opportunity continued exploring Mars for more than fourteen years and traveled more than 45 kilometers, dramatically exceeding its original mission design.



Communication was lost after a major global dust storm in 2018, and the mission was formally concluded in 2019.



Together, Spirit and Opportunity established mobile robotic geology as a powerful method for planetary exploration.



\## Phoenix and Martian Water Ice



NASA's Phoenix lander reached the northern polar region of Mars in 2008.



Unlike the mobile rovers, Phoenix remained at a fixed landing site and used a robotic arm to excavate material beneath the surface.



The mission directly exposed bright subsurface material that disappeared gradually after excavation, consistent with water ice sublimating into the atmosphere.



Phoenix provided direct confirmation of shallow water ice at its high-latitude landing site.



The spacecraft also studied Martian soil chemistry, atmospheric conditions, weather, and interactions between the surface and atmosphere.



Among its important chemical discoveries was perchlorate in Martian soil. Perchlorates are highly oxidized chlorine compounds that influence soil chemistry and the stability of liquid water.



They are also important for future human exploration because they could affect resource extraction, agriculture, human health, and planetary-protection strategies.



\## Curiosity and Gale Crater



NASA's Curiosity rover landed inside Gale crater in August 2012 as part of the Mars Science Laboratory mission.



Curiosity is significantly larger and more scientifically capable than the earlier Spirit and Opportunity rovers. It uses a radioisotope power system rather than relying on solar panels.



The rover's primary objective is not to directly search for living organisms. Instead, it investigates whether ancient Martian environments possessed conditions capable of supporting microbial life.



Gale crater contains Mount Sharp, a layered mountain rising from the crater floor. These sedimentary layers preserve a long geological record of environmental change.



Curiosity discovered ancient stream deposits, lake sediments, clay-bearing rocks, and other evidence showing that Gale crater once contained persistent liquid-water environments.



Measurements showed that ancient lake environments contained several basic chemical ingredients associated with habitability.



The rover has also detected organic molecules preserved in Martian rocks. Organic compounds contain carbon but are not, by themselves, evidence of biological activity. They can be produced through both biological and non-biological processes.



Curiosity continues studying how Mars transitioned from relatively wet ancient conditions toward the colder and drier environment observed today.



\## Perseverance and Jezero Crater



NASA's Perseverance rover landed in Jezero crater on February 18, 2021.



Jezero was selected because orbital observations showed that it once contained a lake and an ancient river delta.



River deltas on Earth efficiently accumulate and preserve sediments, making them valuable environments for studying past conditions and searching for possible biosignatures.



Perseverance investigates the geology of Jezero crater, the history of its ancient lake system, and the potential habitability of different environments.



Its instruments analyze rock textures, mineral composition, chemistry, organic compounds, and geological relationships at high resolution.



One of Perseverance's most important capabilities is collecting carefully selected rock and regolith samples into sealed tubes. These samples are intended to preserve scientifically valuable Martian material for potential future retrieval and detailed laboratory analysis.



Earth laboratories can perform measurements far beyond the capabilities of instruments that can currently be carried by a rover. Returned samples could therefore provide exceptionally detailed information about Martian chronology, geochemistry, climate history, and possible biosignatures.



Sample collection does not mean that evidence of life has already been confirmed. The material is being selected because of its geological and astrobiological value.



\## Ingenuity and Powered Flight on Mars



Perseverance carried a small helicopter called Ingenuity to Mars.



Ingenuity was originally designed primarily as a technology demonstration to determine whether controlled powered flight was possible in the extremely thin Martian atmosphere.



On April 19, 2021, it completed the first powered, controlled flight by an aircraft on another planet.



Flying on Mars is difficult because atmospheric density near the surface is less than one percent of Earth's typical sea-level atmospheric density. Rotor blades must therefore rotate rapidly and be designed to generate sufficient lift in very thin air.



Ingenuity greatly exceeded its original demonstration objectives and completed dozens of flights.



It eventually became an operational scout, helping demonstrate how aerial vehicles could complement surface rovers by observing terrain from above.



Its mission ended after damage to its rotor blades prevented further flight.



The success of Ingenuity established aerial exploration as a practical technique for future Mars missions and potentially for exploration of other planetary environments with atmospheres.



\## Mars Atmosphere and MAVEN



NASA's MAVEN spacecraft entered Mars orbit in 2014 with a mission focused on the upper atmosphere and its interaction with the Sun.



MAVEN stands for Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution.



Modern Mars has a thin atmosphere dominated by carbon dioxide, but geological evidence indicates that ancient Mars supported much more surface water. Maintaining stable surface liquid water generally requires environmental conditions different from those present today.



MAVEN investigates how atmospheric gases escape into space and how the solar wind and ultraviolet radiation influence this process.



Mars lacks a strong global magnetic field, allowing the \[\[Solar Wind]] to interact much more directly with its upper atmosphere than occurs on modern Earth.



Atmospheric particles can become ionized, accelerated, and removed through several mechanisms associated with \[\[Atmospheric Escape]].



MAVEN measurements support the conclusion that loss to space contributed significantly to the transformation of the Martian atmosphere and climate.



However, atmospheric escape is not the only process involved. Carbon dioxide and water can also become stored in surface minerals, polar deposits, or subsurface reservoirs.



Understanding ancient Mars therefore requires combining atmospheric escape measurements with geology, geochemistry, climate modeling, and studies of internal evolution.



\## International Mars Exploration



Mars exploration is an international scientific effort involving missions from multiple countries and space agencies.



The European Space Agency's Mars Express has operated around Mars since 2003 and has contributed extensively to atmospheric, mineralogical, geological, and subsurface studies.



ESA's ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter entered Mars orbit in 2016. It investigates atmospheric trace gases, including methane, and also provides communication relay capabilities for surface missions.



India's Mars Orbiter Mission, also called Mangalyaan, entered Mars orbit in 2014. Operated by the Indian Space Research Organisation, it demonstrated India's ability to conduct interplanetary navigation and Mars-orbit operations while collecting scientific observations of the planet.



The United Arab Emirates' Emirates Mars Mission, using the Hope probe, entered Mars orbit in 2021. It studies the Martian atmosphere and weather on global and regional scales.



China's Tianwen-1 mission entered Mars orbit in 2021 and deployed the Zhurong rover to the surface. The mission combined orbital and surface investigation and made China another nation to successfully operate a spacecraft on Mars.



These missions demonstrate how Mars has become a global target for planetary science and engineering.



\## Water, Climate and Habitability



The history of \[\[Water in the Solar System|water on Mars]] is one of the central themes connecting nearly every generation of Mars exploration.



Orbital images reveal ancient valley networks, channels, deltas, lake basins, and sedimentary deposits. Surface missions have identified minerals that formed or were altered in the presence of water.



Large quantities of water also survive today as ice in the polar caps and subsurface, particularly at higher latitudes.



Ancient Mars therefore experienced environmental conditions very different from those observed today.



An important scientific question is how long liquid-water environments persisted. Short-lived floods, groundwater systems, hydrothermal environments, long-lived lakes, and changing climates may all have existed at different locations and times.



Habitability is not equivalent to inhabited. A habitable environment possesses physical and chemical conditions that could potentially support life as understood from Earth. Demonstrating that such conditions existed does not establish that organisms actually lived there.



Mars exploration therefore searches for potential biosignatures while also studying non-biological processes capable of producing similar chemical or geological patterns.



\## Mars Interior and InSight



NASA's InSight lander reached Mars in 2018 to investigate the planet's deep interior.



Unlike rovers designed primarily for surface geology, InSight focused on geophysics.



Its seismometer detected marsquakes and allowed scientists to investigate the internal structure of the planet using seismic waves.



These measurements provided constraints on the thickness and structure of the Martian crust, the properties of the mantle, and the size and state of the core.



Understanding the interior is essential for reconstructing \[\[Planetary Evolution]]. Internal heat drives volcanism, tectonic activity, mantle convection, and magnetic-field generation.



Mars once possessed a global magnetic dynamo, but that dynamo shut down early in planetary history. Studying the core and mantle helps researchers investigate why.



InSight also monitored weather and other environmental conditions at its landing site.



The mission ended after accumulating dust on its solar panels reduced available electrical power, but its seismic dataset continues to influence models of terrestrial-planet interiors.



\## Phobos and Deimos



Mars exploration also includes its two small moons, \[\[Phobos]] and \[\[Deimos]].



Both are irregular, dark objects whose origins remain uncertain.



One hypothesis proposes that they are captured asteroid-like bodies. Another suggests they formed from debris produced by a large impact on early Mars.



Their nearly circular, equatorial orbits create challenges for simple capture scenarios, making impact-generated formation models particularly important to investigate.



Numerous Mars orbiters have photographed the moons, especially Phobos.



Future exploration and sample-return missions can help determine their composition and origin.



Phobos is especially valuable because its surface may contain material ejected from Mars by impacts. Samples from the moon could therefore contain information about both Phobos and the history of Mars.



Understanding these moons also contributes to reconstructing the long-term \[\[Orbital Dynamics]] of the Martian system.



\## Challenges of Landing and Operating on Mars



Mars is difficult to reach and even more difficult to land on successfully.



Its atmosphere creates a challenging engineering problem. It is thick enough to generate intense heating during atmospheric entry but too thin for parachutes alone to slow heavy spacecraft sufficiently for a safe landing.



Mars missions therefore combine several technologies, including heat shields, supersonic parachutes, retrorockets, airbags, or powered descent systems.



Spirit and Opportunity used airbags during their final landing stages.



Curiosity and Perseverance used a more complex sky-crane system. A rocket-powered descent stage lowered each rover on cables before flying away to crash at a safe distance.



Mars also presents operational challenges after landing. Dust can cover solar panels, temperatures can become extremely low, communication with Earth is delayed, and terrain can damage wheels or trap vehicles.



Planet-wide dust storms can dramatically reduce sunlight available to solar-powered spacecraft.



Mission systems must therefore operate with significant autonomy and survive environments that cannot be repaired directly by engineers on Earth.



\## Future Exploration and Human Missions



Future Mars exploration is expected to combine increasingly capable robotic science with technologies relevant to eventual human missions.



Returning carefully selected Martian samples to Earth remains a major scientific objective, although mission architectures, schedules, and implementation plans can change as agencies reassess cost, risk, and technology.



Future robotic missions may investigate subsurface ice, drill beneath radiation-processed surface material, explore caves or lava tubes, perform improved geophysical measurements, and search for preserved biosignatures.



Human exploration would create much greater engineering challenges.



Astronauts traveling to Mars would face long-duration exposure to cosmic radiation and \[\[Solar Flares|solar energetic particle events]], reduced gravity, isolation, communication delays, limited medical support, and the need for highly reliable life-support systems.



Landing heavy human-rated spacecraft would also be substantially more difficult than landing current robotic vehicles.



Local resources could become important. Martian atmospheric carbon dioxide and water ice might potentially be used to produce oxygen, water, fuel, or other materials through in-situ resource utilization.



Perseverance carried the MOXIE technology demonstration, which successfully produced oxygen from carbon dioxide in the Martian atmosphere. This demonstrated an important principle relevant to future resource utilization.



Human exploration must also address \[\[Planetary Protection]]. Mars is scientifically valuable partly because of the possibility that it once supported habitable environments. Preventing terrestrial contamination is therefore important both for scientific integrity and responsible exploration.



\## Scientific Importance and Open Questions



Mars provides one of the best opportunities to study the long-term evolution of a terrestrial planet other than Earth.



Its ancient surface preserves geological evidence that has been erased on much of Earth by plate tectonics, erosion, and continuous recycling of the crust.



Mars allows scientists to investigate how volcanism, impacts, atmospheric loss, water, climate change, and internal cooling interact over billions of years.



Several misconceptions should be avoided. No Mars mission has confirmed the discovery of extraterrestrial life. Organic molecules detected by rovers are scientifically important but are not automatically biological.



Mars also was not necessarily covered by a single global ocean throughout its early history. Evidence strongly supports widespread ancient water, including rivers, lakes, groundwater and possibly seas, but the timing and extent of these environments remain active research topics.



Present Mars is not completely devoid of water. Large quantities exist as ice, although stable liquid water is difficult to maintain on the modern surface because of low atmospheric pressure and temperature.



Major unanswered questions include whether life ever originated on Mars, how long habitable environments persisted, exactly how much water the planet once possessed, and where much of that water ultimately went.



Scientists also seek to determine when and why Mars lost its global magnetic field, how its interior evolved, whether significant groundwater or ice reservoirs remain at depth, and how frequently volcanism continued into relatively recent geological time.



These questions explain why Mars remains one of the highest-priority targets in \[\[Space Exploration]].



\## Summary



Mars exploration has progressed from early flybys to orbiters, landers, long-lived rovers, helicopters, atmospheric missions, and geophysical investigations.



Mariner missions provided the first successful close observations and global orbital reconnaissance. Viking successfully landed and performed pioneering surface and biology-related experiments.



Later missions established strong evidence that ancient Mars contained rivers, lakes, groundwater, and other water-rich environments.



Spirit and Opportunity performed extensive field geology, Phoenix directly investigated shallow water ice, Curiosity established the past habitability of environments in Gale crater, and Perseverance is exploring the ancient lake and delta system of Jezero crater while collecting scientifically valuable samples.



Ingenuity demonstrated powered flight on another planet.



MAVEN investigates \[\[Atmospheric Escape]], while InSight used marsquakes to reveal the planet's internal structure.



International missions from Europe, India, the United Arab Emirates, China, and the United States have made Mars exploration an increasingly global scientific effort.



Together, these missions are reconstructing how \[\[Mars]] evolved from an early world containing substantial liquid water into the cold, dry planet observed today.



The central unanswered question remains whether any of those ancient habitable environments ever actually supported life.
