\# Planetary Differentiation



\## Overview



Planetary differentiation is the process by which a planetary body separates internally into layers with different compositions and densities. Instead of remaining as a uniform mixture of rock, metal, and other materials, a sufficiently heated planet or moon can reorganize so that denser substances move inward while lighter materials rise toward the surface.



This process is fundamental to the internal structure of many planets, moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, and early planetesimals.



Differentiation is closely related to \[\[Planet Formation]], \[\[Planetary Interiors]], \[\[Planetary Evolution]], \[\[Planetary Volcanism]], and \[\[Planetary Magnetism]].



\## Basic Idea



A newly formed planetary body can initially contain a mixed combination of metal, silicate rock, volatile materials, and other substances.



If enough of the interior melts, materials can move according to density.



Dense metallic material, especially iron-rich material, tends to move toward the center. Less dense silicate material remains above it, while the lightest materials can contribute to crusts, atmospheres, oceans, or icy layers.



The result can be a layered planetary interior.



\## Simplified Differentiation Process



A planetary body accretes from smaller material.



Impacts, gravitational compression, and radioactive decay generate heat.



Parts of the interior melt.



Dense metallic material sinks inward.



Less dense silicate material rises relative to the metal.



A metallic core forms.



A rocky mantle develops around the core.



Further melting and cooling can produce a crust.



This process may continue and evolve for billions of years.



\## Why Differentiation Requires Heat



Solid materials cannot easily separate on a planetary scale if everything remains completely rigid.



Differentiation becomes much more efficient when a body is partially or completely molten.



Heating allows metal and silicate to flow relative to one another.



For this reason, understanding a body's heat sources is essential for understanding whether it differentiated.



\## Major Sources of Internal Heat



Several processes can heat a young planetary body.



Accretion converts the kinetic energy of impacts into heat. Large impacts can locally or globally melt rock.



Gravitational compression also generates heat as material accumulates and becomes compressed under increasing pressure.



Radioactive decay supplies additional heat, especially from isotopes present during early Solar System history.



Core formation itself can also release gravitational energy as dense material sinks toward the center.



\## Short-Lived Radioactive Isotopes



Short-lived radioactive isotopes were particularly important during the earliest stages of Solar System evolution.



One of the most important was:



Aluminum-26



Because it decayed rapidly, bodies that formed early could contain enough aluminum-26 to generate substantial internal heat.



Some relatively small planetesimals became hot enough to melt and differentiate.



\## Long-Lived Radioactive Isotopes



Long-lived radioactive elements continue producing internal heat over billions of years.



Important examples include isotopes of:



Uranium



Thorium



Potassium



These are especially important for the long-term thermal evolution of rocky planets.



\## Metal-Silicate Separation



One of the most important stages of differentiation is the separation of metallic material from silicate rock.



Iron-rich metal is denser than most silicate minerals.



When melting occurs, metal can migrate downward through the interior.



This process eventually forms a metallic core.



\## Core Formation



Core formation is the concentration of dense metallic material toward a planetary body's center.



Planetary cores commonly contain large amounts of:



Iron



Nickel



They can also contain lighter elements such as sulfur, silicon, oxygen, carbon, or hydrogen depending on the body and its formation conditions.



\## Mantle Formation



After much of the dense metal separates into the core, the remaining silicate-rich material forms much of the:



Mantle



The mantle can remain solid overall while still flowing extremely slowly over geological timescales.



Partial melting of the mantle can later generate magma.



\## Crust Formation



The crust is the outermost solid rocky layer of a differentiated terrestrial body.



Crust can form when partial melting produces magma that rises toward the surface and cools.



Different planetary bodies can form crust through different processes.



For example, Earth's modern crust is strongly influenced by plate tectonics, while the Moon's ancient crust was strongly shaped by magma-ocean crystallization.



\## Magma Oceans



Large impacts and intense early heating can melt a substantial fraction of a planet or moon.



A global or nearly global molten layer is called a:



Magma ocean



Magma oceans may have been common during the early evolution of terrestrial planets and large moons.



\## Magma-Ocean Differentiation



As a magma ocean cools, minerals crystallize at different temperatures.



Some crystals are denser than the remaining melt and sink.



Others can be less dense and rise.



This creates additional chemical layering inside the body.



The Moon provides one of the clearest examples of this process.



\## Lunar Differentiation



The early \[\[Moon]] likely experienced a major magma-ocean stage.



As the lunar magma ocean cooled, dense minerals tended to sink into the mantle while relatively light plagioclase feldspar crystals floated upward.



These floating crystals contributed to formation of the Moon's ancient anorthositic highland crust.



\## Lunar Anorthosite



Anorthosite is a rock rich in plagioclase feldspar.



Its abundance in the lunar highlands is important evidence supporting extensive early lunar differentiation and magma-ocean crystallization.



\## KREEP



During lunar magma-ocean crystallization, some elements did not easily enter early-forming minerals.



They became concentrated in the final residual melt.



This material became enriched in potassium, rare earth elements, and phosphorus and is known as:



KREEP



KREEP is important for understanding lunar chemical differentiation and heat production.



\## Earth Differentiation



\[\[Earth]] is strongly differentiated.



Its major internal structure includes:



Crust



Mantle



Outer core



Inner core



The core is rich in iron, while the mantle and crust are dominated by silicate minerals.



Earth's differentiation began very early during planetary formation.



\## Earth's Core Formation



As the early Earth grew, impacts and internal heating caused extensive melting.



Dense metallic iron separated from silicate material and moved toward the center.



This process created Earth's metallic core.



Core formation likely occurred while Earth was still accreting.



\## Earth's Mantle



Earth's mantle is dominated by silicate minerals rich in elements such as:



Magnesium



Iron



Silicon



Oxygen



It accounts for most of Earth's volume.



Although mostly solid, it flows slowly over long timescales.



\## Earth's Crust



Earth has two major modern crust types:



Oceanic crust



Continental crust



Their compositions and histories differ because Earth's active plate tectonics continuously produces and recycles crust.



\## Inner and Outer Core



Earth's core is itself differentiated.



The outer core is liquid.



The inner core is solid.



The difference results mainly from changing pressure and temperature conditions with depth.



\## Core and Magnetic Field



Earth's liquid outer core contains electrically conducting material.



Convection and rotation help generate Earth's global magnetic field through the:



Geodynamo



This connects planetary differentiation with \[\[Planetary Magnetism]].



\## Mercury



\[\[Mercury]] is highly differentiated and has an unusually large metallic core relative to its size.



Its core occupies a much larger fraction of the planet than Earth's core does.



The reason for Mercury's high metal fraction remains debated.



Possible explanations include its original building materials, high-temperature processes, or removal of silicate material during early evolution.



\## Mercury's Crust and Mantle



Above Mercury's large core lies a comparatively thinner silicate mantle and crust.



Data from spacecraft have shown that Mercury's surface composition is more complex than older simple models predicted.



\## Venus



\[\[Venus]] is expected to be differentiated into a metallic core, rocky mantle, and crust.



Its size and bulk density are similar to Earth, suggesting broadly similar internal layering.



However, the detailed state of its core and mantle is less well constrained because direct seismic measurements are unavailable.



\## Mars



\[\[Mars]] is also differentiated into crust, mantle, and core.



Spacecraft observations, gravity measurements, and seismic data have greatly improved knowledge of its internal structure.



\## InSight and Mars Interior



NASA's InSight mission placed a seismometer on Mars.



Marsquake measurements revealed important information about the thickness of the crust, the structure of the mantle, and the size and properties of the core.



This provided direct evidence of Mars's differentiated internal structure.



\## Mars Core



Mars has a relatively large core containing iron and substantial quantities of lighter elements.



Its composition affects the planet's density and thermal evolution.



\## Mars Crustal Dichotomy



Mars has a major difference between its northern and southern hemispheres.



The southern highlands are generally older and more elevated, while the northern lowlands are smoother and lower.



This crustal dichotomy is a major feature of Martian geology, although its exact origin remains debated.



\## Giant Planets



Differentiation also occurs in \[\[Giant Planets]], although their structures differ greatly from rocky planets.



Jupiter and Saturn contain large amounts of hydrogen and helium and may not have simple sharply separated layers everywhere.



Their interiors include regions with changing pressure, composition, and physical state.



\## Jupiter



\[\[Jupiter]] likely contains a central region enriched in heavy elements.



Modern models suggest that this region may be partially mixed with surrounding material rather than forming a perfectly compact solid core.



This is sometimes described as a:



Dilute core



\## Saturn



\[\[Saturn]] also has a deep interior enriched in heavier elements.



Data from the Cassini mission have provided strong constraints on Saturn's internal mass distribution.



Its structure likely includes a heavy-element-rich central region surrounded by metallic and molecular hydrogen-rich layers.



\## Hydrogen Differentiation in Giant Planets



Under extreme pressure inside Jupiter and Saturn, hydrogen behaves very differently from ordinary surface conditions.



At sufficient pressure it becomes electrically conducting:



Metallic hydrogen



This creates an important internal transition.



\## Helium Rain



Inside Saturn, and possibly Jupiter, helium can become partially separated from hydrogen under certain pressure and temperature conditions.



Helium droplets can sink deeper into the planet.



This process is often called:



Helium rain



It represents a form of ongoing compositional differentiation.



\## Helium Rain and Heat



As helium droplets sink, gravitational energy is released.



This can contribute to the internal heat emitted by giant planets, especially Saturn.



\## Ice Giants



\[\[Uranus]] and \[\[Neptune]] are commonly described as ice giants.



Their interiors contain large amounts of materials such as:



Water



Ammonia



Methane-related compounds



along with rock and hydrogen-helium envelopes.



Their internal layering may be complex.



\## Uranus



Uranus appears to emit relatively little internal heat compared with Neptune.



One possible explanation involves inhibited convection caused by stable compositional layering.



This would mean differentiation influences how efficiently heat escapes from the planet.



\## Neptune



Neptune emits substantial internal heat.



Its interior probably contains a mixture of rocky material, volatile-rich material, and hydrogen-helium.



The exact degree of layering and mixing remains uncertain.



\## Dwarf Planets



Some \[\[Dwarf Planets]] are also differentiated.



Whether differentiation occurs depends on factors including:



Size



Composition



Formation time



Radioactive heating



Impact history



\## Pluto



\[\[Pluto]] is believed to be differentiated into a rocky interior and an ice-rich outer region.



Its geology suggests a complex thermal history.



Some models also allow for a subsurface ocean.



\## Ceres



\[\[Ceres]] may have undergone partial differentiation.



Its interior appears to contain significant water-rich material and rock.



Rather than forming a simple fully separated rocky core and icy mantle, its internal structure may be more complex.



\## Vesta



The asteroid Vesta is one of the clearest examples of differentiation in a relatively small body.



Vesta likely developed a:



Metallic core



Rocky mantle



Basaltic crust



very early in Solar System history.



\## Vesta and Meteorites



A group of meteorites known as:



HED meteorites



is strongly linked to Vesta.



HED stands for:



Howardite



Eucrite



Diogenite



These meteorites provide direct laboratory evidence for differentiation on a small protoplanet.



\## Iron Meteorites



Many iron meteorites are believed to come from the metallic cores of differentiated planetesimals that were later broken apart by collisions.



They show that core formation occurred in numerous early Solar System bodies.



\## Achondrites



Achondrite meteorites have experienced melting and differentiation.



Unlike primitive chondrites, they no longer preserve the original unmelted texture of early Solar System material.



Some represent crustal or mantle material from differentiated parent bodies.



\## Primitive Bodies



Not every small body differentiated.



Some asteroids and cometary bodies remained relatively primitive because they:



Formed later



Were too small



Contained insufficient radioactive heat



Never experienced enough melting



These objects preserve more original Solar System material.



\## Chondrites



Chondritic meteorites are relatively primitive materials that did not undergo complete planetary-scale melting and differentiation.



Their bulk compositions are often used as references for estimating the original composition of planetary building blocks.



\## Differentiation and Density



Bulk density provides clues about composition and differentiation.



A body containing a large metallic core may have greater density than one dominated by rock or ice.



However, density alone cannot uniquely determine internal structure because porosity and composition also matter.



\## Gravitational Energy



Differentiation itself releases energy.



When dense material moves downward through a gravitational field, gravitational potential energy is converted into heat.



This means differentiation can reinforce further melting.



\## Positive Feedback



A simplified feedback process is:



Interior begins to melt



↓



Dense metal sinks



↓



Gravitational energy is released



↓



Additional heat is generated



↓



More material can melt and separate



This can accelerate early core formation.



\## Metal Percolation



Molten metal may move through partially molten silicate in interconnected pathways.



This process is sometimes described as:



Percolation



The efficiency depends on melt geometry and physical properties.



\## Diapirism



Dense blobs of molten metal can sink through a silicate mantle as:



Diapirs



This is another possible mechanism for core formation.



\## Giant Impact Mixing



Large impacts can temporarily disrupt existing planetary layering.



They can:



Melt the mantle



Mix materials



Strip crust or mantle



Combine the cores of colliding bodies



Differentiation can then continue after the collision.



\## Moon-Forming Impact



The giant impact associated with formation of Earth's Moon likely caused enormous amounts of melting and mixing.



This event may have strongly affected both Earth's and the Moon's subsequent differentiation.



\## Mantle Differentiation



Differentiation is not limited to separating core from mantle.



Mantles themselves can develop chemically distinct regions through:



Partial melting



Crystal settling



Magma-ocean crystallization



Chemical reactions



\## Partial Melting



When mantle rock partially melts, not all minerals melt equally.



The resulting magma often has a different composition from the solid material left behind.



This process is called:



Partial melting



It is one of the major mechanisms for chemical differentiation inside rocky planets.



\## Melt Extraction



Magma produced by partial melting can rise because it is often less dense than surrounding solid rock.



If it reaches the surface, it can create volcanic crust.



If it stalls underground, it can form intrusive rock.



\## Residual Mantle



After magma is removed, the remaining mantle becomes chemically different from its starting composition.



Repeated melting can therefore create increasingly differentiated planetary interiors.



\## Crustal Differentiation



Crust can also undergo further differentiation.



On Earth, repeated melting, crystallization, erosion, sedimentation, and plate tectonics have produced highly diverse crustal compositions.



Other rocky worlds show simpler but still important crustal evolution.



\## Fractional Crystallization



When magma cools, different minerals crystallize at different temperatures.



If early crystals become physically separated from the remaining liquid, the composition of the melt changes.



This process is called:



Fractional crystallization



\## Fractional Crystallization and Magma Oceans



Fractional crystallization is particularly important in models of magma oceans.



It can produce strong compositional stratification.



Later overturn or mixing may modify that structure.



\## Cumulate Layers



Crystals accumulating during magma crystallization form layers called:



Cumulates



Magma-ocean crystallization can therefore create:



Cumulate mantle layers



\## Gravitational Overturn



Sometimes crystallization creates an unstable arrangement where dense material lies above lighter material.



The interior may then reorganize through:



Gravitational overturn



Dense material sinks while lighter material rises.



This can produce major internal mixing and heating.



\## Lunar Mantle Overturn



Some models of lunar evolution propose that late-stage dense magma-ocean minerals formed near the surface and later sank into the mantle.



This process may have influenced:



Lunar volcanism



KREEP distribution



Mantle composition



\## Chemical Differentiation



Planetary differentiation is fundamentally chemical as well as physical.



Different elements prefer different phases.



Some concentrate in metal.



Others prefer silicate.



Others concentrate in melts or gases.



\## Siderophile Elements



Elements that preferentially dissolve into metallic iron are called:



Siderophile elements



The word means:



Iron-loving



Examples include several elements in the platinum group.



\## Lithophile Elements



Elements that preferentially associate with silicate minerals are called:



Lithophile elements



The word means:



Rock-loving



Examples include:



Aluminum



Calcium



Rare earth elements



\## Chalcophile Elements



Elements that preferentially associate with sulfur-bearing phases are called:



Chalcophile elements



The word means:



Sulfur-loving



\## Atmophile Elements



Elements that preferentially occur in gases or volatile phases are called:



Atmophile elements



Examples include several volatile elements and noble gases.



\## Geochemical Classification



The distribution of siderophile, lithophile, chalcophile, and atmophile elements helps scientists reconstruct:



Core formation



Mantle composition



Volatile history



Planetary formation conditions



\## Highly Siderophile Elements



Highly siderophile elements should strongly prefer metallic cores.



Yet small quantities remain in Earth's mantle.



This has been used to investigate core formation and possible later delivery of material after core segregation.



\## Late Veneer



One hypothesis proposes that some highly siderophile elements were added to Earth's mantle after most core formation had ended.



This additional material is called a:



Late veneer



Its exact nature and timing remain under study.



\## Isotope Fractionation



Differentiation can produce differences in isotope ratios between planetary reservoirs.



Isotope measurements help scientists study:



Core formation



Crust formation



Mantle evolution



Volatile loss



\## Hafnium-Tungsten System



The radioactive isotope system involving:



Hafnium-182



and:



Tungsten-182



is particularly important for dating early core formation.



Hafnium prefers silicate, while tungsten is more siderophile.



Their isotopic relationships provide information about when metal separated from silicate.



\## Core Formation Timescales



Isotope studies suggest that core formation began very early in many planetary bodies.



Small differentiated planetesimals may have formed metallic cores within only a few million years of the earliest Solar System solids.



Large planets continued accreting and differentiating over longer periods.



\## Differentiation and Atmospheres



Interior differentiation also influences atmospheric formation.



Melting allows volatile compounds dissolved in rock or magma to escape through:



Outgassing



Volcanism



These gases can contribute to secondary atmospheres.



\## Outgassing



Gases released from a planetary interior can include:



Water vapor



Carbon dioxide



Nitrogen-bearing species



Sulfur gases



Other volatile compounds



Their exact abundances depend on composition and oxidation state.



\## Early Atmospheres



Rocky planets may initially acquire hydrogen-rich gas from a protoplanetary disk.



Later, differentiation and volcanism can create secondary atmospheres.



Atmospheric loss and impacts can then modify them further.



\## Ocean Formation



On planets where water vapor cools and condenses, interior outgassing can contribute to ocean formation.



Additional water can also be delivered by impacts.



The relative importance of these sources varies between planetary bodies.



\## Differentiation and Volcanism



Differentiation creates chemically distinct mantle reservoirs.



Partial melting of these reservoirs produces magma.



Therefore \[\[Planetary Volcanism]] is deeply connected to internal differentiation.



\## Basaltic Crust



Basalt is a common volcanic rock produced from partial melting of planetary mantles.



Basaltic surfaces occur on:



Earth



Moon



Mars



Venus



Mercury



Vesta



This reflects widespread silicate differentiation.



\## Differentiation and Tectonics



The thermal structure created during differentiation influences later tectonic evolution.



Bodies retaining more internal heat can sustain:



Mantle convection



Volcanism



Crustal deformation



Possibly plate tectonics under suitable conditions



\## Differentiation and Magnetic Fields



A planetary dynamo generally requires electrically conducting material in motion.



Core formation concentrates iron and other conductive materials at the center.



If part of the core remains liquid and convecting, a magnetic dynamo may operate.



Differentiation is therefore a prerequisite for many planetary magnetic fields.



\## Mercury's Magnetic Field



Mercury's large metallic core remains partially liquid.



Its active dynamo generates a global magnetic field.



This shows that a small planet can preserve core activity over billions of years.



\## Mars's Lost Global Dynamo



Mars once possessed a global magnetic field.



Magnetized ancient crust preserves evidence of this early dynamo.



The global dynamo later ceased, although the core remains important to Mars's present interior evolution.



\## Moon's Ancient Dynamo



Lunar rocks preserve evidence that the Moon once had a stronger magnetic field.



The exact mechanism and duration of this ancient lunar dynamo remain subjects of research.



\## Differentiation and Habitability



Differentiation can influence \[\[Planetary Habitability]] by controlling:



Core formation



Magnetic field generation



Volcanism



Atmospheric outgassing



Plate tectonics



Nutrient cycling



Surface composition



However, differentiation alone does not make a planet habitable.



\## Interior-Surface Connection



A planet's interior evolution affects its surface environment.



For example, mantle melting can produce volcanic gases, while core evolution can influence magnetic shielding.



Thus planetary habitability depends partly on processes occurring thousands of kilometers below the surface.



\## Ocean Worlds



Differentiation also occurs in icy moons.



An icy satellite can separate into:



Rocky core



Water-rich mantle



Ice shell



Subsurface ocean



depending on temperature and composition.



\## Europa



\[\[Europa]] is believed to be differentiated.



It likely contains a rocky interior surrounded by a deep water layer.



The outer part of that water layer is frozen into an ice shell, while liquid water probably exists beneath.



\## Ganymede



\[\[Ganymede]] is strongly differentiated.



It likely contains:



Metallic core



Rocky mantle



Multiple high-pressure ice layers



Subsurface liquid-water layers



Its metallic core powers an intrinsic magnetic field.



\## Callisto



\[\[Callisto]] appears to be less completely differentiated than Ganymede.



Its interior may contain a mixture of rock and ice with only partial separation.



This difference provides clues about the formation conditions of Jupiter's moons.



\## Enceladus



\[\[Enceladus]] has a rocky core surrounded by a water-rich region and icy shell.



Its internal differentiation is connected to its subsurface ocean and active plume system.



\## Titan



\[\[Titan]] likely contains a differentiated interior with a rock-rich central region and multiple ice or water-rich layers.



Its exact internal structure remains uncertain.



\## Differentiation in Small Icy Bodies



Even relatively small icy bodies can differentiate because water ice melts at much lower temperatures than silicate rock.



Radioactive heating and tidal heating can therefore allow rock and ice to separate.



\## Tidal Heating



Tidal deformation can provide enough heat to maintain internal melting in moons such as:



Io



Europa



Enceladus



This can drive ongoing differentiation or remobilization of interior materials.



\## Io



\[\[Io]] is extremely volcanically active because of intense tidal heating.



Although already differentiated, continuous heating causes extensive mantle melting and rapid surface recycling.



\## Pressure-Induced Differentiation



At enormous planetary pressures, materials can enter new physical states.



Inside giant planets and large icy bodies, pressure-driven phase changes can produce additional layering.



\## High-Pressure Ice



Deep inside large icy moons, water can form high-pressure crystalline phases of ice.



These phases can exist beneath subsurface oceans.



This creates complex internal structures not found in ordinary surface ice.



\## Diamond Formation Hypotheses



Inside ice giants such as Neptune and Uranus, very high pressure may break down methane-rich compounds.



Some models and experiments suggest carbon could form diamond-like structures and sink deeper.



The importance and scale of this process inside the actual planets remain uncertain.



\## Differentiation and Planetary Density



Differentiation changes where mass is concentrated inside a body but does not necessarily change total mass.



A planet with a dense core has more mass concentrated toward its center.



This affects:



Moment of inertia



Gravity field



Rotation



Tidal response



\## Moment of Inertia



A body's moment of inertia describes how its mass is distributed relative to its rotation axis.



A strongly differentiated body generally has more mass concentrated toward its center.



Measurements of moment of inertia can therefore reveal information about internal layering.



\## Gravity Measurements



Spacecraft can detect tiny variations in gravity.



These measurements help determine:



Core size



Crust thickness



Mass concentrations



Internal density distribution



\## Seismology



Seismic waves travel differently through materials of different:



Density



Composition



Temperature



Physical state



Seismology is therefore one of the most powerful methods for detecting planetary differentiation.



\## Earth Seismology



Earthquakes revealed Earth's:



Crust



Mantle



Liquid outer core



Solid inner core



This remains the most detailed example of planetary interior investigation.



\## Lunar Seismology



Apollo seismometers revealed that the Moon has distinct internal layers.



Future lunar seismic networks could greatly improve understanding of lunar differentiation.



\## Mars Seismology



The InSight mission demonstrated that seismic techniques can successfully study another planet's interior directly.



Its measurements significantly improved models of Mars's crust, mantle, and core.



\## Magnetic Measurements



A global magnetic field can indicate the presence of:



Conductive fluid



Internal motion



Core differentiation



However, the absence of a magnetic field does not mean a body lacks a metallic core.



\## Gravity and Rotation



Measurements of:



Gravity



Rotation



Precession



Tidal deformation



can be combined to constrain interior structure.



\## Spacecraft Tracking



Radio signals from spacecraft can be used to measure tiny changes in spacecraft velocity.



These reveal variations in a planet or moon's gravitational field.



\## Common Misconceptions



\### Differentiation Means a Planet Splits Apart



Incorrect. Differentiation means internal materials separate into layers while the planet remains gravitationally bound.



\### Only Large Planets Differentiate



Incorrect. Even relatively small planetesimals such as Vesta differentiated if they formed early enough and became sufficiently hot.



\### All Asteroids Are Primitive and Undifferentiated



Incorrect. Some asteroids are fragments of differentiated parent bodies.



\### Differentiation Happens Only Once



Incorrect. Early core formation may be followed by mantle melting, crust formation, compositional overturn, volcanism, and continued chemical separation.



\### Every Differentiated Planet Has an Active Magnetic Field



Incorrect. A metallic core alone is insufficient. A dynamo generally also requires appropriate fluid motion and energy sources.



\### Earth's Layers Formed Only Because Heavy Elements Were Pulled Down by Gravity



Gravity is central, but large-scale separation required heating, melting, chemical partitioning, and physical transport of materials.



\### All Planetary Cores Are Pure Iron



Incorrect. Cores can contain nickel and lighter elements, and giant planets have more complex central compositions.



\### An Undifferentiated Body Has No Internal Structure



Incorrect. A body can have porosity, thermal gradients, fractures, and compositional variations without complete large-scale differentiation.



\## Important Terminology



\*\*Planetary Differentiation:\*\* Separation of a planetary body's interior into compositionally and physically distinct regions.



\*\*Core:\*\* Dense central region, commonly enriched in metal in rocky planets.



\*\*Mantle:\*\* Large layer between core and crust, generally dominated by silicate rock in terrestrial planets.



\*\*Crust:\*\* Outer rocky shell produced through differentiation and geological processing.



\*\*Magma Ocean:\*\* Large-scale layer of molten silicate present during early planetary evolution.



\*\*Partial Melting:\*\* Melting of only part of a rock, producing magma with a different composition from the residue.



\*\*Fractional Crystallization:\*\* Progressive crystallization in which early crystals are separated from remaining melt.



\*\*Siderophile Element:\*\* Element with an affinity for metallic iron.



\*\*Lithophile Element:\*\* Element with an affinity for silicate minerals.



\*\*Chalcophile Element:\*\* Element with an affinity for sulfur-bearing phases.



\*\*Outgassing:\*\* Release of volatile substances from a planetary interior.



\## Simplified Rocky-Planet Differentiation



A growing rocky planet is heated by impacts, radioactive decay, and gravitational energy. Much of the interior melts. Dense iron-rich liquid moves toward the center while lower-density silicate remains above.



The metal forms a core, while silicate material forms the mantle. Partial melting and magma transport then create a crust. Continued cooling, convection, volcanism, impacts, and chemical reactions further modify these layers.



The result is a planet with an interior that is chemically and physically very different from the original mixture of planetary building blocks.



\## Simplified Icy-Moon Differentiation



An icy moon begins as a mixture of rock and ice. Radioactive or tidal heating warms the interior. Ice can melt into liquid water, allowing dense rocky material to sink.



A rocky core develops while water and ice remain above it. Continued heating can preserve a subsurface ocean beneath an outer ice shell.



This general process helps explain the internal structures of moons such as Europa, Ganymede, and Enceladus.



\## Scientific Importance



Planetary differentiation explains why planets and moons have cores, mantles, crusts, oceans, and chemically distinct surface materials.



It provides a direct link between the conditions of \[\[Planet Formation]] and the later geological behavior of a world.



The process influences magnetic fields, volcanism, tectonics, atmospheric formation, thermal evolution, and long-term habitability.



Meteorites show that differentiation began extremely early in Solar System history, while modern spacecraft and seismic measurements reveal that its consequences remain visible billions of years later.



\## Open Questions



Important unanswered questions include exactly how quickly Earth's core formed, how much mixing occurred during giant impacts, and how early magma oceans crystallized on terrestrial planets.



Scientists continue investigating why Mercury contains such a large metal fraction, how completely Venus differentiated, how Mars's core composition affected its lost dynamo, and how lunar mantle overturn influenced later volcanism.



Other questions involve whether giant planets possess compact or diffuse central cores, how much helium rain occurs inside Jupiter and Saturn, whether Uranus contains stable compositional layers that prevent convection, and how strongly differentiation controls the subsurface oceans of icy moons.



\## Summary



Planetary differentiation is the gravitational and chemical separation of planetary material into layers.



It begins when a body becomes sufficiently hot for materials to move relative to one another. Dense metallic material can sink to form a core, while silicate rock forms a mantle and later a crust.



Differentiation can be driven by accretional heating, impacts, radioactive decay, gravitational energy, and tidal heating. It occurred not only in planets but also in moons, dwarf planets, and relatively small early planetesimals.



Earth, Mercury, Venus, Mars, the Moon, Vesta, Ganymede, and many other bodies show evidence of extensive differentiation.



The process determines internal density structure, influences volcanism and tectonics, enables some planetary dynamos, contributes to atmospheric outgassing, and shapes the long-term geological evolution of a world.



Planetary differentiation is therefore one of the fundamental processes connecting \[\[Planet Formation]] to the present-day structure and behavior of planets and moons.
