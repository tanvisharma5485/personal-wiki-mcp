\# Atmospheric Escape



\## Overview



Atmospheric escape is the loss of gas from a planet or moon into space.



An atmosphere is held by gravity, but atmospheric particles are constantly moving. Some particles can gain enough energy to overcome a world's gravitational pull and escape permanently.



Atmospheric escape can strongly change a planet's climate, surface pressure, water inventory, atmospheric chemistry, and long-term \[\[Planetary Habitability]].



It is especially important for smaller worlds such as \[\[Mars]], bodies exposed to strong stellar radiation, and planets orbiting close to their stars.



Atmospheric escape is closely connected with \[\[Planetary Atmospheres]], \[\[Solar Wind]], \[\[Space Weather]], \[\[Planetary Magnetism]], \[\[Water in the Solar System]], and \[\[Planetary Evolution]].



\## Why Atmospheres Escape



The ability of a planet to retain an atmosphere depends mainly on gravity, atmospheric temperature, particle mass, stellar radiation, solar or stellar wind interaction, and time.



A massive planet with strong gravity generally retains gas more easily than a small planet.



Hot atmospheric particles move faster than cold particles.



Light gases such as hydrogen and helium escape more easily than heavier gases such as nitrogen, oxygen, or carbon dioxide.



\## Escape Velocity



Escape velocity is the minimum speed an object needs to move away from a planetary body's gravity without additional propulsion.



A body's escape velocity depends mainly on its mass and radius.



More massive and compact worlds generally have higher escape velocities.



Earth's surface escape velocity is about:



11.2 kilometers per second



Mars has a lower escape velocity of about:



5.0 kilometers per second



The Moon's is only about:



2.4 kilometers per second



These differences help explain why smaller bodies generally have more difficulty retaining thick atmospheres over billions of years.



\## Molecular Speed



Gas molecules do not all move at the same speed.



Their velocities follow a statistical distribution.



At higher temperatures, average molecular speeds increase.



Lighter molecules also move faster than heavier molecules at the same temperature.



This makes hydrogen especially vulnerable to escape.



\## Exosphere



The:



Exosphere



is the uppermost region of an atmosphere.



Here, atmospheric density becomes so low that collisions between particles are rare.



Particles can travel long distances before colliding.



Many atmospheric escape processes begin in or above the exosphere.



\## Exobase



The lower boundary of the exosphere is called the:



Exobase



Below the exobase, collisions are frequent enough to keep gas behaving approximately like a fluid.



Above it, individual particle trajectories become more important.



\## Thermal Escape



Thermal escape occurs when atmospheric particles gain energy from heat.



The most important thermal escape mechanisms include:



Jeans escape



Hydrodynamic escape



These processes differ in strength and physical conditions.



\## Jeans Escape



Jeans escape occurs when particles in the high-speed tail of a gas's velocity distribution exceed escape velocity near the exobase.



Most particles remain gravitationally bound.



A small fraction of especially fast particles escape.



Over very long periods, this gradual loss can become significant.



\## Why Light Gases Escape First



Hydrogen atoms are much lighter than oxygen, nitrogen, or carbon dioxide molecules.



At the same temperature, lighter particles typically move faster.



Therefore hydrogen is much more likely to reach escape velocity.



Helium also escapes relatively easily.



This is why small rocky planets often lose their original hydrogen-helium atmospheres.



\## Hydrodynamic Escape



Hydrodynamic escape is a much stronger form of atmospheric loss.



It occurs when intense high-energy radiation heats the upper atmosphere so strongly that gas expands outward in a bulk flow.



Instead of individual molecules occasionally escaping, large quantities of atmospheric gas can flow away together.



\## Early Planetary Atmospheres



Young stars emit strong:



Ultraviolet radiation



Extreme ultraviolet radiation



X-rays



These can strongly heat upper atmospheres.



Young planets may therefore undergo much stronger atmospheric escape than mature planets.



\## Hydrogen-Rich Atmospheres



Many young planets can initially acquire hydrogen and helium directly from their protoplanetary disk.



These are called:



Primary atmospheres



Small rocky planets often lose much or all of these light gases through atmospheric escape.



\## Primary Versus Secondary Atmospheres



A primary atmosphere is captured mainly from the gas surrounding a young star.



A secondary atmosphere forms later from processes such as:



Volcanic outgassing



Impact delivery



Surface-atmosphere chemistry



Examples of secondary-atmosphere gases can include water vapor, carbon dioxide, nitrogen, and sulfur compounds.



\## Hydrodynamic Blow-Off



Under extreme heating, an atmosphere can expand so strongly that it undergoes:



Hydrodynamic blow-off



Large quantities of gas escape rapidly.



Hydrogen flowing outward can sometimes drag heavier particles with it.



\## Dragging Heavy Species



Strong hydrogen escape can transfer momentum to heavier atoms.



This means hydrodynamic escape can remove more than hydrogen alone.



Elements such as oxygen or other heavier species can sometimes be carried away under sufficiently strong conditions.



\## Diffusion-Limited Escape



Hydrogen escaping from an atmosphere may first need to diffuse through heavier gases.



If diffusion is slower than the actual escape process, the overall loss becomes limited by the rate at which hydrogen reaches the upper atmosphere.



This is called:



Diffusion-limited escape



\## Energy-Limited Escape



For strongly irradiated planets, atmospheric escape is sometimes approximated using an:



Energy-limited model



The basic idea is that a fraction of incoming high-energy stellar radiation provides the energy needed to lift atmospheric gas out of the planet's gravitational field.



It is a simplified model and does not capture every physical process.



\## Non-Thermal Escape



Atmospheres can also escape through processes that do not depend primarily on the ordinary thermal speed of gas particles.



These are called:



Non-thermal escape processes



They include:



Ion escape



Sputtering



Photochemical escape



Charge exchange



Pickup by the solar wind



\## Photoionization



High-energy sunlight can remove electrons from atmospheric atoms or molecules.



This process is called:



Photoionization



The resulting ions respond strongly to electric and magnetic fields.



Some can be accelerated into space.



\## Ion Escape



Charged atmospheric particles can be accelerated along electric or magnetic field structures and escape.



Ion escape is especially important when atmospheric plasma interacts with the \[\[Solar Wind]].



\## Solar Wind



The solar wind is a continuous flow of charged particles released from the \[\[Sun]].



It consists mainly of:



Protons



Electrons



Alpha particles



When the solar wind encounters a planetary atmosphere, it can contribute to atmospheric erosion.



\## Solar Wind Pickup



An atmospheric atom can become ionized.



The new ion is then influenced by the magnetic field carried by the solar wind.



It can be accelerated and carried away.



This process is called:



Ion pickup



or:



Pickup ion escape



\## Sputtering



Sputtering occurs when energetic particles strike the upper atmosphere and transfer energy to atmospheric atoms.



Some of those atoms gain enough energy to escape.



This process can be especially important on worlds without strong global magnetic protection.



\## Photochemical Escape



Ultraviolet light drives chemical reactions in the upper atmosphere.



Some reactions produce fast neutral atoms.



If these particles receive enough kinetic energy, they can escape.



This is called:



Photochemical escape



\## Dissociative Recombination



One important photochemical process occurs when a molecular ion recombines with an electron and breaks apart.



The resulting atoms may carry substantial kinetic energy.



Some can escape from the planet.



This mechanism is important for Mars.



\## Charge Exchange



A fast ion can capture an electron from a neutral atmospheric particle.



The resulting fast neutral atom may no longer be controlled by magnetic fields and can escape.



This process is called:



Charge exchange



\## Polar Wind



On magnetized planets, ions can flow outward along open magnetic field lines near the magnetic poles.



This process is called:



Polar wind



Earth loses atmospheric ions through this mechanism.



\## Magnetosphere and Escape



A global magnetic field changes how a planet interacts with the solar wind.



It can deflect much of the direct solar-wind flow around the atmosphere.



However, magnetic fields do not completely prevent atmospheric escape.



They can also create pathways for ion loss through the magnetotail and polar regions.



\## Magnetic Field Is Not a Perfect Shield



It is incorrect to assume that a magnetic field simply seals an atmosphere inside a planet.



Earth has a strong \[\[Earth's Magnetosphere|magnetosphere]] and still continuously loses atmospheric particles.



The effect of a magnetic field on long-term atmospheric retention depends on complicated interactions among the atmosphere, ionosphere, stellar wind, and magnetosphere.



\## Earth Atmospheric Escape



Earth currently loses atmospheric particles to space.



Most present-day loss is relatively small compared with the total atmosphere.



Hydrogen escapes more readily than heavier gases.



Earth's high gravity, relatively moderate atmospheric temperatures, and large volatile inventory have allowed it to retain a substantial atmosphere over billions of years.



\## Earth's Hydrogen Escape



Water molecules can be broken apart by ultraviolet radiation.



Hydrogen produced through this chemistry can eventually reach the upper atmosphere and escape.



This represents one long-term pathway for water loss.



\## Cold Trap



On modern Earth, much atmospheric water condenses in the lower atmosphere before reaching the upper atmosphere.



This creates an effective:



Cold trap



It limits the amount of water vapor reaching altitudes where ultraviolet radiation can efficiently break it apart.



This helps reduce long-term water loss.



\## Moist Greenhouse



If a planet becomes warm enough, much larger quantities of water vapor can enter its upper atmosphere.



This state is sometimes called a:



Moist greenhouse



More water then becomes vulnerable to photodissociation and hydrogen escape.



\## Photodissociation



Ultraviolet photons can break molecules apart.



Water can be dissociated into hydrogen- and oxygen-bearing products.



The lighter hydrogen can escape more easily.



Over long times, this can contribute to planetary water loss.



\## Oxygen After Water Loss



When water is broken apart and hydrogen escapes, oxygen may remain behind.



The oxygen can:



Accumulate in the atmosphere



React with surface rocks



Escape through other processes



Be incorporated into the interior



Therefore water loss does not automatically produce a permanent oxygen-rich atmosphere.



\## Abiotic Oxygen



Atmospheric oxygen is often associated with biology on Earth.



However, strong atmospheric water loss can produce oxygen without life.



This is called:



Abiotic oxygen



It is an important consideration when searching for biosignatures on \[\[Exoplanets]].



\## Venus Atmospheric Escape



\[\[Venus]] is a major example of long-term atmospheric and water evolution.



Today Venus has a massive carbon dioxide atmosphere but very little water compared with Earth.



Evidence suggests Venus lost large amounts of water during its history.



\## Venus and Hydrogen Escape



Water vapor in the atmosphere can be broken apart by ultraviolet radiation.



Hydrogen then escapes efficiently to space.



Venus's high deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio provides important evidence that substantial hydrogen, and therefore water, was lost in the past.



\## Deuterium



Deuterium is a heavier isotope of hydrogen.



It contains:



One proton



One neutron



Ordinary hydrogen contains only one proton.



Because deuterium is heavier, it escapes less efficiently than ordinary hydrogen.



\## D/H Ratio



The ratio of deuterium to ordinary hydrogen is called the:



D/H ratio



When large amounts of hydrogen escape, the remaining reservoir can become enriched in deuterium.



A high D/H ratio can therefore preserve evidence of past atmospheric escape.



\## Venus Water History



The exact original amount of water on Venus remains uncertain.



Possible histories range from limited early surface water to more substantial oceans under some models.



Atmospheric escape is a major part of reconstructing these scenarios.



\## Mars Atmospheric Escape



\[\[Mars]] once had a denser atmosphere than it has today.



Ancient geological evidence shows that liquid water flowed across its surface.



Modern Mars has a thin atmosphere dominated by carbon dioxide.



Atmospheric escape was one of the major processes that transformed the planet.



\## Mars Gravity



Mars is much less massive than Earth.



Its weaker gravity makes atmospheric retention more difficult.



However, low gravity alone does not explain the entire history.



Solar radiation, solar wind, atmospheric chemistry, impacts, and internal activity all played roles.



\## Early Mars



Early Mars may have had:



Thicker atmosphere



More surface water



Warmer periods



More active volcanism



Over billions of years, atmospheric loss and surface sequestration reduced the amount of gas remaining in the atmosphere.



\## MAVEN Mission



NASA's:



MAVEN



mission was designed specifically to study the Martian upper atmosphere and atmospheric escape.



MAVEN stands for:



Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution



Its measurements help determine how solar radiation and the solar wind remove atmospheric gas.



\## MAVEN Findings



MAVEN has observed multiple mechanisms removing particles from Mars.



These include:



Ion escape



Photochemical escape



Sputtering-related processes



Loss rates also change during periods of enhanced solar activity.



\## Mars and Global Magnetic Field



Mars does not currently possess a strong global magnetic field like Earth.



However, parts of the ancient Martian crust remain strongly magnetized.



This shows that Mars once had a global magnetic dynamo.



\## Loss of Martian Dynamo



Mars's global dynamo shut down early in planetary history.



Afterward, the solar wind could interact more directly with much of the upper atmosphere.



This likely influenced atmospheric loss, although the full relationship between magnetic fields and atmospheric escape is complex.



\## Crustal Magnetic Fields



Mars retains localized magnetic regions in its crust.



These create small magnetic structures that modify local solar-wind interactions.



They do not provide a single global magnetosphere like Earth's.



\## Mars Carbon Dioxide Loss



Carbon dioxide can be removed from the Martian atmosphere in multiple ways.



Some escapes into space.



Some becomes incorporated into surface minerals.



Some freezes temporarily at the poles.



Understanding the relative importance of these reservoirs is central to Martian climate history.



\## Mars Water Loss



Water vapor can be transported upward and broken apart by sunlight.



Hydrogen escapes efficiently.



Some oxygen also escapes, while some reacts with Martian surface materials.



This contributes to the planet's oxidized surface.



\## Martian D/H Ratio



Mars has an elevated deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio compared with Earth's oceans.



This is strong evidence that Mars lost substantial quantities of water over its history.



\## Atmospheric Escape During Dust Storms



Large Martian dust storms can warm the atmosphere and transport more water vapor to high altitudes.



This can temporarily increase the amount of hydrogen available for escape.



Therefore surface weather and atmospheric escape can be connected.



\## Mercury



\[\[Mercury]] has no substantial atmosphere.



Instead, it has a very thin:



Exosphere



Mercury's low gravity, high surface temperatures, and intense solar environment make long-term retention of atmospheric gas difficult.



\## Mercury's Exosphere



Mercury's exosphere contains atoms such as:



Sodium



Potassium



Oxygen



Hydrogen



Helium



Calcium



Material is continually supplied and lost.



Sources include surface sputtering, micrometeorite impacts, thermal release, and solar-wind interactions.



\## Moon



The \[\[Moon]] also lacks a substantial atmosphere.



Its weak gravity and exposure to space prevent long-term retention of most gases.



It possesses only an extremely tenuous exosphere.



\## Lunar Exosphere



The lunar exosphere contains very small quantities of gases such as:



Helium



Argon



Sodium



Potassium



Particles are continually supplied and lost.



\## Titan



\[\[Titan]] has a thick atmosphere despite being smaller than Earth.



Its atmosphere is dominated by nitrogen and contains methane.



Its low temperature helps reduce thermal escape.



This demonstrates that planetary mass alone does not determine atmospheric retention.



\## Titan's Cold Atmosphere



Titan is extremely cold because it orbits far from the Sun.



Low atmospheric temperatures reduce molecular speeds.



This helps Titan retain heavy gases despite its relatively low gravity.



\## Titan Atmospheric Loss



Titan still loses atmospheric particles through photochemistry and interaction with Saturn's magnetosphere and the solar environment.



Its methane is also continuously destroyed by atmospheric chemistry and must have been supplied over geological time.



\## Pluto



\[\[Pluto]] has a thin atmosphere dominated mainly by nitrogen when it is sufficiently warm for surface ices to sublimate.



Because Pluto has weak gravity, its upper atmosphere is vulnerable to escape.



New Horizons measurements helped constrain the actual escape rate.



\## Pluto Escape Rates



Before New Horizons, some models predicted extremely rapid atmospheric escape from Pluto.



Observations showed that escape was lower than some earlier estimates.



This illustrates the importance of direct measurements.



\## Triton



\[\[Triton]] has a thin nitrogen-rich atmosphere.



Its low gravity allows atmospheric particles to escape relatively easily.



Seasonal sublimation of surface nitrogen ice continually interacts with the atmosphere.



\## Giant Planets



\[\[Jupiter]] and \[\[Saturn]] have enormous gravity and easily retain hydrogen and helium.



Atmospheric escape is much less effective at removing a significant fraction of their massive envelopes.



\## Ice Giants



\[\[Uranus]] and \[\[Neptune]] also retain hydrogen- and helium-rich outer atmospheres.



Their substantial masses and very low temperatures help preserve these gases.



\## Hot Jupiters



Hot Jupiters are giant \[\[Exoplanets]] orbiting extremely close to their stars.



They receive intense stellar radiation.



Their upper atmospheres can expand dramatically and escape.



\## Extended Exoplanet Atmospheres



Some hot Jupiters possess enormous extended clouds of escaping hydrogen.



These atmospheres can stretch well beyond the visible radius of the planet.



\## HD 209458 b



The hot Jupiter:



HD 209458 b



was one of the first exoplanets where atmospheric escape was detected.



Observations revealed an extended hydrogen atmosphere escaping from the planet.



\## Lyman-Alpha Observations



Hydrogen escape can be detected using ultraviolet observations of the:



Lyman-alpha



spectral line.



Escaping hydrogen can absorb starlight during a planetary transit.



\## Atmospheric Tails



Escaping gas can form a long tail behind a planet.



The structure can resemble a comet-like tail.



It is shaped by:



Stellar radiation



Stellar wind



Planetary gravity



Orbital motion



\## Warm Neptunes



Neptune-sized planets close to their stars can also experience substantial atmospheric escape.



Some may lose significant portions of their hydrogen-helium envelopes.



\## Sub-Neptunes



Many \[\[Exoplanets]] are sub-Neptunes with rocky cores surrounded by hydrogen-rich atmospheres.



Atmospheric escape can remove part or all of these envelopes.



This may transform some sub-Neptunes into smaller rocky planets.



\## Super-Earths



Some super-Earths may be stripped cores of planets that originally possessed larger gaseous envelopes.



Atmospheric escape is therefore central to understanding the observed population of small exoplanets.



\## Radius Valley



Exoplanet surveys have found a relative shortage of planets within a particular radius range between many super-Earths and sub-Neptunes.



This is called the:



Radius valley



Atmospheric loss is one major explanation.



\## Photoevaporation



High-energy radiation from a star can heat and remove a young planet's gaseous envelope.



This process is often called:



Photoevaporation



It can strongly reshape close-in planetary populations.



\## Core-Powered Mass Loss



Another mechanism proposed for removing planetary atmospheres is:



Core-powered mass loss



Heat stored inside a young planet can flow outward and help drive atmospheric escape.



This process does not require stellar high-energy radiation to provide all of the escape energy.



\## Photoevaporation Versus Core-Powered Loss



Both mechanisms can potentially help produce the observed super-Earth/sub-Neptune population.



Determining their relative importance remains an active field of research.



\## Young Stars and Atmospheric Escape



Young stars are generally much more active than older stars.



They can emit intense:



X-rays



Extreme ultraviolet radiation



Flares



Stellar winds



This makes the first hundreds of millions of years especially important for atmospheric evolution.



\## Red Dwarf Stars



Red dwarfs are small, cool stars that can remain magnetically active for long periods.



Their habitable zones are close to the star.



Planets there may receive strong high-energy radiation and stellar-wind exposure.



Atmospheric escape is therefore a major issue in evaluating their habitability.



\## Flares



Powerful stellar flares can temporarily increase high-energy radiation.



Repeated flaring may increase atmospheric heating and ionization.



However, the long-term effect depends on flare frequency, atmosphere composition, magnetic environment, and planetary gravity.



\## Coronal Mass Ejections



\[\[Coronal Mass Ejections]] can produce strong disturbances in the stellar wind and magnetic environment.



Repeated events may enhance atmospheric erosion under some conditions.



\## Space Weather



\[\[Space Weather]] describes changing conditions produced by stellar radiation, plasma, and magnetic activity.



Atmospheric escape can respond to these variations.



For example, escape rates at Mars can increase during solar storms.



\## Stellar Wind



The solar wind is one example of a:



Stellar wind



Other stars also produce outward flows of charged particles.



Young and active stars can produce stronger winds than the modern Sun.



\## Magnetically Unprotected Planets



A planet without a global magnetic field can interact directly with the stellar wind at the upper atmosphere or ionosphere.



This can increase some types of atmospheric loss.



However, whether an intrinsic magnetic field always produces better long-term atmospheric retention is still debated.



\## Induced Magnetosphere



A planet without a strong internal magnetic field can still develop an:



Induced magnetosphere



The stellar wind interacts with its ionosphere and drapes magnetic field around the planet.



Venus and Mars both display induced magnetic structures.



\## Venus Has No Strong Intrinsic Field



Venus lacks a strong internally generated global magnetic field.



Its ionosphere nevertheless interacts with the solar wind and creates an induced magnetosphere.



Despite this, Venus retains an extremely dense atmosphere.



This is an important reminder that magnetic-field strength alone does not determine atmospheric survival.



\## Gravity Versus Magnetic Protection



A useful comparison is:



Venus has no strong intrinsic global field but has a massive atmosphere.



Mars lacks a global field and has a thin atmosphere.



Earth has a global magnetic field and a substantial atmosphere.



These differences show that atmospheric retention depends on many variables, especially gravity, temperature, atmospheric inventory, solar history, and chemistry.



\## Atmospheric Erosion by Impacts



Large impacts can also remove atmosphere.



A sufficiently energetic collision can accelerate atmospheric gas into space.



Repeated impacts during early planetary history may have caused significant atmospheric erosion.



\## Impact Delivery



Impacts can also deliver new gases and volatile-rich material.



Therefore impacts can both:



Remove atmosphere



Add atmosphere



The final effect depends on impactor size, velocity, composition, and planetary conditions.



\## Giant Impacts



Planet-scale collisions during \[\[Planet Formation]] can dramatically alter atmospheres.



A giant impact may:



Strip atmosphere



Vaporize oceans



Release interior gases



Deliver volatiles



Create debris



Atmospheric evolution during planetary formation is therefore highly dynamic.



\## Moon-Forming Impact



The impact associated with formation of Earth's Moon likely had major effects on Earth's early atmosphere and volatile inventory.



Exactly how much atmosphere was lost or retained remains an area of research.



\## Volcanic Replenishment



Atmospheric escape can be partly balanced by \[\[Planetary Volcanism]].



Volcanoes release gases from the interior.



If outgassing occurs faster than escape, an atmosphere can grow.



If escape dominates, atmospheric pressure can decline.



\## Atmospheric Source-Sink Balance



A planet's atmosphere reflects competition among sources and sinks.



Sources can include:



Volcanic outgassing



Impact delivery



Surface sublimation



Chemical production



Sinks can include:



Atmospheric escape



Surface reactions



Condensation



Mineral formation



Biological processes where applicable



\## Surface Sequestration



Not all missing atmospheric gas escaped to space.



Some can become stored in:



Minerals



Ice



Oceans



Soil



Polar deposits



This is especially important when reconstructing Mars's past atmosphere.



\## Carbonate Formation



Carbon dioxide can react with rocks and become stored in:



Carbonate minerals



This process removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere without losing it to space.



\## Atmospheric Collapse



On cold planets, atmospheric gases can condense onto the surface.



This is called:



Atmospheric collapse



It is different from atmospheric escape because the gas remains gravitationally bound to the planet.



\## Seasonal Atmospheric Changes



Some bodies experience strong seasonal exchanges between surface ice and atmosphere.



Pluto, Triton, and Mars all exhibit atmospheric changes influenced by seasonal condensation and sublimation.



\## Atmospheric Retention and Molecular Mass



Heavy molecules are generally easier to retain than light molecules.



A world may lose hydrogen while retaining:



Carbon dioxide



Nitrogen



Argon



This can strongly change atmospheric composition over time.



\## Noble Gases



Noble gas abundances and isotope ratios provide valuable information about atmospheric origins and escape.



Examples include:



Argon



Neon



Xenon



Helium



They are chemically relatively unreactive, making them useful tracers.



\## Isotope Fractionation



Atmospheric escape often removes lighter isotopes more efficiently than heavier isotopes.



This is called:



Isotopic fractionation



The remaining atmosphere becomes relatively enriched in heavier isotopes.



\## Important Isotope Ratios



Scientists study ratios such as:



Deuterium / hydrogen



Nitrogen-15 / nitrogen-14



Argon isotope ratios



Xenon isotopes



These can reveal the history of atmospheric loss.



\## Mars Argon Isotopes



Measurements of argon isotopes on Mars provide evidence that the planet lost a significant portion of its atmosphere over time.



\## Nitrogen Escape



Nitrogen can also escape through non-thermal processes.



Its isotope ratios provide information about atmospheric evolution on worlds such as Mars and Titan.



\## Atmospheric Escape and Oxidation



When hydrogen escapes preferentially, the remaining planet can become more oxidized.



This occurs because hydrogen is chemically reducing.



Removing hydrogen can leave oxygen available to react with surface materials.



\## Mars's Red Surface



Mars's famous red color comes largely from oxidized iron minerals.



Atmospheric and surface oxidation involve many processes, including interactions among water, oxygen-bearing species, rocks, and atmospheric loss.



\## Ocean Loss



Atmospheric escape can eventually contribute to the loss of an entire ocean.



Water first evaporates into the atmosphere.



Ultraviolet light breaks water molecules apart.



Hydrogen escapes.



Oxygen is removed through surface reactions or other escape processes.



Over long enough times, the planet's water inventory decreases.



\## Runaway Greenhouse



In an extreme greenhouse state, surface oceans can evaporate.



Large amounts of water vapor enter the atmosphere.



Water then becomes much more vulnerable to photodissociation and hydrogen escape.



This process is important in models of Venus's history.



\## Stellar Luminosity Evolution



Stars gradually change brightness over their lifetimes.



The young Sun was fainter in visible light than today, but it was much more active in high-energy radiation.



Both factors influence atmospheric and climate evolution.



\## Habitable Zone and Escape



A planet's position in the habitable zone does not guarantee it can retain water or atmosphere.



Atmospheric escape may remove crucial gases.



Conversely, a sufficiently massive or cold planet can retain atmosphere outside the traditional habitable zone.



\## Planet Mass and Habitability



A very low-mass planet can have difficulty retaining atmosphere.



A very high-mass rocky planet may retain a thick hydrogen envelope.



Therefore atmospheric retention contributes to the diversity of potentially habitable conditions.



\## Exomoons



Moons around giant exoplanets may also experience atmospheric escape.



Low-gravity moons are particularly vulnerable.



A large moon with strong outgassing or a cold environment may retain an atmosphere more successfully.



\## Atmospheres of Icy Moons



Several icy moons possess thin atmospheres or exospheres produced by:



Sublimation



Sputtering



Plumes



Surface chemistry



Much of this material can later escape into space.



\## Europa



\[\[Europa]] has a very thin oxygen atmosphere.



It is produced mainly through radiation interacting with surface water ice.



Because Europa's gravity is relatively weak, particles can eventually be lost.



\## Ganymede



\[\[Ganymede]] possesses a tenuous atmosphere containing oxygen-related species.



Its intrinsic magnetic field creates a complex interaction with Jupiter's magnetosphere.



\## Enceladus



\[\[Enceladus]] continuously ejects water vapor and ice grains through its plumes.



A significant amount of this material escapes the moon and contributes to Saturn's E ring.



This is an example where geologic activity directly supplies material that is subsequently lost to space.



\## Io



\[\[Io]] has a thin atmosphere dominated largely by sulfur dioxide.



Volcanic activity continuously replenishes it.



Material is also stripped away and contributes to the Io plasma torus around Jupiter.



\## Atmosphere Replenishment Versus Loss



Io demonstrates that a thin atmosphere can remain present despite rapid loss if volcanic replenishment continuously supplies new material.



Atmospheric pressure therefore depends on both loss rate and supply rate.



\## Measuring Atmospheric Escape



Scientists study atmospheric escape using:



Spacecraft plasma instruments



Mass spectrometers



Ultraviolet spectroscopy



Remote sensing



Atmospheric models



Isotope measurements



Transit spectroscopy of exoplanets



\## Ultraviolet Spectroscopy



Many escaping species strongly absorb or emit ultraviolet light.



Ultraviolet observations are therefore especially valuable for studying upper atmospheres.



\## Transit Spectroscopy



When an exoplanet passes in front of its star, some starlight travels through the planet's atmosphere.



Different atmospheric substances absorb specific wavelengths.



An extended escaping atmosphere can produce an unusually large transit signature at those wavelengths.



\## Helium Escape Observations



Metastable helium can be detected using near-infrared spectroscopy.



This has become an important method for studying atmospheric escape from exoplanets.



It can sometimes be observed more easily from the ground than ultraviolet hydrogen signatures.



\## Comet-Like Exoplanet Tails



Some exoplanets show evidence of enormous escaping tails.



These can contain:



Hydrogen



Helium



Metal atoms



Dust



depending on the planet.



\## Disintegrating Rocky Planets



A few extremely hot rocky exoplanets appear to lose solid material as dust.



These worlds may produce long dusty tails.



This is more extreme than ordinary atmospheric escape and can involve direct surface vaporization.



\## Atmospheric Escape Models



Scientists use numerical models to simulate:



Upper-atmosphere temperature



Ionization



Chemical reactions



Hydrodynamic flow



Stellar wind interactions



Magnetic fields



Radiative heating and cooling



These models help estimate long-term atmospheric survival.



\## Jeans Parameter



A useful measure in thermal escape studies is the:



Jeans parameter



It compares gravitational binding energy with thermal energy.



Large values indicate particles are strongly bound.



Small values make thermal escape more effective.



\## Scale Height



Atmospheric scale height describes how quickly atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude.



It is approximately influenced by:



Temperature



Gravity



Molecular mass



Hot atmospheres, weak gravity, and light gases produce larger scale heights.



\## Puffy Atmospheres



Some low-density exoplanets possess very extended atmospheres.



Their large atmospheric scale heights make them easier to observe.



They may also be particularly vulnerable to atmospheric escape.



\## Roche Lobe



For a planet orbiting very close to its star, stellar gravity affects how tightly atmospheric gas is bound.



The region where planetary gravity dominates is related to the planet's:



Roche lobe



Gas expanding toward or beyond this region can escape more easily.



\## Roche-Lobe Overflow



An extremely close planet can lose material through:



Roche-lobe overflow



Gas flows beyond the region gravitationally controlled by the planet and toward the star or surrounding space.



\## Atmospheric Escape Timescale



The importance of atmospheric escape depends on both loss rate and total atmospheric mass.



A high loss rate may have little long-term effect on an enormous gas giant.



The same rate could remove the atmosphere of a small rocky planet.



\## Cumulative Loss



Even a slow escape rate can become important over billions of years.



Scientists therefore integrate loss rates over stellar and planetary history rather than considering only present-day escape.



\## Early Escape Was Often Stronger



Present atmospheric loss rates may be much lower than ancient rates.



Young stars were generally more active.



Planetary atmospheres may also have been hotter and more extended.



Therefore reconstructing ancient escape requires models of stellar evolution.



\## Atmospheric Escape and Planetary Evolution



Atmospheric escape can transform a planetary body.



A world beginning with a thick hydrogen-rich envelope may become a rocky planet with a secondary atmosphere.



A wet planet can become dry.



A thick atmosphere can become thin.



The chemical composition can change as lighter gases are preferentially removed.



\## Comparative Planetology



Comparing Earth, Venus, and Mars is especially useful.



All three are rocky planets, but their modern atmospheres differ enormously.



Earth has moderate surface pressure and abundant water.



Venus has an extremely dense carbon dioxide atmosphere and little water.



Mars has a thin carbon dioxide atmosphere and evidence for major ancient atmospheric and water loss.



Atmospheric escape is one part of the explanation for these differences.



\## Atmospheric Escape and Planet Formation



Atmospheric evolution begins during \[\[Planet Formation]].



Young rocky planets can capture gas from the protoplanetary disk.



After the disk disappears, intense stellar radiation can strip these envelopes.



Impacts and volcanic outgassing can then create new atmospheres.



Thus atmosphere formation and atmosphere loss operate together.



\## Atmospheric Escape and Migration



\[\[Planetary Migration]] can change atmospheric escape dramatically.



A planet forming far from its star may migrate inward.



As stellar radiation increases, its atmosphere can begin escaping rapidly.



This helps explain some close-in exoplanets.



\## Common Misconceptions



\### Atmospheres Escape Only Because Gravity Is Weak



Incorrect. Gravity is important, but temperature, stellar radiation, chemistry, stellar wind, planetary magnetic environment, and atmospheric composition also matter.



\### A Magnetic Field Completely Prevents Atmospheric Loss



Incorrect. Earth has a strong magnetic field and still loses atmospheric particles.



\### A Planet Without a Magnetic Field Cannot Have a Thick Atmosphere



Incorrect. Venus lacks a strong intrinsic global magnetic field but retains an extremely dense atmosphere.



\### Only Hydrogen Can Escape



Incorrect. Hydrogen escapes most easily, but oxygen, carbon, nitrogen, and other species can also escape through non-thermal or hydrodynamic processes.



\### Mars Lost Its Entire Atmosphere Only to the Solar Wind



Incorrect. Atmospheric escape was important, but gas was also stored in surface materials and polar reservoirs, and multiple escape processes operated.



\### Atmospheric Escape Happens Only on Rocky Planets



Incorrect. Gas giants, ice giants, dwarf planets, moons, and exoplanets can all lose atmospheric material.



\### Atmospheric Loss Is Always Slow



Incorrect. Under intense irradiation, hydrodynamic escape can remove large quantities of gas relatively rapidly.



\### If Oxygen Is Detected on an Exoplanet, Life Must Be Present



Incorrect. Atmospheric escape following water photodissociation can potentially produce abiotic oxygen.



\## Important Terminology



\*\*Atmospheric Escape:\*\* Permanent loss of atmospheric material into space.



\*\*Exosphere:\*\* Outermost atmospheric region where particle collisions are rare.



\*\*Exobase:\*\* Approximate lower boundary of the exosphere.



\*\*Jeans Escape:\*\* Thermal escape of high-speed particles from the tail of the velocity distribution.



\*\*Hydrodynamic Escape:\*\* Bulk atmospheric outflow driven by strong heating.



\*\*Photoevaporation:\*\* Atmospheric removal driven largely by stellar high-energy radiation.



\*\*Sputtering:\*\* Escape caused by energetic particles transferring energy to atmospheric atoms.



\*\*Ion Pickup:\*\* Loss of newly ionized atmospheric particles carried away by stellar wind.



\*\*Photochemical Escape:\*\* Escape produced by energetic products of atmospheric chemical reactions.



\*\*Diffusion-Limited Escape:\*\* Escape limited by how quickly a species can diffuse through the atmosphere.



\*\*Isotopic Fractionation:\*\* Preferential removal of lighter isotopes during escape.



\*\*Primary Atmosphere:\*\* Atmosphere captured mainly from the protoplanetary disk.



\*\*Secondary Atmosphere:\*\* Atmosphere produced later by outgassing, impacts, and other processes.



\## Simplified Jeans Escape



A planet's upper atmosphere contains gas particles moving at many different speeds.



Most particles move too slowly to escape.



A small fraction happen to move much faster.



If one of these particles is above the exobase and exceeds the required escape speed, it can leave the planet permanently.



Because hydrogen is light and moves rapidly, it is especially vulnerable to this process.



\## Simplified Hydrodynamic Escape



A young star emits intense ultraviolet and X-ray radiation.



The radiation heats the upper atmosphere of a nearby planet.



The atmosphere expands to very high altitudes.



Hydrogen begins flowing away from the planet in large quantities.



If the flow is strong enough, heavier species can also be dragged outward.



Over time, the planet can lose a substantial fraction of its original atmosphere.



\## Simplified Mars Atmospheric Loss



Early Mars possesses a thicker atmosphere and more surface water.



The planet's interior cools and its global magnetic dynamo ceases.



Solar ultraviolet radiation ionizes and heats the upper atmosphere.



Solar-wind interactions, photochemical reactions, and other processes remove atmospheric particles.



Water molecules are broken apart.



Hydrogen escapes efficiently.



Some atmospheric carbon and oxygen escape, while additional material becomes trapped in rocks or ice.



Over billions of years, Mars develops the thin atmosphere seen today.



\## Scientific Importance



Atmospheric escape is essential for explaining why planetary atmospheres evolve so differently.



It affects surface pressure, water retention, climate, oxidation state, and the long-term stability of potentially habitable conditions.



It helps explain the evolution of Mars and Venus, the loss of primordial atmospheres from rocky planets, the extended atmospheres of hot Jupiters, and the division between many super-Earths and sub-Neptunes.



Atmospheric escape is also important when evaluating possible biosignatures because some gases, including oxygen, can be produced or concentrated through non-biological atmospheric evolution.



\## Open Questions



Scientists continue investigating exactly how much atmosphere Mars lost to space compared with the amount stored in rocks and polar reservoirs.



The early water inventory and escape history of Venus remain uncertain.



Researchers also continue studying whether planetary magnetic fields generally increase or decrease long-term atmospheric retention under different stellar conditions.



For exoplanets, major questions include how rapidly red-dwarf planets lose their atmospheres, whether habitable-zone planets around active stars can retain water, how photoevaporation and core-powered mass loss divide super-Earths from sub-Neptunes, and how atmospheric escape can be distinguished from biological processes when interpreting future atmospheric observations.



\## Summary



Atmospheric escape is the permanent loss of gases from a planetary atmosphere into space.



Light particles such as hydrogen escape most easily, but many mechanisms can remove heavier species as well.



Thermal processes include Jeans escape and hydrodynamic escape. Non-thermal processes include sputtering, ion pickup, photochemical escape, charge exchange, and plasma-driven loss.



Atmospheric escape has played a major role in the history of \[\[Mars]], contributed to the water evolution of \[\[Venus]], and continuously occurs on \[\[Earth]].



It is especially powerful on young planets exposed to intense ultraviolet and X-ray radiation.



Among \[\[Exoplanets]], atmospheric escape can transform gas-rich sub-Neptunes into smaller rocky worlds and can create enormous hydrogen or helium tails around close-in planets.



Atmospheric retention depends on the combined effects of gravity, temperature, composition, stellar radiation, stellar wind, magnetic environment, atmospheric replenishment, and geological history.



For this reason, atmospheric escape is one of the central processes linking \[\[Planetary Atmospheres]], \[\[Space Weather]], \[\[Planetary Evolution]], \[\[Water in the Solar System]], and \[\[Planetary Habitability]].
