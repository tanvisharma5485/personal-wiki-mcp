\# Sedna



\## Overview



Sedna is one of the most distant known large bodies in the Solar System. It is a trans-Neptunian object with an extremely elongated orbit and a perihelion far beyond the orbit of \[\[Neptune]]. Because Neptune cannot strongly perturb Sedna at its current closest approach to the Sun, Sedna is usually classified as a detached object rather than an ordinary member of the \[\[Scattered Disc]].



Sedna is scientifically important because its unusual orbit may preserve evidence of conditions that existed very early in Solar System history. Its trajectory has been used to investigate the Sun's birth environment, early stellar encounters, the migration of the giant planets, possible distant planetary perturbers, and the structure of the outermost Solar System.



Sedna is closely connected with \[\[Trans-Neptunian Objects]], \[\[Scattered Disc]], \[\[Oort Cloud]], \[\[Planetary Migration]], \[\[Orbital Dynamics]], and \[\[Solar System Formation]].



\## Discovery and Naming



Sedna was discovered in 2003 by a team led by astronomers Michael Brown, Chad Trujillo, and David Rabinowitz during a survey for distant Solar System objects. Because it was extremely far from the Sun at discovery, it immediately stood out from the better-known Kuiper Belt population.



The object was named Sedna after a sea goddess in Inuit mythology who is associated with the cold depths of the ocean. The name was chosen to reflect the extreme cold and remoteness of the object's environment.



Sedna's official minor-planet designation is:



90377 Sedna



Its discovery greatly expanded astronomers' view of the region beyond the conventional \[\[Kuiper Belt]].



\## Orbit



Sedna follows one of the most unusual known orbits among large Solar System bodies. Its orbit is highly eccentric, meaning it is strongly elongated rather than nearly circular.



Its perihelion, the point where it comes closest to the Sun, is about 76 astronomical units away. This is more than twice Neptune's average orbital distance.



Its aphelion extends to roughly 900 AU or more, depending on the orbital solution.



Sedna therefore spends most of its orbit at enormous distances from the Sun.



One complete orbit takes roughly:



11,000 Earth years



This means that no human civilization has observed Sedna complete even a tiny fraction of one revolution around the Sun.



\## Why Sedna Is Detached



Sedna's perihelion is far beyond Neptune's orbit.



Neptune orbits at approximately:



30 AU



Sedna never approaches Neptune closely enough for ordinary present-day gravitational encounters to explain its current orbit.



This is why Sedna is described as:



Detached



Its orbit is effectively detached from strong direct control by the known giant planets.



This feature is the central scientific mystery surrounding Sedna.



\## Sedna Versus Scattered Disc Objects



Typical \[\[Scattered Disc]] objects have eccentric orbits whose perihelia remain close enough to Neptune for the planet to continue altering their trajectories.



Sedna is different.



Its perihelion is much farther away, so its present orbit cannot easily be produced or maintained through ordinary Neptune scattering alone.



This distinction places Sedna in a population often described as detached, extended scattered, or possibly inner-Oort-Cloud-like, depending on the classification system being used.



\## Extreme Trans-Neptunian Object



Sedna is often discussed as an example of an:



Extreme trans-Neptunian object



or:



ETNO



This broad term is used for objects with exceptionally large semimajor axes, distant perihelia, or other unusual orbital characteristics.



Different studies use somewhat different numerical definitions, so ETNO is not a single universally fixed category.



\## Possible Inner Oort Cloud Connection



Sedna is sometimes considered a candidate member of the:



Inner \[\[Oort Cloud]]



The classical Oort Cloud is thought to extend thousands to tens of thousands of AU from the Sun.



Sedna is much closer than most traditional Oort Cloud objects, but its detached orbit may represent a transitional population between the trans-Neptunian region and the inner Oort Cloud.



Because the exact inner boundary of the Oort Cloud is uncertain, Sedna's classification remains partly dependent on the dynamical model being used.



\## Formation Problem



The main question about Sedna is simple:



How did it acquire an orbit with such a distant perihelion?



The known giant planets can scatter objects to enormous semimajor axes, but such scattering normally leaves their perihelia near the planetary region.



Some additional process is therefore required to lift Sedna's perihelion away from Neptune.



Several major hypotheses have been proposed.



\## Stellar Encounter Hypothesis



One of the leading explanations is that Sedna's orbit was altered by a passing star early in Solar System history.



The Sun probably formed inside a star cluster containing many neighboring stars.



During this stage, stellar separations were much smaller than in the Sun's present neighborhood.



A relatively close stellar passage could have gravitationally perturbed distant planetesimals and raised their perihelia.



Sedna may therefore preserve evidence about the Sun's original stellar birth environment.



\## Solar Birth Cluster



Stars generally form in groups rather than completely alone.



The young Sun likely spent its early life inside a cluster.



If another star passed within a few hundred or thousand AU, its gravity could significantly alter the orbits of distant small bodies.



Such an encounter could create a population of detached objects with Sedna-like trajectories.



If this explanation is correct, Sedna is not merely a distant dwarf-planet candidate; it is also a fossil of the Sun's birth cluster.



\## Encounter With a Passing Field Star



A stellar encounter could also occur after the Sun left its birth cluster.



However, close encounters become much less probable in the present sparse stellar environment.



For this reason, an early cluster encounter is generally considered more plausible than a very close recent flyby, although both types of scenarios can be modeled.



\## Planet Nine Hypothesis



Another proposed explanation involves an undiscovered distant planet.



The hypothetical object often called:



Planet Nine



has been suggested as a possible gravitational influence on the orbits of Sedna and other extreme TNOs.



Models typically place this proposed planet far beyond Neptune on an elongated orbit, with a mass several times that of Earth.



Its gravity could potentially raise perihelia, alter inclinations, and organize some distant TNO orbits.



However, Planet Nine has not been directly detected.



It remains a hypothesis.



\## Sedna Does Not Prove Planet Nine



Sedna's existence alone is not evidence that conclusively demonstrates a distant planet.



Multiple mechanisms can generate Sedna-like orbits.



These include:



Stellar encounters



Early cluster dynamics



Planetary migration



A distant planet



Temporary early planets that were later ejected



Combinations of these mechanisms



Any explanation must account not just for Sedna but for the broader population of detached and extreme TNOs.



\## Early Additional Planet Hypotheses



Some models propose that one or more planetary-mass bodies existed in the early outer Solar System but were later ejected.



Before ejection, such a body could have perturbed distant planetesimals and helped create detached orbits.



This hypothetical ancient planet is not necessarily the same as the modern Planet Nine proposal.



\## Neptune Migration



\[\[Planetary Migration]] may also contribute to producing detached objects.



As Neptune migrated outward, its resonances moved through the primordial planetesimal disk.



Resonant interactions can change orbital eccentricity and perihelion.



In some cases, objects may become detached from direct Neptune scattering.



However, generating Sedna's very distant perihelion is more challenging than producing moderately detached TNOs.



\## Resonance Effects



An object temporarily trapped in a mean-motion resonance with Neptune can experience coupled changes in eccentricity and inclination.



Certain secular or Kozai-like mechanisms can increase perihelion distance while the object remains resonant.



If Neptune later migrates or the object leaves the resonance, it may be stranded on a more detached orbit.



This mechanism may explain some detached objects, although Sedna's extreme orbit remains especially difficult.



\## Galactic Tides



The gravitational field of the Milky Way can perturb very distant Solar System bodies.



This effect is called:



Galactic tides



It becomes increasingly important at thousands of AU and is a major process shaping the \[\[Oort Cloud]].



Sedna's orbit is generally too close to the Sun for Galactic tides alone to readily explain its present perihelion, but they become increasingly relevant for even more distant objects.



\## Current Distance



Sedna is currently relatively close to perihelion compared with the rest of its enormous orbit.



This is one major reason it was discovered.



At aphelion, Sedna would be dramatically fainter and far more difficult to detect.



This creates a major observational bias in surveys of Sedna-like objects.



\## Perihelion Bias



Distant eccentric bodies are easiest to discover when near perihelion.



An object may spend thousands of years far away and only a small fraction of its orbit close enough to be detected.



Therefore the few known detached objects likely represent only a fraction of the true population.



Population estimates must account for this bias.



\## Size



Sedna is estimated to be roughly:



1,000 kilometers



in diameter.



The exact value depends on thermal measurements, assumptions about surface reflectivity, and observational uncertainties.



It is therefore one of the larger known trans-Neptunian objects.



Its size makes it a strong candidate for hydrostatic equilibrium, although it is not officially classified as a dwarf planet by the International Astronomical Union.



\## Dwarf Planet Candidate



A \[\[Dwarf Planets|Dwarf Planet]] must orbit the Sun, not be a moon, have enough gravity to become approximately round, and not have cleared its orbital neighborhood.



Sedna likely satisfies several of these criteria.



However, because its shape has not been directly resolved with sufficient precision, formal classification remains cautious.



It is commonly described as a dwarf-planet candidate.



\## Surface Color



Sedna has an unusually red surface.



It is among the redder known large bodies in the outer Solar System.



The color is probably related to complex organic-rich materials created when radiation chemically alters carbon-bearing ices.



These radiation-processed compounds are often broadly discussed using the term:



Tholins



\## Tholins



Tholins are complex mixtures of organic-rich substances produced when simple compounds containing carbon, hydrogen, and sometimes nitrogen are exposed to energetic radiation.



They are not a single chemical compound.



Tholin-like materials may contribute to reddish surfaces on:



Sedna



\[\[Pluto]]



\[\[Centaurs]]



Other trans-Neptunian bodies



\## Surface Composition



Spectroscopic studies have suggested that Sedna's surface may contain mixtures involving:



Water ice



Methane-related material



Nitrogen-bearing or hydrocarbon-rich compounds



Complex organics



The exact abundance of each component remains uncertain because Sedna is extremely faint and distant.



\## Methane



Methane ice is known on several large distant objects.



If present on Sedna, ultraviolet radiation and cosmic rays can gradually convert methane into more complex hydrocarbons.



These products can help produce a dark red surface.



\## Nitrogen and Other Volatiles



Sedna is extremely cold.



At such temperatures, volatile compounds may remain stable on or below the surface for very long periods.



Whether substances such as nitrogen, methane, or carbon monoxide remain exposed depends on surface temperature, gravity, and the object's long thermal history.



\## Temperature



Sedna receives extremely little sunlight.



Its equilibrium surface temperature is only a few tens of kelvins.



Exact temperature changes over its orbit because solar heating decreases dramatically as Sedna travels outward.



At aphelion, it becomes one of the coldest large known bodies gravitationally bound to the Sun.



\## Seasonal Effects



Because Sedna's orbit is extremely eccentric, the amount of solar energy it receives changes greatly over thousands of years.



Near perihelion, surface temperatures are higher than at aphelion.



This could affect:



Volatile sublimation



Surface frost



Atmospheric formation



Thermal cycling



However, all such processes occur at extremely low temperatures compared with Earth.



\## Possible Atmosphere



Sedna may be capable of developing a very tenuous temporary atmosphere near perihelion if sufficiently volatile surface ices sublime.



Potential gases could include nitrogen or methane.



No substantial atmosphere has been definitively detected.



Far from the Sun, most volatile material would be expected to freeze onto the surface again.



\## Volatile Retention



Sedna's combination of low temperature and moderate gravity allows it to retain volatile substances more effectively than many smaller or warmer bodies.



Atmospheric retention depends on molecular mass, temperature, gravity, and geological timescale.



The most volatile compounds can nevertheless migrate or escape over billions of years.



\## Internal Structure



Sedna's internal structure is unknown.



A plausible large-TNO interior could contain:



Rocky material



Water ice



Other frozen volatiles



If Sedna formed early and contained enough radioactive elements, internal heating may have caused some degree of \[\[Planetary Differentiation]].



\## Possible Differentiation



Large icy bodies can heat through:



Radioactive decay



Accretional energy



Residual formation heat



If internal water ice melts, dense rock sinks while lower-density water-rich material rises.



This can produce a layered structure.



Whether Sedna differentiated extensively is not currently known.



\## Possible Subsurface Ocean



A sufficiently large icy object may preserve internal liquid water for long periods if radioactive heating and ice-shell insulation are strong enough.



Sedna's relatively small size compared with Pluto makes a present-day ocean less certain.



Ammonia or salts could lower water's freezing point if present.



At present, there is no direct evidence for a Sedna ocean.



\## Cryovolcanism



If Sedna ever possessed internal liquid or partially molten water-rich material, \[\[Cryovolcanism]] may have been possible.



However, no surface features have been resolved well enough to determine whether cryovolcanism occurred.



Unlike Pluto, Sedna has never been visited by a spacecraft.



\## Surface Geology



Sedna is so distant that telescopes cannot resolve detailed surface geology.



Scientists currently cannot directly map:



Craters



Mountains



Faults



Cryovolcanoes



Glaciers



Surface layering



Most physical knowledge comes from brightness, color, spectroscopy, and thermal measurements.



\## Rotation



Sedna rotates on its axis.



Its rotational period has been estimated through variations in brightness.



Early observations once suggested a very slow rotation, leading to speculation about an unseen moon.



Later measurements indicated a much faster rotation, so a massive satellite is not required to explain the spin.



\## Search for Satellites



No confirmed moon of Sedna is currently known.



A satellite would be scientifically valuable because its orbit would allow Sedna's mass to be determined much more accurately.



Without a moon or spacecraft encounter, mass is difficult to measure directly.



\## Density Uncertainty



Sedna's density is poorly constrained because its mass is unknown.



If a satellite were discovered, scientists could calculate the system mass and combine it with Sedna's estimated size to determine bulk density.



Density would reveal the approximate proportion of rock and ice.



\## Albedo



Sedna reflects a moderate fraction of incoming sunlight compared with many extremely dark small bodies.



Surface reflectivity, or albedo, is important because visible brightness alone cannot determine size.



A small bright object and a larger dark object can appear equally bright.



Thermal measurements help break this degeneracy.



\## Thermal Observations



Infrared observations measure the faint thermal radiation emitted by Sedna.



Even at temperatures of only a few tens of kelvins, the object emits energy at long infrared wavelengths.



Combining thermal radiation with visible brightness improves estimates of diameter and albedo.



\## Spectroscopy



Spectroscopy separates reflected sunlight into wavelengths.



Different surface materials absorb characteristic wavelengths.



Because Sedna is faint, obtaining high-quality spectra is difficult, but spectroscopy remains one of the main techniques for studying its surface composition.



\## Orbital Period and Human Timescales



Sedna's roughly 11,000-year orbital period means its motion provides a dramatic example of the scale of outer Solar System dynamics.



When Sedna was last near a comparable orbital position, human civilization was in a very different stage of development.



Its next full orbit will occur thousands of years in the future.



\## Slow Motion in the Sky



Because Sedna is so distant, it moves very slowly against background stars.



Distant-object surveys identify this slow motion by comparing images taken at different times.



The closer an object is to Earth, the faster its apparent motion generally appears.



\## Discovery Near Perihelion



Sedna was fortunate to be detectable because it is currently in the relatively inner part of its orbit.



If it had been near aphelion, its brightness would have been dramatically lower.



This suggests that many Sedna-like objects may exist but remain undetected because they are currently too far away.



\## Population Implications



If one object like Sedna was discovered during a limited sky survey and limited orbital window, the underlying population may be substantial.



Astronomers use survey detection probabilities to estimate how many similar bodies may exist.



These estimates remain uncertain because only a small number of comparable detached objects are known.



\## Other Sedna-Like Objects



Several other bodies have distant perihelia, although few are as extreme as Sedna.



Examples include:



2012 VP113



Leleākūhonua



and other extreme detached objects.



Together, these bodies form an important population for testing outer Solar System models.



\## 2012 VP113



2012 VP113 has a perihelion near 80 AU, comparable to or slightly beyond Sedna's.



Its discovery demonstrated that Sedna was not necessarily unique.



The existence of multiple highly detached objects strengthened interest in the dynamical mechanisms capable of producing such orbits.



\## Leleākūhonua



541132 Leleākūhonua, formerly designated 2015 TG387, is another extremely distant object.



Its orbit extends far beyond Neptune and has a high perihelion.



It is sometimes nicknamed:



The Goblin



Its discovery added another data point to the population of extreme distant bodies.



\## Orbital Clustering



Some studies have reported that several extreme TNOs appear to have similarly oriented orbits.



This apparent clustering helped motivate the Planet Nine hypothesis.



However, distant-object surveys have strong selection effects.



Different research teams continue studying whether the clustering remains statistically significant after observational biases are fully modeled.



\## Longitude of Perihelion



One orbital quantity used in clustering studies is:



Longitude of perihelion



It describes the direction in space toward an orbit's perihelion.



If many objects share similar values beyond what random distributions predict, this could indicate a common dynamical influence.



However, survey geometry can also preferentially detect certain orientations.



\## Argument of Perihelion



Another orbital parameter is the:



Argument of perihelion



It describes where perihelion occurs relative to the object's orbital plane.



Several orbital elements together are needed to characterize distant-object clustering.



\## Inclination



Sedna's orbit is tilted relative to the main planetary plane.



Its inclination is significant but not extreme compared with some distant TNOs.



Any formation model must reproduce not only its perihelion and semimajor axis but also its inclination.



\## Orbital Eccentricity



Sedna's high eccentricity is responsible for the enormous difference between perihelion and aphelion distance.



This orbital shape means sunlight changes by more than an order of magnitude over one revolution.



\## Semimajor Axis



Sedna's semimajor axis is hundreds of AU.



According to Kepler's third law, such a large orbit naturally produces an orbital period of thousands of years.



The semimajor axis can be revised slightly as observations improve.



\## Orbital Precision



Because Sedna has been observed for only a tiny fraction of its orbit, its extremely distant future position cannot be measured from a complete revolution.



Nevertheless, modern astrometry over decades allows astronomers to constrain its orbit accurately enough for dynamical studies.



Longer observation arcs continue improving the solution.



\## Numerical Simulations



Researchers simulate Sedna's orbit under different early Solar System conditions.



They may include:



The known giant planets



Migrating Neptune



A stellar birth cluster



Passing stars



Galactic tides



Hypothetical distant planets



Large numbers of test particles



The resulting synthetic populations are then compared with observed detached objects.



\## A Good Model Must Explain a Population



A successful explanation should not merely place one simulated object on Sedna's exact orbit.



It should reproduce a reasonable population of objects with:



Similar perihelia



Semimajor axes



Inclinations



Orbital orientations



Population sizes



It must also remain consistent with known planets and other TNO populations.



\## Connection to the Oort Cloud



The \[\[Oort Cloud]] is believed to have formed when the giant planets scattered icy planetesimals to very large distances.



Passing stars and Galactic tides then raised their perihelia, separating them from repeated encounters with the planets.



Sedna may represent a milder version of this process.



This is why it is sometimes described as an inner-Oort-Cloud candidate.



\## Classical Oort Cloud Difference



Classical Oort Cloud objects may orbit at tens of thousands of AU and have an approximately spherical distribution.



Sedna remains much closer to the Sun and belongs to a more flattened or intermediate population.



Therefore calling Sedna simply an ordinary Oort Cloud comet would be misleading.



\## Sedna Versus Kuiper Belt



The conventional \[\[Kuiper Belt]] lies mainly tens of AU from the Sun, roughly around 30–50 AU.



Sedna's perihelion alone is about 76 AU, and most of its orbit is vastly farther away.



It therefore lies dynamically beyond the normal classical Kuiper Belt.



\## Sedna Versus Centaurs



\[\[Centaurs]] occupy unstable orbits among the giant planets.



Sedna never approaches the giant planets.



It is therefore dynamically almost the opposite: an exceptionally detached outer object.



\## Sedna Versus Comets



Sedna likely contains volatile-rich material similar in broad composition to other distant icy planetesimals.



However, it does not approach the Sun closely enough to develop the strong sublimation-driven coma characteristic of ordinary \[\[Comets]].



It is therefore not currently an active comet.



\## Sedna and Solar System Boundary Concepts



Sedna does not mark the physical edge of the Solar System.



The Sun's gravitational influence extends vastly farther.



The \[\[Oort Cloud]] may reach tens of thousands of AU, and the \[\[Heliosphere]] has a completely different boundary determined by the solar wind interacting with interstellar space.



Different definitions of "Solar System edge" therefore refer to different physical phenomena.



\## Formation Location



Sedna may not have formed where it currently orbits.



It probably formed closer to the Sun in the primordial planetesimal disk and was later moved outward.



The exact original distance depends on which dynamical model is correct.



\## Primitive Material



Because Sedna has remained extremely cold, much of its interior may preserve primitive material from the early Solar System.



However, its surface has been exposed to billions of years of radiation.



This means surface chemistry may differ substantially from its original composition.



\## Space Weathering



Sedna's surface is affected by:



Cosmic rays



Ultraviolet radiation



Solar wind, although weak at great distances



Micrometeorite impacts



Radiation can chemically alter ices and create complex organic compounds.



\## Radiation Dose



Although solar radiation weakens greatly with distance, Galactic cosmic rays remain important.



Over billions of years they can penetrate into the surface and alter chemical bonds.



This can create a radiation-processed crust.



\## Surface Renewal



Without strong geological activity, an old radiation-processed surface can remain exposed for extremely long periods.



Impacts or volatile migration could expose fresher material, but whether Sedna experiences significant resurfacing is unknown.



\## Cratering



Sedna is expected to possess impact craters.



However, none can currently be resolved directly.



Crater density would provide valuable information about the population of distant impactors and the age of its surface.



A future spacecraft mission would be required for detailed geological mapping.



\## Potential Mission



Sedna is a scientifically attractive spacecraft target because of its unique dynamical history.



A flyby could determine:



Surface geology



Composition



Mass



Shape



Rotation



Possible atmosphere



Possible moons



Crater population



However, reaching Sedna is extremely difficult.



\## Travel Challenges



Sedna's great distance means a mission would require:



Very high launch energy



Long travel time



Power far from the Sun



Large communication distances



Precise long-term navigation



Solar panels would be inefficient at such distances, making radioisotope power especially valuable.



\## Perihelion Opportunity



Sedna is moving toward perihelion during the twenty-first century and reaches its closest approach to the Sun in the coming decades.



This period offers comparatively favorable geometry for observation and possible mission planning.



After perihelion, it will gradually move outward and become increasingly difficult to reach and observe.



\## Why a Sedna Mission Matters



A close encounter could distinguish between competing formation scenarios by measuring physical properties unavailable from Earth.



If Sedna's surface and composition resemble cold classical Kuiper Belt objects, that may suggest one formation region.



If it resembles different distant populations, another origin may be favored.



Precise mass, density, geology, and crater counts would also help reconstruct the early outer Solar System.



\## Future Surveys



Wide-field telescopic surveys should discover additional Sedna-like objects.



A larger sample is essential because one orbit cannot define an entire population.



Future discoveries can reveal whether detached objects show real clustering, how common they are, and what range of perihelia and inclinations exists.



\## Vera C. Rubin Observatory



The Vera C. Rubin Observatory is expected to identify many new distant Solar System objects through repeated deep imaging.



Its observations should significantly improve statistical tests of:



Detached populations



Extreme TNOs



Orbital clustering



Planet Nine models



Outer Solar System structure



\## James Webb Space Telescope



The \[\[James Webb Space Telescope]] can study infrared spectral features on distant objects such as Sedna.



Infrared observations can help identify surface ices and organic-rich materials that are difficult to characterize in visible wavelengths.



\## Scientific Importance



Sedna is important not because it is the largest or closest distant world, but because of its orbit.



Its perihelion is too far from Neptune for ordinary current planetary scattering to explain it easily.



That means Sedna may preserve evidence of processes no longer strongly visible in today's Solar System.



It could record:



The Sun's birth cluster



A close stellar encounter



Early \[\[Planetary Migration]]



Ancient planetary instability



A lost planet



A currently undiscovered distant planet



or some combination of these effects.



For this reason, Sedna functions as a dynamical fossil of the Solar System's earliest history.



\## Common Misconceptions



Sedna is not a planet. It is currently considered a large detached trans-Neptunian object and dwarf-planet candidate.



Sedna is not the farthest possible object in the Solar System. The Oort Cloud likely extends vastly farther.



Sedna is not an ordinary Kuiper Belt object. Its perihelion lies well beyond the main Kuiper Belt.



Sedna is not controlled by strong present-day encounters with Neptune. This lack of interaction is precisely what makes its orbit scientifically unusual.



The existence of Sedna does not prove Planet Nine. Several mechanisms could generate a Sedna-like orbit.



Sedna is not an active comet. It remains too far from the Sun for ordinary strong cometary activity.



A red surface does not mean Sedna is physically hot. Its red color refers to reflected light and likely radiation-processed chemistry, while its temperature is extremely low.



\## Open Questions



The origin of Sedna's orbit remains unresolved. Scientists do not yet know whether an early stellar encounter, the Sun's birth cluster, planetary migration, an unseen distant planet, or another mechanism was primarily responsible.



The size of the total Sedna-like population is also uncertain because observational bias strongly favors discovering objects near perihelion.



Sedna's exact mass, density, shape, internal structure, and potential satellite system remain poorly known.



Researchers also want to determine whether it retains volatile ices capable of supporting a temporary atmosphere, whether it differentiated internally, and whether subsurface liquid ever existed.



Perhaps most importantly, astronomers need many more detached objects. Their collective orbital distribution will provide much stronger evidence about the architecture and early history of the outer Solar System than Sedna can provide alone.



\## Summary



Sedna is an extremely distant and unusual \[\[Trans-Neptunian Objects|Trans-Neptunian Object]] with an elongated orbit extending from roughly 76 AU at perihelion to hundreds of AU at aphelion.



Its orbital period is approximately 11,000 years.



Unlike typical \[\[Scattered Disc]] objects, Sedna never approaches \[\[Neptune]] closely enough for ordinary present-day planetary encounters to explain its orbit.



This makes it a detached object and possible member of an inner \[\[Oort Cloud]] population.



Several mechanisms may explain its origin, including stellar encounters in the Sun's birth cluster, early \[\[Planetary Migration]], resonance effects, ancient lost planets, or the hypothetical Planet Nine.



Sedna is roughly a thousand kilometers across and has a very red, extremely cold surface likely containing ices and radiation-processed organic-rich materials.



Because its orbit may preserve evidence from the earliest Solar System, Sedna is one of the most important objects for studying the distant boundary between the trans-Neptunian region and the Oort Cloud.
