\# Coronal Mass Ejections



\## Overview



A Coronal Mass Ejection, commonly abbreviated as CME, is a large-scale eruption of plasma and magnetic field from the Sun's corona into interplanetary space.



CMEs can carry enormous quantities of:



\- Plasma

\- Magnetic field

\- Energy

\- Momentum



They are among the most important drivers of severe space weather near Earth.



A CME is different from a solar flare.



A solar flare is primarily a rapid release of electromagnetic and magnetic energy in the solar atmosphere.



A CME is a large physical eruption of magnetized solar plasma into space.



\## Basic Definition



A Coronal Mass Ejection is a large expulsion of magnetized plasma from the solar corona.



The expelled material can travel through the heliosphere and interact with:



\- Planets

\- Magnetospheres

\- Spacecraft

\- Comets

\- The solar wind



\## Where CMEs Originate



CMEs commonly originate in magnetically active regions of the Sun.



They can be associated with:



\- Sunspots

\- Solar flares

\- Filament eruptions

\- Prominence eruptions

\- Magnetic flux ropes

\- Active regions

\- Quiet-Sun filament systems



Not every CME is associated with a strong solar flare.



\## Solar Corona



The corona is the Sun's outer atmosphere.



It consists of very hot, magnetized plasma extending millions of kilometers into space.



CMEs represent major disruptions of the coronal magnetic field.



\## Magnetic Origin of CMEs



The Sun's corona contains magnetic fields that can become:



\- Twisted

\- Sheared

\- Stressed

\- Unstable



Magnetic energy can accumulate over time.



When the configuration becomes unstable, a large magnetic structure may erupt outward.



\## Magnetic Reconnection



Magnetic reconnection is a central process in many CME eruptions.



During reconnection:



\- Magnetic field connectivity changes

\- Magnetic energy is converted into heat and motion

\- Plasma can accelerate

\- Large-scale magnetic structures can be released



Reconnection can occur below an erupting magnetic structure and help accelerate the CME.



\## Magnetic Flux Rope



Many CME models involve a structure called a:



Magnetic flux rope



A flux rope is a bundle of twisted magnetic field lines.



It may contain plasma and can form before or during an eruption.



When destabilized, the flux rope may rise through the corona and become part of the CME.



\## CME Structure



A CME can contain:



\- Magnetized plasma

\- Compressed plasma

\- Magnetic flux ropes

\- Shock fronts

\- Dense plasma structures

\- Cavities

\- Prominence material



Its exact structure varies from event to event.



\## Classical Three-Part CME Structure



Some CMEs observed in coronagraph images show a three-part structure:



\- Bright leading front

\- Dark cavity

\- Bright core



The bright core can be associated with erupted prominence or filament material.



Not all CMEs display this textbook structure.



\## CME Leading Edge



The leading edge is the outermost visible boundary of the expanding CME.



It may compress surrounding solar-wind plasma as it moves outward.



\## CME Cavity



Some CMEs contain a darker region behind the bright front.



This cavity may correspond to the volume occupied by a magnetic flux rope.



\## CME Core



The bright core seen in some eruptions may contain dense prominence material.



\## Prominences and CMEs



A solar prominence is relatively cool, dense plasma suspended in the corona by magnetic fields.



When a prominence becomes unstable, it may erupt outward.



Prominence eruptions are frequently associated with CMEs.



\## Filaments and CMEs



A filament is the same type of structure as a prominence, but viewed against the bright solar disk.



A filament eruption can trigger or accompany a CME.



\## CME Versus Solar Flare



Solar flare:



\- Rapid energy release

\- Strong electromagnetic radiation

\- Often localized in an active region

\- Radiation reaches Earth in about eight minutes



CME:



\- Large eruption of magnetized plasma

\- Travels through space much more slowly than light

\- May take one to several days to reach Earth

\- Can strongly disturb Earth's magnetosphere



They are related but distinct phenomena.



\## CME Versus Solar Wind



Solar wind:



Continuous plasma flow from the Sun.



CME:



Large transient eruption superimposed on the background solar wind.



A CME can move faster than the surrounding solar wind and create a shock.



\## CME Versus Solar Energetic Particle Event



CME:



Large plasma and magnetic-field eruption.



Solar Energetic Particle event:



Increase in high-energy charged particles.



Fast CMEs can drive shocks that accelerate energetic particles.



\## CME Mass



A typical CME can contain an enormous mass of plasma.



Values can vary greatly, but large events may carry around:



10^12 to 10^13 kilograms



of material.



Exact estimates depend on observation and geometry.



\## CME Energy



The kinetic energy of a large CME can reach roughly:



10^23 to 10^25 joules



depending on mass and speed.



Additional energy is contained in magnetic fields and thermal plasma.



\## CME Speed



CMEs can travel at very different speeds.



Slow CMEs may move at:



A few hundred kilometers per second



Fast CMEs can exceed:



1,000 km/s



The fastest events can travel above:



2,000 km/s



\## CME Travel to Earth



Earth is approximately:



1 Astronomical Unit



from the Sun.



Depending on CME speed and interplanetary conditions, an Earth-directed CME may arrive in roughly:



One to several days



Very fast CMEs can arrive much sooner than slow ones.



\## CME Propagation



As a CME travels away from the Sun, it interacts with the surrounding solar wind.



Its motion is influenced by:



\- Background solar-wind speed

\- Magnetic fields

\- Plasma density

\- Previous CMEs

\- Interplanetary structures



\## CME Expansion



CMEs expand as they move outward.



Their physical size can become enormous by the time they reach Earth's orbit.



A CME can span millions to tens of millions of kilometers.



\## CME Direction



The direction of a CME is critical for determining whether it will affect Earth.



A strong CME traveling away from Earth may have little direct geomagnetic impact.



An Earth-directed CME can be highly geoeffective.



\## Earth-Directed CME



An Earth-directed CME is one whose trajectory intersects or passes close to Earth.



It can produce:



\- Interplanetary shocks

\- Solar-wind compression

\- Geomagnetic storms

\- Aurora

\- Satellite disturbances



\## Halo CME



A halo CME appears to surround the occulting disk of a coronagraph.



This appearance can occur when the CME moves approximately toward or away from the observer.



From an Earth-viewing spacecraft, a full halo CME can indicate an eruption directed roughly toward or away from Earth.



Additional observations are needed to determine direction.



\## Partial Halo CME



A partial halo CME surrounds only part of the coronagraph's occulting disk.



It may still be Earth-directed depending on geometry.



\## Coronagraph



A coronagraph blocks the bright visible disk of the Sun so the faint corona can be observed.



Coronagraphs are essential for detecting and tracking CMEs near the Sun.



\## White-Light CME Observation



CMEs are commonly observed in white light through sunlight scattered by free electrons in the plasma.



This process is called:



Thomson scattering



\## Thomson Scattering



Thomson scattering is the scattering of electromagnetic radiation by free charged particles, especially electrons.



Coronagraph images of CMEs often depend on this scattered sunlight.



\## CME Observation Geometry



The apparent brightness and shape of a CME depend strongly on viewing angle.



A CME seen from the side is often easier to measure than one traveling directly toward the observer.



This creates uncertainty in speed and direction estimates.



\## Plane-of-Sky Speed



Coronagraph images often measure CME motion projected onto the plane of the sky.



This may differ from the true three-dimensional speed.



\## Projection Effects



Projection effects can make a CME appear:



\- Slower

\- Faster

\- Wider

\- Narrower



than its actual three-dimensional properties.



Multiple viewpoints can reduce this uncertainty.



\## STEREO Mission



The Solar Terrestrial Relations Observatory, or:



STEREO



was designed to observe the Sun and CMEs from different viewpoints.



Multiple perspectives help reconstruct CME:



\- Direction

\- Shape

\- Speed

\- Propagation



\## LASCO



LASCO stands for:



Large Angle and Spectrometric Coronagraph



It operates aboard the SOHO spacecraft.



LASCO has provided a large catalog of CME observations.



\## SOHO



SOHO stands for:



Solar and Heliospheric Observatory



It studies the Sun and solar environment.



Its coronagraphs have played a major role in CME research.



\## Solar Orbiter



Solar Orbiter studies the Sun using remote-sensing and in-situ instruments.



Its observations contribute to understanding:



\- Solar magnetic fields

\- Eruptions

\- Solar-wind sources

\- Energetic particles



\## Parker Solar Probe



Parker Solar Probe travels close to the Sun and measures plasma and magnetic fields directly.



Its data help scientists understand the early evolution of CMEs and the surrounding solar wind.



\## CME Detection



CMEs can be detected using:



\- Coronagraphs

\- Extreme ultraviolet imaging

\- Radio observations

\- Heliospheric imagers

\- In-situ plasma instruments

\- Magnetometers



\## Extreme Ultraviolet Imaging



Before a CME becomes visible in a coronagraph, extreme ultraviolet images may show:



\- Filament eruption

\- Coronal dimming

\- Flare loops

\- Expanding magnetic structures



\## Coronal Dimming



After some eruptions, regions of the corona become temporarily darker in extreme ultraviolet images.



This is called:



Coronal dimming



It can indicate that plasma has been removed during a CME.



\## EUV Wave



Some major eruptions produce large-scale wave-like disturbances across the solar corona.



These are often called:



EUV waves



They can be associated with rapidly expanding CMEs.



\## CME Shock



A fast CME moving faster than the characteristic wave speeds in surrounding plasma can generate a shock.



This is called:



CME-driven shock



\## Interplanetary Shock



As the CME propagates through the solar wind, its shock can travel far into interplanetary space.



When such a shock reaches Earth, solar-wind parameters can change suddenly.



\## Sudden Commencement



The arrival of an interplanetary shock can compress Earth's magnetosphere.



This can produce a sudden increase in Earth's magnetic field called a:



Sudden storm commencement



or:



Sudden impulse



depending on event characteristics.



\## CME Sheath Region



The region between a CME-driven shock and the main CME ejecta often contains compressed and turbulent solar-wind plasma.



This is called the:



Sheath region



It can itself be strongly geoeffective.



\## Magnetic Cloud



Some interplanetary CMEs display a structure called a:



Magnetic cloud



Typical characteristics include:



\- Smooth magnetic-field rotation

\- Strong magnetic field

\- Low proton temperature

\- Organized magnetic structure



Magnetic clouds are often interpreted as flux ropes.



\## ICME



When a CME is observed in interplanetary space, it is often called an:



Interplanetary Coronal Mass Ejection



or:



ICME



The term distinguishes the eruption near the Sun from its evolved form in the solar wind.



\## CME to ICME Evolution



Near Sun:



CME



Farther through interplanetary space:



ICME



The structure evolves due to:



\- Expansion

\- Interaction with solar wind

\- Compression

\- Reconnection

\- Turbulence



\## Geomagnetic Storm



A geomagnetic storm is a large disturbance of Earth's magnetosphere.



Strong Earth-directed CMEs are major causes of severe geomagnetic storms.



\## Geoeffectiveness



A CME's ability to produce a geomagnetic storm depends on more than its speed.



Important factors include:



\- CME direction

\- Magnetic-field strength

\- Magnetic-field orientation

\- Solar-wind pressure

\- Duration

\- Sheath properties



\## Southward Magnetic Field



Earth's magnetic field near the dayside magnetopause points mainly northward.



When the interplanetary magnetic field carried by a CME has a strong southward component, magnetic reconnection with Earth's field becomes more efficient.



This can greatly increase geomagnetic activity.



\## Bz Component



The north-south component of the interplanetary magnetic field is commonly represented as:



Bz



A strongly negative Bz corresponds to a southward magnetic field in commonly used geocentric coordinates.



Strong and sustained southward Bz is an important condition for major geomagnetic storms.



\## Magnetic Reconnection at Earth



When CME magnetic fields interact favorably with Earth's field:



\- Magnetic reconnection occurs

\- Solar-wind energy enters the magnetosphere

\- Magnetotail energy increases

\- Particle precipitation increases

\- Auroras intensify



\## Magnetosphere Compression



A fast CME can increase solar-wind dynamic pressure.



This can push Earth's magnetopause inward.



During severe storms, satellites in high orbits may temporarily become more exposed to disturbed solar-wind conditions.



\## Aurora



CMEs can produce intense auroral displays.



During strong geomagnetic storms, auroras may become visible at much lower latitudes than usual.



\## Aurora Borealis



The northern aurora is called:



Aurora Borealis



\## Aurora Australis



The southern aurora is called:



Aurora Australis



\## CME and Power Grids



Large geomagnetic storms caused by CMEs can induce electric currents in long conductors.



These are called:



Geomagnetically Induced Currents



or:



GICs



\## Geomagnetically Induced Currents



GICs can flow through:



\- Power transmission lines

\- Pipelines

\- Ground systems

\- Transformers



Strong currents can interfere with electrical infrastructure.



\## Power Transformer Effects



Geomagnetically induced currents can cause:



\- Transformer heating

\- Voltage instability

\- Protective system activation

\- Equipment stress



Severe space-weather events can therefore threaten power-grid reliability.



\## CME and Satellites



CMEs can affect satellites through:



\- Magnetosphere compression

\- Increased radiation

\- Surface charging

\- Internal charging

\- Communication disturbances

\- Orbital drag changes



\## Satellite Charging



Energetic particles and disturbed plasma environments can accumulate electric charge on spacecraft surfaces.



This can produce:



\- Discharges

\- Electronic interference

\- Component damage



\## Internal Charging



High-energy particles can penetrate spacecraft materials and deposit charge inside components.



This can lead to electrical anomalies.



\## Satellite Drag



Geomagnetic storms heat Earth's upper atmosphere.



The atmosphere expands upward.



Low-Earth-orbit satellites then encounter increased atmospheric density.



This increases:



Orbital drag



\## Orbital Decay



Increased drag can temporarily accelerate orbital decay of low-Earth-orbit spacecraft.



Satellite operators may need to adjust orbit predictions or perform maneuvers.



\## GPS and Navigation



Geomagnetic storms can disturb Earth's ionosphere.



This can affect signals from:



\- GPS

\- Galileo

\- Other navigation systems



Possible effects include:



\- Position errors

\- Signal scintillation

\- Loss of lock



\## Ionospheric Scintillation



Rapid variations in ionospheric electron density can cause fluctuations in radio-signal amplitude and phase.



This is called:



Scintillation



It can affect satellite communication and navigation.



\## Radio Communication



CME-driven geomagnetic storms can alter ionospheric conditions.



This may disrupt:



\- HF communication

\- Satellite communication

\- Radar systems



\## Aviation



Severe space weather can affect aviation through:



\- Radio communication problems

\- Navigation errors

\- Radiation exposure on polar routes



\## Astronaut Radiation Risk



CMEs can be associated with strong solar energetic particle events.



These particles can pose significant risks to astronauts outside Earth's protective atmosphere.



\## Lunar Missions



The Moon lacks a thick atmosphere and global Earth-like magnetic shield.



Astronauts on the Moon can therefore be exposed to strong solar particle events.



Radiation shelters are important for long-duration lunar exploration.



\## Mars Missions



Mars has a thin atmosphere and lacks a strong global magnetic field.



Solar energetic particles associated with major eruptions can increase radiation exposure for:



\- Surface crews

\- Orbiting spacecraft



\## CME Interaction With Mercury



Mercury has a global magnetic field but a small magnetosphere.



Strong solar eruptions can compress Mercury's magnetosphere significantly.



\## CME Interaction With Venus



Venus lacks an intrinsic global magnetic field.



Its atmosphere interacts directly with solar-wind plasma through an induced magnetosphere.



CMEs can strongly modify this interaction.



\## CME Interaction With Mars



Mars has localized crustal magnetic fields rather than a strong global dipole.



CMEs can enhance:



\- Atmospheric escape

\- Ionospheric disturbances

\- Particle precipitation



\## CME Interaction With Jupiter



Jupiter has a huge magnetosphere dominated strongly by internal processes and plasma from Io.



Solar-wind disturbances, including CMEs, can still compress and modify its outer magnetosphere.



\## CME Interaction With Saturn



Saturn's magnetosphere responds to changes in solar-wind pressure and magnetic fields.



CMEs can influence its magnetospheric dynamics.



\## Outer Planet CMEs



As CMEs travel outward, they expand and evolve.



By the time they reach the outer Solar System, their structure can be much broader and more altered.



\## CME and Comets



CMEs can interact dramatically with comet plasma tails.



Magnetic-field changes can cause:



\- Tail disconnection events

\- Plasma-tail distortion



Comets act as natural probes of solar-wind disturbances.



\## Comet Tail Disconnection Event



A comet's ion tail can sometimes detach from the comet.



Magnetic reconnection caused by changing solar-wind conditions, including CME passage, can contribute to these events.



\## CME and the Heliosphere



CMEs travel through the heliosphere.



Their effects can propagate far beyond Earth's orbit.



Multiple CMEs can merge and create large-scale interplanetary disturbances.



\## CME-CME Interaction



A fast CME can catch up with a slower CME launched earlier.



The two may interact.



This can alter:



\- Speed

\- Magnetic structure

\- Shock strength

\- Arrival time



\## Cannibal CME



When one fast CME overtakes and merges with another, the event is sometimes informally called a:



Cannibal CME



Such interactions can create especially complex space-weather conditions.



\## Preconditioning of the Solar Wind



A previous CME can modify the solar wind before another CME arrives.



This may reduce drag or change magnetic conditions.



As a result, later CMEs can propagate differently than expected.



\## CME Drag



CMEs interact with the background solar wind.



A fast CME generally tends to slow down.



A slow CME may accelerate toward the ambient solar-wind speed.



This process is often described using:



Aerodynamic-like drag models



although the physical medium is plasma.



\## Drag-Based Models



Drag-based models estimate CME arrival times by treating propagation as controlled partly by interaction with surrounding solar wind.



They are useful for operational space-weather forecasting.



\## CME Arrival Prediction



Predicting CME arrival at Earth requires estimates of:



\- Launch time

\- Speed

\- Direction

\- Width

\- Solar-wind conditions



Uncertainty remains significant.



\## Arrival-Time Error



Forecast arrival times can differ from actual arrival by several hours or more.



Complex interactions can increase uncertainty.



\## CME Magnetic Field Prediction



Predicting whether an arriving CME will contain strong southward magnetic field is more difficult than predicting its arrival.



This is one of the most important challenges in space-weather forecasting.



\## Why Magnetic Orientation Matters



A fast CME with mostly northward magnetic field may produce a weaker geomagnetic storm than a somewhat slower CME with a strong sustained southward field.



Therefore:



Speed alone does not determine storm severity.



\## Space Weather Forecasting



Space-weather forecasts combine information from:



\- Solar images

\- Coronagraphs

\- Heliospheric imagers

\- Radio bursts

\- Solar energetic particles

\- Solar-wind measurements

\- Magnetometers



\## L1 Space Weather Monitoring



Spacecraft near the Sun-Earth L1 point measure the solar wind before it reaches Earth.



These observations can provide tens of minutes of warning before an interplanetary disturbance reaches Earth's magnetosphere.



\## Sun-Earth L1



The Sun-Earth L1 point lies approximately:



1.5 million kilometers



sunward of Earth.



Space-weather spacecraft located near this region can sample approaching solar-wind disturbances.



\## DSCOVR



DSCOVR monitors solar-wind plasma and magnetic fields near the Sun-Earth L1 region.



Its data are important for near-real-time space-weather monitoring.



\## ACE



The Advanced Composition Explorer, or:



ACE



also measures solar-wind and energetic-particle conditions near L1.



\## WIND



The WIND spacecraft studies:



\- Solar wind

\- Plasma

\- Magnetic fields

\- Radio waves



Its measurements contribute to space-weather and heliophysics research.



\## CME Radio Bursts



Fast CMEs can produce shock-related radio emissions.



One important type is:



Type II radio burst



\## Type II Radio Burst



Type II solar radio bursts are associated with shock waves traveling through the solar corona and interplanetary medium.



They can indicate that a fast CME-driven shock is present.



\## Type III Radio Burst



Type III radio bursts are produced by beams of energetic electrons traveling through solar plasma.



They are often associated with flare and eruptive activity.



\## Type IV Radio Burst



Type IV radio emission can be associated with energetic electrons trapped in expanding magnetic structures.



These emissions provide additional information about solar eruptions.



\## CME Acceleration



CMEs can accelerate strongly in the low corona.



Acceleration is often associated with magnetic forces during eruption.



Fast acceleration can occur over relatively short timescales.



\## Lorentz Force



The Lorentz force acting on electric currents and magnetic fields is important in CME dynamics.



Magnetic pressure and magnetic tension can help drive the eruption outward.



\## Magnetic Pressure



Strong magnetic fields store energy and exert pressure.



Changes in magnetic pressure can contribute to CME acceleration.



\## Magnetic Tension



Curved magnetic field lines exert a restoring force known as magnetic tension.



The balance between tension and other forces influences the stability of magnetic structures.



\## CME Initiation Models



Several physical models have been proposed for CME initiation.



Examples include:



\- Flux-rope instability

\- Tether-cutting reconnection

\- Breakout reconnection

\- Torus instability

\- Kink instability



These models are not mutually exclusive in all events.



\## Tether-Cutting Model



In the tether-cutting model, reconnection within strongly sheared magnetic fields can weaken the magnetic structures holding down an erupting flux rope.



This allows the structure to rise.



\## Breakout Model



The breakout model involves reconnection above or around a stressed magnetic system.



This reconnection removes overlying magnetic restraint and allows eruption.



\## Torus Instability



Torus instability can occur when the overlying magnetic field decreases sufficiently rapidly with height.



A current-carrying flux rope may then become unstable and accelerate outward.



\## Kink Instability



A strongly twisted magnetic flux rope can become unstable to a helical deformation called:



Kink instability



This can contribute to eruption in some cases.



\## Confined Eruption



Some magnetic structures begin to rise but fail to escape into interplanetary space.



These are called:



Confined eruptions



A strong overlying magnetic field can prevent a full CME.



\## Failed Eruption



A failed eruption occurs when an erupting filament or magnetic structure rises but does not escape the Sun.



Not every filament eruption becomes a CME.



\## CME Width



CMEs vary greatly in angular width.



They can be:



\- Narrow

\- Partial halo

\- Full halo



Wide CMEs generally involve larger-scale coronal disturbances.



\## Narrow CME



A narrow CME occupies a relatively small angular region in coronagraph images.



Some narrow events are associated with:



Jets



or localized eruptions.



\## Solar Jets



Solar jets are narrow, collimated plasma eruptions.



Some can produce small CME-like disturbances.



They are generally much narrower than major CMEs.



\## Stealth CME



A stealth CME is a CME with weak or difficult-to-identify low-coronal signatures.



It may lack an obvious:



\- Strong flare

\- Clear filament eruption

\- Bright EUV disturbance



Stealth CMEs can complicate forecasting.



\## Slow CME



Slow CMEs may travel near the background solar-wind speed.



They can still produce geomagnetic effects if their magnetic field orientation is favorable.



\## Fast CME



Fast CMEs can:



\- Drive strong shocks

\- Accelerate energetic particles

\- Reach Earth more quickly

\- Cause strong space-weather events



\## CME Frequency



CME occurrence changes with the solar activity cycle.



Near solar maximum:



CMEs occur more frequently.



Near solar minimum:



They are less frequent.



\## Solar Maximum



Solar maximum is associated with:



\- More sunspots

\- More active regions

\- More flares

\- More CMEs



\## Solar Minimum



Solar minimum is generally quieter.



However, CMEs can still occur.



\## CME Latitude



During solar maximum, CMEs can originate over a wide range of solar latitudes.



Near solar minimum, eruptions are more concentrated around regions associated with the solar activity belt.



\## Solar Cycle



The Sun's activity cycle lasts approximately:



11 years



CME rate generally follows this cycle.



\## 22-Year Magnetic Cycle



The Sun's magnetic polarity reverses during each approximately 11-year solar cycle.



Two cycles return the large-scale polarity to the same orientation.



This creates an approximately:



22-year magnetic cycle



\## Historic CME Events



Several major historical space-weather events are associated with powerful solar eruptions and CMEs.



Important examples include:



\- Carrington Event

\- March 1989 geomagnetic storm

\- Halloween Storms of 2003



\## Carrington Event



The Carrington Event occurred in:



1859



It included a powerful solar eruption followed by an extreme geomagnetic storm.



Effects included:



\- Intense auroras

\- Telegraph disturbances



It is widely used as a benchmark for severe space-weather risk.



\## 1989 Quebec Power Failure



In March 1989, a major geomagnetic storm caused the collapse of the Hydro-Québec power grid.



The storm was associated with strong solar activity and interplanetary disturbances.



It demonstrated the vulnerability of electrical infrastructure to space weather.



\## Halloween Storms



In October and November 2003, a series of strong solar eruptions produced:



\- Powerful flares

\- CMEs

\- Solar energetic particles

\- Geomagnetic storms



These events affected satellites, communications, and technological systems.



\## CME and Solar Energetic Particle Acceleration



Fast CME-driven shocks can accelerate particles to high energies.



These particles can travel through interplanetary space.



Large events can create radiation hazards for spacecraft and astronauts.



\## Shock Acceleration



Particles can gain energy by repeatedly interacting with turbulent magnetic fields around a shock.



This process can be related to:



Diffusive shock acceleration



\## Energetic Protons



High-energy protons are especially important for radiation hazards.



They can penetrate spacecraft shielding and biological tissue.



\## Ground Level Enhancement



In rare extreme solar energetic particle events, particles can reach energies high enough to produce detectable increases in secondary particles at Earth's surface.



These events are called:



Ground Level Enhancements



or:



GLEs



\## CME and Cosmic Rays



Strong CME structures and shocks can temporarily reduce the intensity of galactic cosmic rays measured near Earth.



This phenomenon is called:



Forbush decrease



\## Forbush Decrease



A Forbush decrease is a temporary reduction in galactic cosmic-ray intensity following the passage of an interplanetary disturbance such as an ICME.



The reduction can last for days.



\## CME Magnetic Topology



The magnetic structure of a CME can remain organized over very large distances.



Flux-rope orientation is especially important for geomagnetic effects.



\## Flux Rope Rotation



A CME's magnetic flux rope may rotate during eruption or propagation.



This can change its magnetic orientation before reaching Earth.



\## CME Deflection



CMEs do not always travel radially outward.



They can be deflected by:



\- Coronal holes

\- Large-scale magnetic fields

\- Nearby active regions

\- Solar-wind structures



\## Coronal Hole Influence



Coronal holes are regions of open magnetic field that produce high-speed solar wind.



Their magnetic pressure can influence CME trajectories.



\## CME Compression



When a CME moves rapidly through the solar wind, plasma can accumulate ahead of it.



This compressed region can increase:



\- Density

\- Temperature

\- Magnetic-field strength



\## CME Heating



Shock compression and turbulence can heat plasma in the sheath.



\## CME Turbulence



CME sheath regions can contain strong plasma and magnetic turbulence.



This can influence:



\- Particle transport

\- Geomagnetic activity

\- Shock structure



\## Magnetohydrodynamics



CMEs are often modeled using:



Magnetohydrodynamics



or:



MHD



MHD describes the behavior of electrically conducting plasma interacting with magnetic fields.



\## CME Simulations



Computer simulations are used to model:



\- CME initiation

\- Magnetic reconnection

\- Propagation

\- Shock formation

\- Interaction with the solar wind

\- Arrival at planets



\## Global Heliospheric Models



Large-scale numerical models simulate CMEs moving through the heliosphere.



These models help forecast:



\- Arrival time

\- Speed

\- Density

\- Shock conditions



\## ENLIL Model



ENLIL is a heliospheric model commonly used to simulate solar-wind and CME propagation through interplanetary space.



It is used in operational space-weather forecasting.



\## Cone Models



Simplified CME models can approximate the eruption as a cone expanding through the heliosphere.



These models estimate:



\- Width

\- Direction

\- Speed



\## Flux-Rope Models



More advanced models represent CME magnetic structure explicitly.



These can potentially improve predictions of magnetic-field orientation.



\## CME Forecasting Challenge



The biggest operational challenge is often not determining whether a CME will arrive.



It is predicting:



The magnetic field it will carry when it arrives.



\## Magnetic Field Measurement Limitation



Near the Sun, direct measurements of the coronal magnetic field are difficult.



Scientists often infer magnetic structure from:



\- Photospheric magnetograms

\- EUV images

\- Filaments

\- Coronal loops



\## In-Situ Measurement



When a CME reaches a spacecraft, instruments can directly measure:



\- Plasma density

\- Temperature

\- Speed

\- Magnetic field

\- Particle populations



These are called:



In-situ measurements



\## Remote Sensing Versus In-Situ



Remote sensing:



Observes the CME from a distance using images and radiation.



In-situ:



Measures the plasma directly as it passes the spacecraft.



Both approaches are required for a complete understanding.



\## CME at Other Planets



Spacecraft around other planets can detect CME-related disturbances.



This allows scientists to study solar eruptions at different distances from the Sun.



\## Multipoint Measurements



When multiple spacecraft at different locations observe the same CME, scientists can reconstruct its evolution across the heliosphere.



This is called:



Multipoint observation



\## CME and Planetary Atmospheres



Strong solar eruptions can increase atmospheric escape from planets lacking strong magnetic protection.



This is especially important for:



\- Mars

\- Venus

\- Exoplanets around active stars



\## Atmospheric Escape



Atmospheric escape is the loss of atmospheric particles into space.



CMEs can increase:



\- Ionization

\## CME and Exoplanets



Other stars also produce large magnetic eruptions.

Red dwarf stars can be highly magnetically active.

\## Kp Index



The Kp index measures global geomagnetic activity.



It ranges from:



0 to 9

\## Dst Index



The Dst index measures changes in Earth's equatorial magnetic field associated primarily with the magnetospheric ring current.

\## Ring Current



The ring current is an electric current carried by energetic charged particles encircling Earth.



During geomagnetic storms, the ring current can intensify substantially.

\## Common Misconceptions



\### A CME Is the Same as a Solar Flare



Incorrect.



A flare is a rapid energy and radiation release.



A CME is a massive eruption of plasma and magnetic field.



\### Every CME Hits Earth



Incorrect.



Most CMEs travel in directions that do not intersect Earth.



\### Every Earth-Directed CME Causes a Severe Storm



Incorrect.



Geomagnetic impact depends strongly on magnetic-field orientation and other properties.



\### CME Radiation Reaches Earth in Eight Minutes



Incorrect.



Electromagnetic radiation from solar flares reaches Earth in about eight minutes.



CME plasma generally requires many hours to several days.



\### CMEs Are Made of Fire



Incorrect.



They consist primarily of:



\- Plasma

\- Magnetic fields



\### The Fastest CME Always Produces the Strongest Storm



Incorrect.



Magnetic orientation, especially sustained southward Bz, is crucial.



\### Solar Flares Cause Power-Grid Currents Directly



Oversimplified.



Power-grid disturbances are mainly associated with geomagnetic storms caused by solar-wind and magnetic-field disturbances such as CMEs.



\### Every CME Produces Aurora Everywhere



Incorrect.



Auroral visibility depends on storm intensity, latitude, local conditions, and darkness.



\### Earth's Magnetic Field Blocks All CME Effects



Incorrect.



Earth's magnetosphere provides substantial protection, but strong solar-wind energy can still enter through magnetic reconnection.



\### CMEs Only Affect Earth



Incorrect.



They propagate throughout the heliosphere and interact with many planets, moons, spacecraft, and comets.



\## Important Terminology



\### Coronal Mass Ejection



Large eruption of magnetized plasma from the solar corona.



\### CME



Abbreviation for Coronal Mass Ejection.



\### ICME



Interplanetary Coronal Mass Ejection.



\### Magnetic Flux Rope



Twisted bundle of magnetic field lines often associated with CME structure.



\### CME Shock



Shock wave driven by a fast CME.



\### Sheath



Compressed turbulent plasma between a CME-driven shock and magnetic ejecta.



\### Magnetic Cloud



Organized ICME structure with smooth magnetic-field rotation and low proton temperature.



\### Halo CME



CME appearing to surround the coronagraph's occulting disk.



\### Earth-Directed CME



CME traveling toward Earth's location.



\### Geoeffective



Capable of significantly disturbing Earth's space environment.



\### Bz



North-south component of the interplanetary magnetic field.



\### Coronal Dimming



Temporary decrease in EUV brightness associated with plasma evacuation during an eruption.



\### Solar Energetic Particles



High-energy charged particles associated with solar activity.



\### Geomagnetic Storm



Large disturbance of Earth's magnetosphere.



\### Forbush Decrease



Temporary reduction in galactic cosmic-ray intensity caused by an interplanetary disturbance.



\### Thomson Scattering



Scattering of sunlight by free electrons, important for observing CMEs in white light.



\### Magnetohydrodynamics



Physics of magnetized electrically conducting plasma.



\## CME Versus Solar Flare



CME:



Large eruption of plasma and magnetic field.



Solar flare:



Rapid release of radiation and magnetic energy.



\## CME Versus Solar Wind



CME:



Transient eruption.



Solar wind:



Continuous background plasma flow.



\## CME Versus Prominence



CME:



Large outward eruption into space.



Prominence:



Dense plasma structure suspended in the corona.



A prominence may erupt and become part of a CME.



\## CME Versus Geomagnetic Storm



CME:



Solar/interplanetary phenomenon.



Geomagnetic storm:



Response of Earth's magnetosphere.

Coronal Mass Ejections connect activity on the Sun with environments throughout the Solar System.

\## Summary



A Coronal Mass Ejection is a large eruption of magnetized plasma from the Sun's corona.



CMEs are driven by changes and instabilities in solar magnetic fields.



Magnetic reconnection plays a major role in many eruptions.



CMEs can contain:



\- Plasma

\- Magnetic flux ropes

\- Shock waves

\- Prominence material



They can travel from a few hundred to more than two thousand kilometers per second.



An Earth-directed CME may take roughly one to several days to reach Earth.



Fast CMEs can drive shocks and accelerate energetic particles.



When a CME reaches Earth, its impact depends strongly on:



\- Speed

\- Density

\- Magnetic-field strength

\- Magnetic orientation



A strong sustained southward magnetic field can produce efficient magnetic reconnection with Earth's magnetosphere and cause a severe geomagnetic storm.



Possible effects include:



\- Aurora

\- Satellite disturbances

\- Navigation errors

\- Radio disruption

\- Increased atmospheric drag

\- Geomagnetically induced currents



CMEs are different from solar flares.



Flare electromagnetic radiation reaches Earth in about eight minutes, while CME plasma takes much longer.



CMEs are observed using:



\- Coronagraphs

\- EUV imagers

\- Radio instruments

\- Heliospheric imagers

\- In-situ spacecraft



Predicting CME arrival has improved substantially, but predicting the magnetic-field orientation before arrival remains a major challenge.



CMEs are central to understanding solar activity, space weather, heliospheric plasma physics, planetary atmospheres, and the safety of future human exploration beyond Earth.
