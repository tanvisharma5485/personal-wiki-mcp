\# Solar Magnetic Field



\## Overview



The solar magnetic field is the magnetic field generated by the Sun's electrically conducting plasma.



It controls a large fraction of solar activity and strongly influences:



\- Sunspots

\- Solar flares

\- Coronal Mass Ejections

\- Coronal loops

\- Solar wind

\- Coronal holes

\- Solar energetic particles

\- Space weather

\- The heliosphere



The solar magnetic field is dynamic rather than fixed.



It changes continuously because the Sun is made of moving plasma.



\## Origin of the Solar Magnetic Field



The Sun's magnetic field is generated by a process known as the:



Solar dynamo



The solar dynamo operates because the Sun contains:



\- Electrically conducting plasma

\- Differential rotation

\- Convection

\- Large-scale plasma circulation



These motions stretch, twist, transport, and regenerate magnetic fields.



\## Solar Dynamo



A dynamo converts kinetic energy from plasma motion into magnetic energy.



In the Sun, important ingredients include:



\- Rotation

\- Differential rotation

\- Convection

\- Meridional circulation

\- Magnetic flux transport



The solar dynamo produces the magnetic activity cycle observed at the solar surface.



\## Differential Rotation



The Sun does not rotate as a rigid solid body.



Different latitudes rotate at different rates.



The equatorial regions rotate faster than higher latitudes.



This is called:



Differential rotation



Differential rotation can stretch magnetic fields in the east-west direction.



\## Convection



The outer part of the Sun contains a convection zone.



Hot plasma rises while cooler plasma sinks.



These turbulent motions interact strongly with magnetic fields.



\## Convection Zone



The convection zone is the outer region of the solar interior where energy is transported mainly by convective motion.



It extends from deep inside the Sun to just below the photosphere.



Magnetic fields generated or transported in this region can eventually emerge at the surface.



\## Tachocline



The tachocline is a transition region between:



\- The differentially rotating convection zone

\- The more uniformly rotating radiative interior



It is often considered important in solar dynamo theory.



Strong rotational shear occurs there.



\## Magnetic Field Generation



Solar magnetic fields are created and modified by motion of electrically conducting plasma.



Important processes include:



\- Stretching

\- Twisting

\- Folding

\- Advection

\- Magnetic diffusion

\- Reconnection



\## Magnetic Flux



Magnetic flux measures the amount of magnetic field passing through a surface.



The Sun continuously produces, transports, concentrates, and disperses magnetic flux.



\## Magnetic Flux Tubes



Magnetic fields can become concentrated into structures often described as:



Magnetic flux tubes



These structures can rise through the convection zone due to magnetic buoyancy.



When they reach the photosphere, they may produce:



\- Sunspots

\- Active regions

\- Magnetic loops



\## Magnetic Buoyancy



A strong magnetic structure can become less dense than surrounding plasma.



It may then rise upward through the convection zone.



This process is called:



Magnetic buoyancy



\## Emergence of Magnetic Flux



When magnetic field rises through the photosphere, the process is called:



Magnetic flux emergence



Flux emergence can create:



\- Pores

\- Sunspots

\- Bipolar active regions

\- Coronal loops



\## Bipolar Active Regions



Many active regions contain two dominant magnetic polarities.



One region has magnetic field directed outward.



The other has magnetic field directed inward.



These form:



Bipolar magnetic regions



\## Magnetic Polarity



Magnetic polarity describes the direction of the magnetic field relative to the solar surface.



Positive polarity:



Field directed outward.



Negative polarity:



Field directed inward.



\## Sunspots and Magnetic Fields



Sunspots are regions where magnetic fields become especially concentrated.



Their fields can reach strengths of:



Several thousand gauss



Strong magnetic fields suppress normal convective heat transport, making sunspots cooler and darker than the surrounding photosphere.



\## Active Regions



Active regions are areas containing strong and complex magnetic fields.



They can produce:



\- Sunspots

\- Solar flares

\- CMEs

\- Coronal loops

\- Filaments



\## Magnetic Complexity



Some active regions have simple magnetic structures.



Others contain:



\- Strong gradients

\- Mixed polarities

\- Twisted fields

\- Sheared fields



Greater magnetic complexity is associated statistically with increased flare potential.



\## Polarity Inversion Line



A polarity inversion line is a boundary separating opposite magnetic polarities.



These regions are important because magnetic fields can become strongly sheared along them.



Major solar eruptions often occur near complex polarity inversion lines.



\## Magnetic Shear



Magnetic shear occurs when magnetic fields are strongly distorted relative to a simpler configuration.



Sheared magnetic fields can store:



Free magnetic energy



\## Free Magnetic Energy



Free magnetic energy is magnetic energy available above a minimum-energy reference state.



This stored energy can power:



\- Solar flares

\- CMEs

\- Plasma heating

\- Particle acceleration



\## Potential Magnetic Field



A potential field is an idealized magnetic configuration containing no electric currents in the modeled volume.



It represents a relatively low-energy state.



Real active regions can depart strongly from this condition.



\## Non-Potential Magnetic Field



A non-potential field contains electric currents and stored magnetic free energy.



Strongly non-potential fields are important in flare-producing regions.



\## Electric Currents



Moving charged particles produce electric currents.



In solar plasma, electric currents interact with magnetic fields and can store or dissipate energy.



\## Lorentz Force



The force acting on charged particles and current-carrying plasma in magnetic fields is related to the:



Lorentz force



It is fundamental to solar magnetic dynamics.



\## Magnetic Pressure



Magnetic fields exert pressure.



Strong magnetic pressure can influence plasma motion and atmospheric structure.



\## Magnetic Tension



Curved magnetic field lines behave as if they possess tension.



Magnetic tension acts to resist bending and can help stabilize or restrain plasma structures.



\## Plasma Beta



Plasma beta is the ratio of:



Plasma pressure



to:



Magnetic pressure



In many parts of the solar corona:



Plasma beta < 1



This means magnetic forces dominate plasma behavior.



\## Photospheric Magnetic Field



The photosphere provides the most accessible measurements of the solar magnetic field.



Scientists routinely map its magnetic properties using:



Magnetograms



\## Magnetogram



A magnetogram is a map showing magnetic field strength and polarity across the Sun.



It can reveal:



\- Sunspots

\- Active regions

\- Magnetic polarity

\- Magnetic gradients



\## Zeeman Effect



The Zeeman effect occurs when magnetic fields split spectral lines.



This allows astronomers to measure:



\- Magnetic-field strength

\- Magnetic polarity

\- Field orientation



\## Spectropolarimetry



Spectropolarimetry combines spectroscopy with measurements of polarization.



It is widely used for detailed measurements of solar magnetic fields.



\## Vector Magnetic Field



A vector magnetic-field measurement attempts to determine:



\- Field strength

\- Direction

\- Inclination

\- Azimuth



These measurements are especially useful for studying active regions.



\## Line-of-Sight Magnetic Field



Some instruments measure mainly the component of magnetic field along the observer's line of sight.



This is called the:



Line-of-sight field



\## Magnetic Field Extrapolation



Direct magnetic measurements in the corona are difficult.



Scientists therefore often use photospheric measurements to estimate the magnetic field above the surface.



This process is called:



Magnetic field extrapolation



\## Potential Field Source Surface Model



A commonly used global model is the:



Potential Field Source Surface model



or:



PFSS



It assumes the magnetic field is approximately current-free below an outer source surface.



At the source surface, field lines are forced to become radial.



\## Nonlinear Force-Free Field Models



More complex active-region fields can be modeled using:



Nonlinear force-free field



or:



NLFFF



methods.



These attempt to reconstruct current-carrying coronal magnetic fields.



\## Solar Magnetic Field Structure



The Sun's magnetic field exists on many spatial scales.



It includes:



\- Global dipole-like field

\- Active-region fields

\- Sunspot fields

\- Small-scale network fields

\- Open magnetic fields

\- Closed magnetic loops



\## Global Solar Magnetic Field



On large scales, the Sun's field can resemble a dipole during quieter phases of the solar cycle.



However, it becomes much more complex near solar maximum.



\## Solar Dipole



A dipole magnetic field has two opposite magnetic poles.



Near solar minimum, the Sun's large-scale field is often relatively dipole-like.



\## Solar Magnetic Poles



The Sun has large-scale magnetic polar regions.



Unlike geographic poles, the magnetic polarity reverses periodically.



\## Magnetic Polarity Reversal



Near solar maximum, the Sun's global magnetic field reverses polarity.



North becomes south and south becomes north magnetically.



This happens approximately every:



11 years



\## 22-Year Magnetic Cycle



Because the magnetic polarity reverses every approximately 11 years, it takes about:



22 years



for the Sun's magnetic orientation to return to the same state.



This is called the:



22-year magnetic cycle



\## Solar Cycle



The solar magnetic cycle drives the familiar approximately:



11-year sunspot cycle



Solar activity rises and falls between:



\- Solar minimum

\- Solar maximum



\## Solar Minimum



Near solar minimum:



\- Few sunspots are present

\- Large-scale magnetic field is relatively organized

\- Polar magnetic fields are stronger

\- Large polar coronal holes are common



\## Solar Maximum



Near solar maximum:



\- Sunspot number increases

\- Magnetic structure becomes highly complex

\- Solar flares become more frequent

\- CMEs become more frequent

\- Polar magnetic fields weaken and reverse



\## Hale's Polarity Law



Sunspot groups generally obey:



Hale's polarity law



During one solar cycle, leading sunspots in one hemisphere tend to have one magnetic polarity.



The opposite hemisphere has the opposite pattern.



The polarity pattern reverses during the next cycle.



\## Joy's Law



Bipolar sunspot groups are statistically tilted relative to the solar equator.



The following polarity tends to occur at a somewhat higher latitude.



This relationship is called:



Joy's law



\## Babcock-Leighton Mechanism



The Babcock-Leighton mechanism explains how:



\- Tilted bipolar active regions

\- Flux dispersal

\- Meridional circulation



can contribute to rebuilding the Sun's large-scale magnetic field.



\## Magnetic Flux Transport



Solar surface magnetic fields are transported by:



\- Differential rotation

\- Meridional circulation

\- Convective motions



This process is called:



Surface magnetic flux transport



\## Meridional Circulation



Meridional circulation is a large-scale flow carrying plasma approximately north-south across the solar surface.



It helps transport magnetic flux toward the poles.



\## Polar Field Reversal



Magnetic flux from decaying active regions can migrate toward the poles.



This gradually cancels and replaces the existing polar field.



Eventually, the magnetic poles reverse.



\## Magnetic Network



The quiet solar surface contains a web of small magnetic concentrations called the:



Magnetic network



These fields are often concentrated along the boundaries of convective cells.



\## Supergranulation



Supergranulation is a pattern of large-scale convective cells on the solar surface.



Magnetic fields tend to accumulate near their boundaries.



\## Magnetic Carpet



The constantly changing small-scale magnetic field across the solar surface is sometimes called the:



Magnetic carpet



It is produced by continual:



\- Flux emergence

\- Flux movement

\- Flux cancellation

\- Reconnection



\## Magnetic Flux Cancellation



Opposite magnetic polarities can move together and disappear from surface observations.



This is called:



Magnetic flux cancellation



It can be associated with magnetic reconnection.



\## Magnetic Reconnection



Magnetic reconnection occurs when magnetic field lines change their connectivity.



It allows magnetic energy to be converted into:



\- Heat

\- Plasma motion

\- Radiation

\- Energetic particles



\## Reconnection and Solar Flares



Solar flares are powered primarily by rapid release of stored magnetic energy.



Magnetic reconnection is central to this process.



\## Reconnection and CMEs



Magnetic reconnection can:



\- Help initiate eruptions

\- Accelerate CMEs

\- Form flare loops

\- Restructure the corona



\## Magnetic Field Lines



Magnetic field lines are conceptual lines used to represent:



\- Field direction

\- Field geometry



They do not behave like physical wires but help visualize magnetic structure.



\## Closed Magnetic Field Lines



Closed magnetic field lines connect back to the Sun at both ends.



They form structures such as:



\- Coronal loops

\- Streamers

\- Active-region arcades



\## Open Magnetic Field Lines



Open field lines extend outward into the heliosphere.



They provide pathways for:



\- Solar wind

\- Energetic particles



\## Coronal Holes



Coronal holes are regions containing relatively open magnetic fields.



They are important sources of:



Fast solar wind



\## Coronal Loops



Coronal loops trace closed magnetic fields.



Hot plasma is guided along these loops.



They are common above:



\- Active regions

\- Sunspots



\## Magnetic Arcades



A magnetic arcade is a series of neighboring coronal loops.



Arcades can form above:



\- Polarity inversion lines

\- Filaments

\- Post-flare regions



\## Magnetic Flux Rope



A magnetic flux rope is a twisted bundle of magnetic field lines.



Flux ropes are important in:



\- Solar prominences

\- Filaments

\- CMEs



\## Twist



Magnetic twist describes how field lines wind around a central axis.



Highly twisted structures can store significant magnetic energy.



\## Kink Instability



If a flux rope becomes sufficiently twisted, it can become unstable.



This is called:



Kink instability



It can contribute to solar eruptions.



\## Torus Instability



A rising current-carrying magnetic structure can become unstable if the overlying magnetic field weakens sufficiently rapidly with height.



This is called:



Torus instability



It is an important CME initiation mechanism.



\## Magnetic Helicity



Magnetic helicity measures the degree of:



\- Twist

\- Writhe

\- Linkage



in a magnetic field.



It is approximately conserved in highly conducting plasma.



\## Magnetic Helicity in Active Regions



Active regions can accumulate magnetic helicity through:



\- Flux emergence

\- Photospheric motions

\- Differential rotation



Helicity can be carried away by CMEs.



\## Coronal Magnetic Energy



The corona can store enormous amounts of magnetic energy.



Changes in coronal magnetic structure power:



\- Flares

\- CMEs

\- Jets

\- Heating



\## Magnetic Energy Density



Magnetic energy is stored wherever magnetic fields are present.



Stronger magnetic fields generally contain greater magnetic energy density.



\## Magnetic Reconnection Current Sheet



Reconnection frequently occurs in narrow structures called:



Current sheets



A current sheet contains strong electric currents and sharp changes in magnetic field direction.



\## Solar Jets



Solar jets are narrow plasma eruptions often powered by magnetic reconnection.



They frequently occur where different magnetic systems interact.



\## Solar Prominences



Prominences are dense, relatively cool plasma structures held within the corona by magnetic fields.



Their shape and stability depend strongly on magnetic topology.



\## Solar Filaments



When prominences are viewed against the solar disk, they are called:



Filaments



They commonly lie above:



Polarity inversion lines



\## Filament Channels



A filament channel is a region of strongly sheared magnetic field along a polarity inversion line.



It can support a filament.



\## Magnetic Support of Prominences



Magnetic tension and magnetic topology can support dense prominence plasma against solar gravity.



\## Solar Flares



A solar flare occurs when stored magnetic energy is released rapidly.



Typical consequences include:



\- Plasma heating

\- Particle acceleration

\- X-ray emission

\- UV emission

\- Radio bursts



\## Coronal Mass Ejections



CMEs are large eruptions of magnetized plasma.



They carry solar magnetic field into interplanetary space.



\## Interplanetary Magnetic Field



As solar wind flows away from the Sun, it carries the solar magnetic field outward.



This field in interplanetary space is called the:



Interplanetary Magnetic Field



or:



IMF



\## Frozen-In Magnetic Field



In highly conducting plasma, magnetic field lines are often approximately carried with the moving plasma.



This concept is known as:



Flux freezing



or:



Frozen-in magnetic field behavior



\## Parker Spiral



Because the Sun rotates while solar wind flows outward, the interplanetary magnetic field becomes spiral-shaped.



This geometry is called the:



Parker spiral



\## Parker Spiral Geometry



Near the Sun, the magnetic field is more radial.



Farther outward, solar rotation bends it into an increasingly spiral shape.



\## Eugene Parker



Eugene Parker developed the theoretical model of the solar wind and explained the spiral configuration of the interplanetary magnetic field.



\## Heliospheric Magnetic Field



The magnetic field carried throughout the heliosphere by the solar wind is often called the:



Heliospheric magnetic field



It is an extension of the Sun's magnetic field.



\## Heliosphere



The heliosphere is the region of space dominated by the solar wind and the Sun's magnetic influence.



The solar magnetic field shapes much of its structure.



\## Heliospheric Current Sheet



The heliospheric current sheet is a vast surface separating regions of opposite magnetic polarity in the heliospheric magnetic field.



It has a wavy shape.



\## Ballarina Skirt Shape



The heliospheric current sheet is sometimes described as resembling a:



Ballerina skirt



because the rotating Sun produces a warped, wavy structure.



\## Sector Structure



Spacecraft near Earth often observe alternating sectors of magnetic polarity.



These sectors are associated with crossings of the heliospheric current sheet.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Solar Wind



The solar wind carries magnetic field outward from the Sun.



Magnetic-field structure influences:



\- Solar-wind acceleration

\- Particle transport

\- Space-weather propagation



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Space Weather



Most major space-weather phenomena originate from changes in the Sun's magnetic field.



Important examples include:



\- Solar flares

\- CMEs

\- High-speed solar-wind streams

\- Energetic particle events



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Earth



The Sun's magnetic field influences Earth through the solar wind.



When interplanetary magnetic fields reach Earth, they interact with:



Earth's magnetosphere



\## Magnetic Reconnection at Earth



When the interplanetary magnetic field contains a strong southward component, it can reconnect efficiently with Earth's magnetic field.



This allows energy to enter the magnetosphere.



\## Bz Component



The north-south component of the interplanetary magnetic field is commonly called:



Bz



A strong sustained southward Bz can increase geomagnetic storm intensity.



\## Geomagnetic Storm



A geomagnetic storm occurs when Earth's magnetosphere is strongly disturbed.



Earth-directed CMEs carrying suitable magnetic orientations are important causes.



\## Auroras



Magnetic reconnection and magnetospheric energy transfer can accelerate and guide charged particles into Earth's upper atmosphere.



This produces:



Aurora



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Mercury



Mercury has its own global magnetic field.



It interacts directly with the strong solar wind and solar magnetic field close to the Sun.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Venus



Venus lacks a strong intrinsic global magnetic field.



Solar magnetic fields carried by the solar wind interact with its ionosphere and create an induced magnetosphere.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Mars



Mars lacks a strong global dipole field but contains localized crustal magnetic regions.



The solar wind interacts with:



\- Martian ionosphere

\- Crustal fields

\- Upper atmosphere



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Jupiter



Jupiter has a powerful intrinsic magnetic field.



Its magnetosphere is strongly internally driven but also responds to changes in solar-wind magnetic pressure.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Saturn



Saturn's magnetosphere also interacts with changing solar-wind magnetic conditions.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Comets



Comet plasma tails interact with the interplanetary magnetic field.



Rapid magnetic changes can contribute to:



Comet tail disconnection events



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Cosmic Rays



The heliospheric magnetic field helps modulate:



Galactic cosmic rays



entering the inner Solar System.



\## Cosmic-Ray Modulation



Near solar maximum, the heliospheric magnetic field becomes stronger and more disturbed.



This generally reduces galactic cosmic-ray intensity near Earth.



Near solar minimum, cosmic-ray intensity is usually higher.



\## Solar Energetic Particles



Solar energetic particles travel through the heliosphere while interacting with magnetic fields.



Their propagation depends strongly on:



\- Field-line geometry

\- Turbulence

\- Shock waves



\## Particle Guiding



Charged particles tend to spiral around magnetic field lines.



This means the solar magnetic field helps guide energetic particles through interplanetary space.



\## Magnetic Mirroring



Charged particles moving through increasing magnetic-field strength can be reflected.



This process is called:



Magnetic mirroring



\## Magnetic Turbulence



The solar wind contains fluctuating and turbulent magnetic fields.



Turbulence affects:



\- Particle transport

\- Plasma heating

\- Solar-wind evolution



\## Alfvén Waves



Alfvén waves are disturbances involving:



\- Magnetic fields

\- Plasma motion



They propagate along magnetic field lines.



They are important in:



\- Coronal heating

\- Solar-wind acceleration



\## Magnetohydrodynamics



The large-scale behavior of solar magnetic plasma is often described using:



Magnetohydrodynamics



or:



MHD



MHD combines electromagnetic fields with fluid dynamics.



\## Resistive MHD



Ideal MHD assumes extremely high electrical conductivity.



Resistive MHD includes finite electrical resistance, allowing processes such as:



Magnetic reconnection



\## Magnetic Diffusion



Magnetic diffusion describes the gradual spreading or dissipation of magnetic fields due to finite electrical resistivity.



\## Magnetic Reynolds Number



The magnetic Reynolds number compares:



Magnetic advection



with:



Magnetic diffusion



Solar plasma often has a very large magnetic Reynolds number.



This means magnetic fields are usually strongly coupled to plasma motion.



\## Flux Freezing



When the magnetic Reynolds number is very high, magnetic fields approximately move with the plasma.



This is the basis of:



Flux freezing



\## Breakdown of Flux Freezing



In narrow regions such as current sheets, flux-freezing behavior can break down.



This allows magnetic reconnection.



\## Magnetic Null Point



A magnetic null is a location where magnetic field strength approaches zero.



Such regions can become important sites of reconnection.



\## Separatrix



A separatrix divides magnetic regions with different field-line connectivity.



\## Separator



A separator is a special magnetic field line connecting magnetic null regions.



It can be associated with reconnection.



\## Quasi-Separatrix Layer



A quasi-separatrix layer is a region where field-line connectivity changes rapidly.



These regions can be important during solar eruptions.



\## Magnetic Topology



Magnetic topology describes how magnetic field lines are connected.



Topological structures strongly influence:



\- Reconnection

\- Flare ribbons

\- Plasma confinement

\- Eruptions



\## Magnetic Arcades and Flares



During a flare, magnetic reconnection can transform highly stressed fields into lower-energy loop arcades.



These are observed as:



Post-flare arcades



\## Flare Ribbons and Magnetic Fields



Flare ribbons form in the lower solar atmosphere where energy from reconnecting coronal magnetic fields is deposited.



Their motion reflects evolving magnetic reconnection.



\## Magnetic Reconnection Rate



The rate of reconnection influences how quickly magnetic energy is released.



Rapid reconnection is required to explain powerful flares.



\## Magnetic Field Strength With Height



Magnetic field strength generally decreases with distance from the solar surface.



However, structure depends on:



\- Active regions

\- Coronal holes

\- Global magnetic geometry



\## Coronal Magnetic Measurement



Measuring magnetic fields directly in the corona remains difficult.



Researchers use methods including:



\- Spectropolarimetry

\- Radio observations

\- Coronal seismology

\- Field extrapolation



\## Coronal Seismology



Waves observed in coronal loops can be used to estimate:



Magnetic-field strength



This method is called:



Coronal seismology



\## Faraday Rotation



When polarized radio waves travel through magnetized plasma, their polarization angle rotates.



This is called:



Faraday rotation



It can provide information about coronal and heliospheric magnetic fields.



\## Radio Measurements



Radio observations can probe magnetic fields in:



\- Corona

\- Solar wind

\- CME structures



\## Parker Solar Probe



Parker Solar Probe directly measures magnetic fields extremely close to the Sun.



Its observations help scientists study:



\- Solar-wind origin

\- Magnetic turbulence

\- Switchbacks

\- Coronal magnetic structure



\## Magnetic Switchbacks



Parker Solar Probe discovered frequent sharp reversals or folds in the near-Sun magnetic field.



These are called:



Magnetic switchbacks



Their origin remains an active research topic.



\## Switchback Properties



During a switchback:



\- Magnetic-field direction changes sharply

\- Solar-wind velocity can increase

\- Field magnitude may remain relatively steady



They may be linked to processes in the corona or solar wind.



\## Solar Orbiter



Solar Orbiter studies the Sun from varying locations.



It measures:



\- Photospheric magnetic fields

\- Coronal structures

\- Solar wind

\- Heliospheric magnetic fields



\## Solar Dynamics Observatory



The Solar Dynamics Observatory continuously observes solar magnetic activity.



Its HMI instrument provides detailed magnetic maps.



\## HMI



HMI stands for:



Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager



It measures:



\- Magnetic fields

\- Photospheric velocities

\- Solar oscillations



\## Wilcox Solar Observatory



The Wilcox Solar Observatory has provided long-term measurements of the Sun's large-scale magnetic field.



These observations are valuable for solar-cycle studies.



\## Synoptic Magnetic Maps



Synoptic maps combine magnetic observations over one solar rotation.



They provide a global view of magnetic structure.



\## Carrington Rotation



A commonly used solar rotation reference period is called:



Carrington rotation



It is approximately:



27.2753 days



as viewed synodically from Earth.



\## Synodic Rotation



Synodic rotation measures apparent rotation relative to Earth.



Because Earth also orbits the Sun, this differs from the Sun's sidereal rotation.



\## Sidereal Rotation



Sidereal rotation measures solar rotation relative to distant stars.



Different solar latitudes have different sidereal rotation rates.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Solar Rotation



Rotation influences magnetic-field generation and transport.



Differential rotation is especially important for stretching and organizing magnetic fields.



\## Omega Effect



In dynamo theory, differential rotation can convert:



Poloidal magnetic field



into:



Toroidal magnetic field



This process is often called the:



Omega effect



\## Poloidal Magnetic Field



A poloidal magnetic field has components running approximately north-south and radially.



The large-scale dipolar field is primarily poloidal.



\## Toroidal Magnetic Field



A toroidal magnetic field wraps approximately east-west around the Sun.



Strong toroidal fields are associated with sunspot-producing magnetic structures.



\## Alpha Effect



Some dynamo theories include processes that regenerate poloidal field from toroidal field.



These are often collectively called:



Alpha effects



The exact physical mechanism depends on the dynamo model.



\## Mean-Field Dynamo Theory



Mean-field dynamo theory describes large-scale magnetic-field generation statistically from turbulent plasma motions.



It is widely used in theoretical solar physics.



\## Flux-Transport Dynamo



Flux-transport dynamo models emphasize the transport of magnetic fields by:



\- Meridional circulation

\- Differential rotation

\- Turbulent diffusion



\## Dynamo Uncertainty



The solar dynamo is not fully understood.



Open questions include:



\- Where different dynamo processes dominate

\- How cycles begin and end

\- Why cycle strength changes

\- How grand minima occur



\## Solar Magnetic Field and the Butterfly Diagram



Sunspot emergence latitude changes through the solar cycle.



This creates the:



Butterfly diagram



The pattern provides information about the migration of magnetic activity.



\## Maunder Minimum



The Maunder Minimum was a period of unusually low sunspot activity roughly between:



1645 and 1715



It may represent a prolonged low state of the solar magnetic dynamo.



\## Grand Solar Minima



Grand solar minima are extended periods of low solar magnetic activity.



They provide insight into long-term solar dynamo variability.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Solar Irradiance



Magnetic activity affects the Sun's brightness through:



\- Dark sunspots

\- Bright faculae



The net solar irradiance varies slightly through the solar cycle.



\## Faculae



Faculae are bright magnetic regions on the photosphere.



Their increased brightness often outweighs average sunspot darkening over a solar cycle.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Coronal Heating



Magnetic fields are central to the coronal heating problem.



Possible heating mechanisms include:



\- Magnetic reconnection

\- Nanoflares

\- Alfvén-wave dissipation

\- Turbulence



\## Magnetic Braiding



Photospheric motions can twist and braid magnetic field lines.



This may create small-scale current sheets and reconnection events.



\## Nanoflares



Nanoflares are tiny magnetic energy-release events.



Large numbers of them may contribute to heating the solar corona.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Solar Wind Acceleration



Open magnetic fields guide solar-wind plasma outward.



Magnetic waves and turbulence may transfer energy to the wind.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Coronal Holes



Coronal holes correspond to regions where magnetic field lines extend outward.



These regions are major sources of fast solar wind.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Heliospheric Structure



The changing solar magnetic field controls:



\- Heliospheric current sheet shape

\- Sector boundaries

\- Solar-wind magnetic connectivity

\- Cosmic-ray modulation



\## Magnetic Connectivity



Magnetic connectivity describes which locations are connected by magnetic field lines.



This is important for understanding how particles and disturbances travel.



\## Magnetic Footpoint Mapping



Scientists trace magnetic field lines from spacecraft locations back toward possible solar source regions.



This is called:



Magnetic footpoint mapping



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Spacecraft



Spacecraft throughout the Solar System measure:



\- Magnetic-field strength

\- Magnetic direction

\- Plasma conditions



These measurements reveal how the solar magnetic field evolves with distance.



\## Voyager Measurements



Voyager spacecraft measured magnetic fields in the outer heliosphere and beyond the heliopause.



Their data helped characterize the transition between solar and interstellar magnetic environments.



\## Heliospheric Boundary



The heliosphere eventually meets the interstellar medium.



The solar magnetic field becomes strongly compressed and distorted near this boundary.



\## Heliosheath



The heliosheath is the region between:



\- Termination shock

\- Heliopause



Solar magnetic fields remain important there.



\## Heliopause



The heliopause marks the boundary where the solar wind is balanced by the surrounding interstellar medium.



It is not a boundary where magnetic fields simply disappear.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Interstellar Space



The heliospheric magnetic field interacts with the:



Local interstellar magnetic field



This interaction helps shape the heliosphere.



\## Magnetic Reconnection at the Heliopause



Magnetic reconnection may occur where heliospheric and interstellar magnetic fields interact.



This could influence plasma and particle transport near the heliopause.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Planetary Magnetospheres



The interplanetary magnetic field interacts with planetary magnetic fields.



This can cause:



\- Reconnection

\- Magnetosphere compression

\- Particle acceleration

\- Auroras



\## Earth's Magnetosphere



Earth's magnetic field forms a magnetosphere that deflects much of the solar wind.



Interaction with the solar magnetic field controls much of Earth's space weather.



\## Mercury's Magnetosphere



Mercury has a small intrinsic magnetic field.



Its magnetosphere is strongly affected by the nearby solar magnetic field.



\## Jupiter's Magnetosphere



Jupiter has a powerful magnetosphere.



Solar-wind magnetic disturbances can still modify its outer regions.



\## Saturn's Magnetosphere



Saturn's magnetosphere also responds to solar-wind magnetic pressure and IMF orientation.



\## Uranus and Neptune



The magnetic fields of Uranus and Neptune are strongly tilted relative to their rotation axes.



Their interactions with the solar magnetic field therefore change dramatically as the planets rotate.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Planetary Atmospheres



Solar magnetic activity influences planetary atmospheres indirectly through:



\- Solar wind

\- CMEs

\- XUV radiation

\- Energetic particles



These effects can contribute to atmospheric escape.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Mars Atmospheric Escape



Mars lacks a strong global magnetic field.



Solar-wind magnetic disturbances can increase ion escape from its upper atmosphere.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Venus Atmospheric Escape



Venus lacks an intrinsic global field.



Its upper atmosphere develops an induced magnetic environment through interaction with the solar wind.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Lunar Environment



The Moon lacks a global magnetic field.



Localized crustal magnetic anomalies exist but most of the surface is directly exposed to solar-wind magnetic conditions.



\## Solar Magnetic Field and Exoplanets



Stars other than the Sun possess magnetic fields.

\## Red Dwarfs



Red dwarfs can have strong magnetic fields and intense activity.

\## Early Solar System

\## Magnetic Field and Solar Age



Stars generally lose angular momentum as they age.



Their rotation slows.



Magnetic activity also typically declines.



The present Sun is less magnetically active than many young Sun-like stars.

\## Common Misconceptions



\### The Sun Has One Permanent Magnetic Field



Incorrect.



The solar magnetic field is highly dynamic and changes continuously.



\### The Sun's Magnetic Poles Never Reverse



Incorrect.



They reverse around solar maximum approximately every 11 years.



\### Sunspots Create the Solar Magnetic Field



Incorrect.



Sunspots are manifestations of magnetic fields generated by the solar dynamo.



\### Magnetic Field Lines Are Physical Wires



Incorrect.



They are conceptual tools used to represent field direction and geometry.



\### The Solar Magnetic Field Ends Near the Sun



Incorrect.



It is carried outward by the solar wind throughout the heliosphere.



\### Every Strong Magnetic Region Produces a Major Flare



Incorrect.



Strong fields increase potential energy storage, but not every active region erupts.



\### Magnetic Reconnection Means Magnetic Field Is Destroyed



Oversimplified.



Reconnection changes field connectivity and converts magnetic energy into other forms.



\### Solar Magnetism Only Matters for the Sun



Incorrect.



Solar magnetic fields influence planetary magnetospheres, atmospheres, spacecraft, cosmic rays, and the heliosphere.



\### The Solar Magnetic Field Is a Simple Dipole



Only approximately during some quiet phases.



Near solar maximum it becomes extremely complex.



\### Stronger CME Speed Always Means Stronger Geomagnetic Storm



Incorrect.



CME magnetic orientation is crucial, especially sustained southward Bz.



\## Important Terminology



\### Solar Magnetic Field



Magnetic field generated by electrically conducting plasma inside the Sun.



\### Solar Dynamo



Process generating and regenerating the Sun's magnetic field.



\### Magnetic Flux



Measure of magnetic field passing through a surface.



\### Magnetic Polarity



Direction of magnetic field relative to the solar surface.



\### Magnetogram



Map of solar magnetic-field properties.



\### Zeeman Effect



Splitting of spectral lines by magnetic fields.



\### Active Region



Magnetically concentrated solar region that can contain sunspots and produce eruptions.



\### Polarity Inversion Line



Boundary separating opposite magnetic polarities.



\### Magnetic Shear



Distortion of magnetic fields from a lower-energy configuration.



\### Magnetic Reconnection



Process changing magnetic connectivity and releasing magnetic energy.



\### Magnetic Flux Rope



Twisted bundle of magnetic field lines.



\### Magnetic Helicity



Measure of twist, writhe, and linkage in magnetic structures.



\### Open Magnetic Field



Field line extending into the heliosphere.



\### Closed Magnetic Field



Field line returning to the Sun.



\### Interplanetary Magnetic Field



Solar magnetic field carried outward by the solar wind.



\### Parker Spiral



Spiral structure of the heliospheric magnetic field caused by solar rotation and outward solar wind.



\### Heliospheric Current Sheet



Large surface separating opposite magnetic polarity sectors in the heliosphere.



\### Plasma Beta



Ratio of plasma pressure to magnetic pressure.



\### Magnetohydrodynamics



Physics describing conducting plasma interacting with magnetic fields.



\### Magnetic Switchback



Sharp temporary reversal or fold in the near-Sun magnetic field.

\## Summary



The solar magnetic field is generated by the motion of electrically conducting plasma inside the Sun.



The main physical mechanism is the:



Solar dynamo



Important components include:



\- Differential rotation

\- Convection

\- Meridional circulation

\- Magnetic flux transport



Magnetic fields rise through the convection zone and emerge at the photosphere.



Concentrated magnetic fields produce:



\- Sunspots

\- Active regions



Magnetic energy can become stored in:



\- Twisted fields

\- Sheared fields

\- Flux ropes



Rapid magnetic reconnection can release this energy and power:



\- Solar flares

\- CMEs

\- Plasma heating

\- Particle acceleration



The Sun's global magnetic polarity reverses approximately every 11 years.



A full magnetic cycle therefore lasts around:



22 years



Open solar magnetic fields extend into interplanetary space.



The solar wind carries these fields outward and produces the:



Parker spiral



The heliospheric magnetic field influences:



\- Space weather

\- Planetary magnetospheres

\- Cosmic rays

\- Energetic particles

\- Heliospheric structure



Solar magnetic fields are measured using:



\- Magnetograms

\- Spectropolarimetry

\- Zeeman splitting

\- Spacecraft instruments

\- Coronal models



The solar magnetic field is one of the most important physical systems controlling activity throughout the Solar System.
