\# Sunspots



\## Overview



Sunspots are temporary dark regions visible on the Sun's photosphere.



They appear darker than the surrounding solar surface because they are cooler than nearby regions, although they are still extremely hot.



Sunspots are produced by concentrations of strong magnetic fields that interfere with the normal transport of heat through the Sun's convective outer layers.



Sunspots are important indicators of solar magnetic activity and are closely connected with:



\- Solar flares

\- Coronal Mass Ejections

\- Active regions

\- Solar magnetic fields

\- The solar cycle

\- Space weather



\## Where Sunspots Occur



Sunspots occur on the:



Photosphere



The photosphere is the visible layer of the Sun from which most visible sunlight escapes.



Sunspots are not holes in the Sun.



They are regions where intense magnetic fields modify the transport of energy through the solar plasma.



\## Why Sunspots Look Dark



Sunspots are cooler than the surrounding photosphere.



The average visible solar surface has a temperature of approximately:



5,800 K



The darkest central regions of large sunspots can have temperatures of roughly:



3,500–4,500 K



Because cooler material emits less visible radiation per unit area, sunspots appear dark in contrast with the hotter surrounding photosphere.



If a sunspot could be isolated from the rest of the Sun, it would still be extremely bright.



\## Sunspot Structure



A well-developed sunspot commonly contains two major regions:



\- Umbra

\- Penumbra



\## Umbra



The umbra is the dark central region of a sunspot.



It contains:



\- Very strong magnetic fields

\- Lower temperatures than the surrounding photosphere

\- Predominantly more vertical magnetic fields



The umbra is usually the darkest part of the sunspot.



\## Penumbra



The penumbra is the lighter region surrounding the umbra.



It contains filament-like structures and more inclined magnetic fields.



The penumbra is generally:



\- Brighter than the umbra

\- Darker than the normal photosphere



\## Penumbral Filaments



The penumbra contains elongated bright and dark structures called:



Penumbral filaments



These structures reflect complex interactions between:



\- Magnetic fields

\- Convection

\- Plasma flows



\## Evershed Effect



Sunspot penumbrae often show a radial outward flow of plasma.



This is called the:



Evershed effect



It was discovered by astronomer John Evershed.



The flow is closely associated with the inclined magnetic structure of the penumbra.



\## Magnetic Nature of Sunspots



Sunspots contain magnetic fields much stronger than the average magnetic field of the quiet solar surface.



Large sunspot magnetic fields can reach strengths of several thousand gauss.



These strong fields influence the motion of electrically conducting solar plasma.



\## Magnetic Pressure



Magnetic fields exert pressure and tension on plasma.



Strong magnetic fields inside sunspots alter convective energy transport.



This contributes to their reduced temperature relative to the surrounding photosphere.



\## Convection



Energy from the Sun's interior is transported through much of its outer region by convection.



Hot plasma rises while cooler plasma sinks.



Strong sunspot magnetic fields suppress or reorganize this convective motion.



As a result, less heat reaches the visible surface locally.



\## Sunspots Are Not Completely Without Convection



Convection is strongly modified inside sunspots rather than simply switched off.



Fine structures observed in:



\- Umbrae

\- Penumbrae



show that magnetized convection continues in complex forms.



\## Umbra-Penumbra Formation



Small magnetic concentrations can initially appear without a fully developed penumbra.



As magnetic flux increases and the structure evolves, a penumbra may form around the umbra.



\## Pores



Small dark magnetic features on the photosphere that lack a developed penumbra are called:



Pores



Some pores grow into sunspots, while others disappear without developing further.



\## Sunspot Groups



Sunspots often occur in groups rather than individually.



A sunspot group may contain:



\- One large spot

\- Several major spots

\- Numerous smaller spots

\- Complex magnetic structures



Sunspot groups are usually part of larger solar active regions.



\## Active Regions



An active region is an area of concentrated solar magnetic activity.



It may contain:



\- Sunspots

\- Strong magnetic fields

\- Coronal loops

\- Solar flares

\- Filaments

\- Coronal Mass Ejections



Sunspots provide visible evidence of these magnetic regions at the photosphere.



\## Magnetic Polarity



Sunspots have magnetic polarity.



The magnetic field can be directed:



\- Outward from the solar surface

\- Inward toward the solar surface



Sunspot groups frequently contain spots of opposite magnetic polarity.



\## Bipolar Sunspot Groups



Many active regions contain two major magnetic polarities.



These are called:



Bipolar active regions



One polarity is commonly located toward the direction of solar rotation and the other behind it.



\## Leading and Following Spots



In a bipolar sunspot group:



Leading spot:



Located farther in the direction of solar rotation.



Following spot:



Located behind the leading spot.



Their magnetic polarity patterns follow systematic rules during the solar cycle.



\## Hale's Polarity Law



Sunspot groups generally follow:



Hale's polarity law



During a given solar cycle:



\- Leading sunspots in one solar hemisphere usually have one magnetic polarity.

\- Leading sunspots in the opposite hemisphere usually have the opposite polarity.



During the next solar cycle, these polarities reverse.



This is evidence of the Sun's approximately 22-year magnetic cycle.



\## Joy's Law



Bipolar sunspot groups are generally tilted relative to the solar equator.



The following polarity tends to occur at a slightly higher latitude than the leading polarity.



The average tilt tends to increase with latitude.



This statistical relationship is called:



Joy's law



\## Solar Magnetic Cycle



The Sun's magnetic activity follows an approximately:



22-year magnetic cycle



The familiar approximately 11-year sunspot cycle represents about half of this full magnetic cycle.



Around each solar maximum, the Sun's global magnetic polarity reverses.



After another approximately 11 years, it returns to the previous polarity orientation.



\## Solar Cycle



The number of visible sunspots changes over an approximately:



11-year solar activity cycle



The cycle progresses broadly from:



Solar minimum



↓



Increasing activity



↓



Solar maximum



↓



Declining activity



↓



Next solar minimum



\## Solar Minimum



During solar minimum:



\- Few sunspots are visible

\- Active regions are less common

\- Solar flares are generally less frequent

\- CME activity is generally lower



The Sun is not completely inactive during solar minimum.



\## Solar Maximum



During solar maximum:



\- Many sunspots may be visible

\- Active regions are more numerous

\- Solar flares occur more frequently

\- CMEs are more frequent

\- Solar magnetic structure becomes highly complex



\## Sunspot Number



Solar activity is commonly tracked using a quantity called the:



Sunspot number



It is based on observations of:



\- Individual sunspots

\- Sunspot groups



It provides a long-term measure of solar activity.



\## International Sunspot Number



The International Sunspot Number is a standardized index derived from observations by networks of solar observers.



It allows solar activity to be compared across long periods.



\## Historical Sunspot Records



Systematic sunspot observations provide one of the longest records of solar magnetic activity.



These records allow scientists to reconstruct changes in the solar cycle over centuries.



\## Early Sunspot Observations



Sunspots were observed before the invention of the telescope in some cultures when atmospheric conditions reduced the Sun's brightness sufficiently.



However, telescopic observations in the early seventeenth century transformed the scientific study of sunspots.



\## Galileo Galilei



Galileo Galilei made detailed telescopic observations of sunspots.



By tracking their movement across the solar disk, early astronomers showed that:



\- Sunspots were associated with the Sun

\- The Sun rotates



\## Christoph Scheiner



Christoph Scheiner was another important early telescopic observer of sunspots.



Detailed observations contributed to understanding solar rotation and the changing solar surface.



\## Thomas Harriot



Thomas Harriot made early telescopic observations of sunspots in the early seventeenth century.



\## Sunspots and Solar Rotation



Sunspots move across the visible solar disk because the Sun rotates.



Tracking sunspots provides a way to measure solar rotation.



\## Differential Rotation



The Sun is not a solid body.



Its surface rotates at different rates at different latitudes.



This is called:



Differential rotation



The equatorial regions rotate faster than higher latitudes.



Sunspot observations helped reveal this behavior.



\## Sunspot Lifetime



Sunspots can last for:



\- Hours

\- Days

\- Weeks

\- Occasionally months for large groups



Small spots generally have shorter lifetimes.



Large, stable sunspot groups can persist through multiple solar rotations.



\## Sunspot Growth



A sunspot region can develop as magnetic flux emerges through the photosphere.



The region may progress through stages involving:



\- Small pores

\- Growing spots

\- Formation of penumbrae

\- Complex sunspot groups



\## Sunspot Decay



Sunspots eventually decay as their magnetic structure disperses and evolves.



Decay can involve:



\- Fragmentation

\- Loss of magnetic flux

\- Disappearance of penumbrae

\- Reduction in spot area



\## Magnetic Flux Emergence



Magnetic flux can rise from beneath the photosphere into the solar atmosphere.



This process is called:



Magnetic flux emergence



Emerging magnetic regions can develop into sunspot groups and active regions.



\## Magnetic Flux



Magnetic flux represents the amount of magnetic field passing through a surface.



Large active regions can contain enormous amounts of magnetic flux.



\## Flux Tubes



Magnetic fields beneath the solar surface can become concentrated into structures sometimes described as:



Magnetic flux tubes



Buoyant magnetic structures can rise through the convection zone and emerge at the photosphere.



\## Solar Dynamo



The Sun's magnetic field is generated by a dynamo operating within its electrically conducting plasma.



The solar dynamo involves:



\- Convection

\- Rotation

\- Differential rotation

\- Large-scale plasma flows

\- Magnetic-field generation and transport



Sunspots are surface manifestations of this deeper magnetic activity.



\## Convection Zone



The convection zone is the outer region of the Sun's interior where energy is transported mainly by convective motion.



It extends beneath the photosphere.



The interaction between convection and magnetic fields is fundamental to sunspot formation.



\## Tachocline



The tachocline is a transition region between:



\- The Sun's differentially rotating convection zone

\- The more uniformly rotating radiative interior



It is important in many models of the solar dynamo.



\## Photospheric Granulation



The normal photosphere has a cellular pattern called:



Granulation



Granules are produced by convection.



Hot plasma rises near granule centers and cooler plasma descends near their edges.



\## Sunspots and Granulation



Inside sunspots, normal granulation is strongly modified by magnetic fields.



Fine magnetoconvective structures can still occur.



\## Umbral Dots



Small bright features observed within sunspot umbrae are called:



Umbral dots



They are associated with localized magnetoconvective processes.



\## Light Bridges



Bright structures sometimes cross or divide a sunspot umbra.



These are called:



Light bridges



They can appear as a sunspot evolves or begins to fragment.



\## Sunspot Magnetic Field Measurement



Sunspot magnetic fields can be measured using changes in spectral lines caused by magnetic fields.



One important phenomenon is the:



Zeeman effect



\## Zeeman Effect



The Zeeman effect occurs when a magnetic field splits atomic spectral lines into multiple components.



It provides a powerful method for measuring solar magnetic fields.



\## George Ellery Hale



In 1908, George Ellery Hale used the Zeeman effect to demonstrate strong magnetic fields in sunspots.



This was a major discovery in solar physics.



It established that sunspots are fundamentally magnetic phenomena.



\## Magnetogram



A magnetogram is a map of magnetic-field properties on the Sun's surface.



Magnetograms reveal:



\- Magnetic polarity

\- Field strength

\- Active-region structure

\- Magnetic gradients



\## Sunspot Magnetic Complexity



Sunspot groups differ greatly in magnetic complexity.



Simple groups may contain clearly separated opposite polarities.



Complex groups can contain strongly mixed polarities.



Magnetic complexity is important for assessing flare probability.



\## Mount Wilson Magnetic Classification



A commonly used magnetic classification system includes:



\- Alpha

\- Beta

\- Gamma

\- Delta



Combinations of these classifications may also be used.



\## Alpha Classification



An alpha sunspot group is dominated by one magnetic polarity.



It has relatively simple magnetic structure.



\## Beta Classification



A beta group contains both magnetic polarities with a relatively simple separation.



\## Gamma Classification



A gamma group has a complex distribution of magnetic polarities that cannot be easily separated into a simple bipolar structure.



\## Delta Configuration



A delta configuration contains opposite-polarity umbrae within a common penumbra.



Delta regions can store large amounts of magnetic free energy and are associated with increased probability of strong solar flares.



\## Magnetic Polarity Inversion Line



The boundary between regions of opposite magnetic polarity is called the:



Polarity inversion line



Strongly sheared magnetic fields near such boundaries are often important in flare-producing regions.



\## Magnetic Shear



Magnetic shear describes a magnetic configuration strongly distorted from a simpler low-energy state.



Sheared fields can store:



Free magnetic energy



This energy can later be released during solar eruptions.



\## Free Magnetic Energy



Free magnetic energy is magnetic energy available above a lower-energy reference configuration.



Active regions with substantial free magnetic energy can power:



\- Solar flares

\- CMEs



\## Sunspots and Solar Flares



Sunspots do not themselves explode.



However, the complex magnetic active regions containing sunspots can produce solar flares.



Large, rapidly evolving, magnetically complex sunspot groups are more likely to produce major flares than simple regions.



\## Sunspots and Coronal Mass Ejections



Many CMEs originate from active regions associated with sunspots.



However:



\- Not every sunspot group produces a CME.

\- Not every CME originates from a major sunspot group.



Some CMEs originate from filament systems outside prominent active regions.



\## Sunspots and Coronal Loops



Magnetic fields emerging from sunspots extend upward into the solar atmosphere.



Hot plasma can follow these magnetic structures, creating:



Coronal loops



Loops frequently connect regions of opposite magnetic polarity.



\## Sunspots and Solar Flares Versus CMEs



Sunspot:



Visible magnetic feature on the photosphere.



Solar flare:



Rapid magnetic-energy release in the solar atmosphere.



CME:



Large eruption of magnetized plasma into space.



These phenomena are related through solar magnetic activity but are not interchangeable.



\## Sunspot Classification by Appearance



Sunspots can also be classified according to their morphological development.



One historical system is the:



Zurich classification



More detailed modified systems have also been developed.



\## McIntosh Classification



The McIntosh sunspot classification describes sunspot groups using properties including:



\- Group structure

\- Largest spot's penumbra

\- Distribution of spots



It is useful in solar activity analysis and flare forecasting.



\## Butterfly Diagram



Sunspots do not appear randomly at all solar latitudes.



At the beginning of a solar cycle, they tend to emerge at moderate latitudes.



As the cycle progresses, new sunspots tend to appear progressively closer to the equator.



When sunspot latitude is plotted against time, the pattern resembles butterfly wings.



This is called the:



Butterfly diagram



\## Spörer's Law



The tendency for sunspots to appear at progressively lower latitudes as a solar cycle advances is known as:



Spörer's law



\## Solar Cycle Overlap



Near the transition between cycles:



\- Old-cycle sunspots may appear near low latitudes.

\- New-cycle sunspots may begin appearing at higher latitudes.



Their magnetic polarities help distinguish the cycles.



\## Maunder Minimum



The Maunder Minimum was a period of unusually low observed sunspot activity approximately between:



1645 and 1715



It is one of the best-known examples of a prolonged solar activity minimum.



\## Grand Solar Minimum



A grand solar minimum is an extended period during which solar activity remains unusually low across multiple cycles.



The Maunder Minimum is a historical example.



\## Dalton Minimum



The Dalton Minimum was another period of relatively low solar activity, approximately spanning the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries.



It was less extreme than the Maunder Minimum.



\## Sunspots and Climate



Changes in solar activity can influence the amount and spectral distribution of solar energy reaching Earth.



However, sunspot number alone does not determine Earth's climate.



Modern climate change cannot be explained simply by changes in sunspot activity.



\## Why More Sunspots Can Coincide With Greater Solar Brightness



Sunspots themselves reduce local brightness.



However, active regions also contain bright magnetic structures called:



Faculae



The increased brightness from faculae can outweigh the darkening caused by sunspots.



Therefore, the Sun's total energy output is generally slightly greater near solar maximum.



\## Faculae



Faculae are bright magnetic structures on the solar photosphere.



They are especially noticeable near the solar limb.



They commonly accompany sunspot active regions.



\## Total Solar Irradiance



Total Solar Irradiance, abbreviated:



TSI



is the total solar power received per unit area at Earth's distance from the Sun across all wavelengths.



TSI varies slightly over the solar cycle.



\## Sunspot Darkening



When a large sunspot group crosses the visible solar disk, it can temporarily reduce the Sun's total brightness.



This effect can be measured precisely from space.



\## Facular Brightening



Bright faculae surrounding active regions can increase solar brightness.



Over the solar cycle, facular brightening generally exceeds average sunspot darkening, producing slightly higher TSI near solar maximum.



\## Sunspots and Space Weather



Sunspots themselves are not space weather traveling toward Earth.



Instead, they indicate magnetic active regions capable of producing:



\- Solar flares

\- CMEs

\- Energetic particle events



These eruptions can affect Earth's space environment.



\## Sunspots and Radio Communication



A sunspot does not directly disrupt radio communication.



However, solar flares from sunspot active regions can alter Earth's ionosphere and produce radio blackouts.



\## Sunspots and Auroras



Sunspots do not directly create auroras.



Periods with many sunspots generally correspond to increased solar activity, making eruptions capable of enhancing auroras more common.



\## Sunspots and Earth's Magnetosphere



Sunspots themselves remain on the Sun.



Earth's magnetosphere is affected by solar-wind disturbances and magnetic fields associated with solar activity.



\## Sunspot Observation



Sunspots can be observed using:



\- Properly filtered telescopes

\- Solar projection

\- Dedicated solar telescopes

\- Space-based observatories



Directly looking at the Sun without appropriate protection can permanently damage eyesight.



\## Solar Viewing Safety



Never look directly at the Sun through:



\- Naked eyes

\- Binoculars

\- Telescopes

\- Camera optical viewfinders



without appropriate solar viewing protection.



Optical devices concentrate sunlight and can cause severe eye injury.



\## Solar Filters



Telescopic solar observation requires a properly designed solar filter installed according to the equipment manufacturer's safety instructions.



Improvised materials are not appropriate substitutes.



\## Projection Method



The Sun can sometimes be observed indirectly by projecting its image onto a surface.



Projection avoids direct viewing through the optical system.



Equipment must still be used appropriately because concentrated sunlight can heat optical components.



\## Space-Based Sunspot Observation



Spacecraft can observe the Sun continuously without many limitations caused by:



\- Earth's day-night cycle

\- Weather

\- Atmospheric distortion



\## Solar Dynamics Observatory



NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory, or:



SDO



observes the Sun in many wavelengths and measures photospheric magnetic fields.



It provides detailed observations of:



\- Sunspots

\- Active regions

\- Solar flares

\- Coronal structures



\## Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager



The Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager, or:



HMI



aboard SDO measures:



\- Photospheric magnetic fields

\- Solar oscillations

\- Surface velocities



Its data are important for studying sunspot structure and evolution.



\## Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope



The Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope provides extremely high-resolution observations of the solar surface and atmosphere.



It can study fine structures associated with:



\- Sunspots

\- Magnetic fields

\- Convection

\- Chromosphere



\## Spectropolarimetry



Spectropolarimetry analyzes:



\- Spectral information

\- Polarization of light



It is one of the most powerful techniques for measuring solar magnetic fields.



\## Polarization



Magnetic fields alter the polarization properties of spectral lines.



Scientists use these changes to reconstruct the direction and strength of magnetic fields.



\## Vector Magnetic Field



A vector magnetic-field measurement attempts to determine:



\- Field strength

\- Direction

\- Orientation



rather than only the line-of-sight component.



These measurements are valuable for understanding flare-producing active regions.



\## Sunspot Seismology



Helioseismic methods can be used to investigate:



\- Subsurface flows

\- Wave propagation

\- Internal structure beneath sunspots



Strong magnetic fields make wave behavior complex.



\## Helioseismology



Helioseismology studies oscillations and waves traveling through the Sun.



It provides information about the solar interior that cannot be observed directly.



\## Sunspot Oscillations



Sunspots exhibit oscillatory behavior.



These oscillations can occur in:



\- Umbra

\- Penumbra

\- Overlying atmosphere



Studying them provides information about magnetized solar plasma.



\## Umbral Oscillations



The dark umbra can show periodic intensity and velocity variations.



These are associated with magnetohydrodynamic waves in the sunspot atmosphere.



\## Sunspot Waves



Magnetic structures can guide waves upward from the photosphere into the:



\- Chromosphere

\- Corona



This makes sunspots useful laboratories for studying wave propagation in magnetized plasma.



\## Sunspot Size



Sunspots vary greatly in size.



Small spots may be comparable in scale to Earth or smaller.



Large sunspot groups can extend across regions many times Earth's diameter.



\## Sunspot Area



Sunspot size is often expressed in:



Millionths of the visible solar hemisphere



This allows scientists to compare spots independently of apparent image scale.



\## Large Sunspot Groups



The largest sunspot groups can cover enormous areas of the solar photosphere.



Some can become visible to the unaided eye under strongly attenuated viewing conditions, although direct solar viewing is unsafe without proper protection.



\## Wilson Effect



Sunspots observed near the solar limb can show an apparent depression of the umbra relative to the surrounding photosphere.



This historical observational phenomenon is called the:



Wilson effect



Its interpretation involves the atmospheric structure and opacity of sunspots.



\## Wilson Depression



The visible surface within a sunspot can lie somewhat deeper than the surrounding photospheric surface because magnetic pressure reduces gas pressure and changes opacity.



This geometric depression is called the:



Wilson depression



\## Sunspot Pressure Balance



Inside a sunspot, magnetic pressure contributes to the total pressure balance.



Therefore, gas pressure can be lower than in surrounding nonmagnetic regions at comparable geometric heights.



\## Magnetoconvection



Convection occurring in the presence of strong magnetic fields is called:



Magnetoconvection



Sunspots are major natural laboratories for studying magnetoconvection.



\## Penumbral Magnetoconvection



The filamentary penumbra results from complex interactions between:



\- Inclined magnetic fields

\- Convective flows

\- Radiative energy transport



\## Sunspot Decay and Moving Magnetic Features



Small magnetic elements can move outward from sunspots during their evolution.



These are sometimes called:



Moving magnetic features



They may contribute to dispersal of magnetic flux.



\## Moat Flow



A horizontal outward flow can occur around mature sunspots.



This is called:



Moat flow



It is associated with the surrounding photospheric velocity field.



\## Active Region Evolution



An active region may progress through:



Magnetic flux emergence



↓



Pore formation



↓



Sunspot development



↓



Increasing magnetic complexity



↓



Possible flare/CME activity



↓



Fragmentation and decay



↓



Magnetic flux dispersal



Not every active region follows exactly the same sequence.



\## Solar Dynamo and Sunspot Emergence



The solar dynamo generates organized magnetic fields within the Sun.



Buoyant magnetic structures can rise through the convection zone and emerge at the surface.



Sunspot groups represent concentrated manifestations of this magnetic flux.



\## Differential Rotation and Magnetic Fields



Because different solar latitudes rotate at different rates, magnetic fields can become stretched and reorganized.



Differential rotation is therefore an important component of solar dynamo processes.



\## Meridional Circulation



The Sun also has large-scale flows in the north-south direction called:



Meridional circulation



These flows help transport magnetic flux across the solar surface and are important in solar-cycle models.



\## Surface Flux Transport



Surface flux transport describes how magnetic fields are redistributed by:



\- Differential rotation

\- Meridional circulation

\- Convective motions



This transport influences the evolution of the Sun's large-scale magnetic field.



\## Polar Magnetic Fields



Magnetic flux from decaying active regions can be transported toward the solar poles.



This contributes to reversal and rebuilding of the Sun's polar magnetic fields during the solar cycle.



\## Babcock-Leighton Mechanism



The Babcock-Leighton mechanism describes how the emergence, tilt, and decay of bipolar active regions can contribute to regeneration of the Sun's large-scale magnetic field.



Joy's law and magnetic flux transport are important components of this process.



\## Sunspot Cycle Prediction



Scientists attempt to predict:



\- Timing of solar maximum

\- Maximum sunspot number

\- Cycle strength

\- Duration



Predictions use:



\- Polar magnetic fields

\- Previous cycle behavior

\- Dynamo models

\- Statistical methods



\## Why Solar Cycle Prediction Is Difficult



The solar dynamo is a nonlinear physical system.



Cycle-to-cycle variations depend on complex interactions between:



\- Magnetic fields

\- Plasma flows

\- Convection

\- Flux emergence



This limits long-term predictive accuracy.



\## Sunspot Forecasting



Short-term observations of sunspot groups help estimate probabilities of:



\- Solar flares

\- Energetic eruptions



Forecasting considers:



\- Magnetic classification

\- Growth rate

\- Magnetic gradients

\- Shear

\- Recent activity



\## Sunspot Number Versus Flare Risk



A high global sunspot number indicates an active Sun but does not determine whether a particular major flare will occur.



Flare risk depends strongly on the magnetic complexity of individual active regions.



\## Sunspot Number Versus CME Risk



More sunspots generally accompany increased solar activity.



However, CME occurrence cannot be predicted from sunspot number alone.



\## Sunspot Number Versus Solar Wind



The solar wind exists continuously during both solar minimum and maximum.



Its structure changes with the solar cycle, but it is not simply proportional to sunspot number.



\## Sunspots on Other Stars



Dark magnetic regions similar to sunspots occur on other stars.



These are called:



Starspots



\## Starspots



Starspots are relatively cool, magnetically active regions on stellar surfaces.



They can be much larger relative to their stars than typical sunspots are relative to the Sun.



\## Detecting Starspots



Starspots can be detected through:



\- Periodic brightness variations

\- Spectroscopy

\- Doppler imaging

\- Transit observations



\## Stellar Rotation From Starspots



As a spotted star rotates, dark regions move across the visible hemisphere.



This can produce periodic changes in brightness.



Scientists use these variations to estimate stellar rotation periods.



\## Starspots and Exoplanet Transits



Starspots can affect measurements of exoplanet transits.



When a planet crosses a starspot, the observed transit light curve can show anomalies.



Starspots can therefore influence estimates of:



\- Planet radius

\- Atmospheric properties

\- Transit depth



\## Starspots and Stellar Activity



Large starspot coverage is often associated with:



\- Strong magnetic fields

\- Stellar flares

\- High magnetic activity



This is particularly important for young stars and active red dwarfs.



\## Red Dwarf Starspots



Red dwarf stars can have extensive magnetic activity and large starspots.



Their activity can influence planets orbiting close to the star.



\## Starspots and Habitability



Starspots themselves are not necessarily dangerous to planets.



However, they indicate magnetic activity that can be associated with:



\- Strong stellar flares

\- High-energy radiation

\- Possible stellar CMEs



These phenomena can affect planetary atmospheres and habitability.



\## Sunspots and Solar Irradiance



Sunspots reduce local solar brightness.



But the total effect of solar magnetic activity includes both:



\- Dark sunspots

\- Bright faculae



The net change in total solar irradiance over the solar cycle is relatively small.



\## Spectral Solar Irradiance



Solar activity affects different wavelengths by different amounts.



Ultraviolet radiation can vary proportionally much more over the solar cycle than total visible energy output.



These variations can influence Earth's upper atmosphere.



\## Sunspots and Ultraviolet Radiation



Active regions associated with sunspots produce enhanced ultraviolet and extreme-ultraviolet emission.



This influences:



\- Thermosphere

\- Ionosphere

\- Atmospheric chemistry



\## Sunspots and the Heliosphere



The changing solar magnetic cycle associated with sunspot activity alters the large-scale structure of the heliosphere.



This affects:



\- Heliospheric magnetic field

\- Solar-wind structure

\- Cosmic-ray modulation



\## Sunspots and Galactic Cosmic Rays



Galactic cosmic-ray intensity near Earth generally varies inversely with solar activity.



Near solar maximum, stronger and more disturbed heliospheric magnetic fields tend to reduce the number of galactic cosmic rays reaching the inner Solar System.



This relationship is not caused by sunspots directly but by broader solar magnetic activity.

\## Common Misconceptions



\### Sunspots Are Holes in the Sun



Incorrect.



Sunspots are cooler magnetic regions in the photosphere.



\### Sunspots Are Cold



Misleading.



They are cooler than the surrounding photosphere but remain thousands of kelvins in temperature.



\### Sunspots Are Black Objects



Incorrect.



They appear dark only because the surrounding solar surface is much brighter.



\### Sunspots Cause Solar Flares by Themselves



Oversimplified.



Flares result from magnetic-energy release in active regions. Sunspots are visible manifestations of strong magnetic fields associated with those regions.



\### Every Sunspot Produces a Flare



Incorrect.



Many sunspots never produce major flares.



\### A Large Sunspot Always Produces a Large Flare



Incorrect.



Magnetic complexity and evolution are more important than size alone.



\### More Sunspots Mean the Sun Is Dimmer Overall



Generally incorrect on solar-cycle timescales.



Bright faculae associated with magnetic activity usually outweigh average sunspot darkening, so total solar irradiance is slightly higher near solar maximum.



\### Sunspots Directly Cause Auroras



Incorrect.



Auroras are produced by energetic particles interacting with planetary atmospheres. Solar eruptions associated with active regions can enhance auroras.



\### Sunspots Directly Cause Earth's Weather



Incorrect.



Individual sunspots do not directly control ordinary day-to-day terrestrial weather.



\### Sunspots Stay Permanently on the Sun



Incorrect.



They form, evolve, and decay.



\### Sunspots Occur Everywhere Equally



Incorrect.



Their latitude distribution changes systematically throughout the solar cycle.



\## Important Terminology



\### Sunspot



Dark, relatively cool, strongly magnetized region of the solar photosphere.



\### Umbra



Dark central part of a sunspot.



\### Penumbra



Lighter filamentary region surrounding the umbra.



\### Pore



Small dark magnetic feature without a developed penumbra.



\### Active Region



Magnetically concentrated region capable of producing sunspots and solar eruptions.



\### Magnetic Polarity



Direction of a magnetic field relative to the solar surface.



\### Magnetogram



Map of solar magnetic-field properties.



\### Zeeman Effect



Splitting of spectral lines by magnetic fields.



\### Evershed Effect



Radial plasma flow observed in sunspot penumbrae.



\### Wilson Depression



Geometric depression of the visible surface within a sunspot.



\### Facula



Bright magnetic photospheric structure associated with active regions.



\### Differential Rotation



Variation of solar rotation rate with latitude.



\### Solar Dynamo



Physical mechanism generating the Sun's magnetic field.



\### Butterfly Diagram



Plot showing the changing latitude of sunspots over successive solar cycles.



\### Hale's Polarity Law



Systematic reversal and hemispheric organization of bipolar sunspot magnetic polarities.



\### Joy's Law



Statistical relationship describing the tilt of bipolar sunspot groups.



\### Magnetoconvection



Convection occurring in magnetized plasma.



\### Magnetic Flux



Measure of magnetic field passing through a surface.



\### Polarity Inversion Line



Boundary separating opposite magnetic polarities.



\## Sunspot Formation Sequence



A simplified sequence is:



Solar dynamo generates magnetic fields



↓



Magnetic structures develop within the convection zone



↓



Magnetic flux rises toward the photosphere



↓



Small magnetic pores appear



↓



Magnetic flux becomes concentrated



↓



Sunspot umbra develops



↓



Penumbra may form



↓



Sunspot group evolves



↓



Magnetic complexity may increase



↓



Solar flares or CMEs may occur



↓



Magnetic field disperses



↓



Sunspot decays

\## Why Sunspots Matter for Space Weather



Sunspots themselves generally do not travel toward Earth.



Their importance comes from the magnetic active regions they identify.



These regions can produce:



\- Solar flares

\- CMEs

\- Energetic particle events



Monitoring sunspots therefore helps scientists assess solar activity and potential space-weather hazards.



\## Why Sunspots Matter for Stellar Physics



Sunspots provide the nearest detailed example of magnetic starspots.



Understanding them helps scientists interpret:



\- Stellar magnetic activity

\- Stellar rotation

\- Stellar flares

\- Exoplanet transit measurements

\- Habitability around active stars



\## Open Questions



Important research questions include:



\- Exactly how are sunspot magnetic fields assembled below the photosphere?

\- What determines whether emerging magnetic flux forms a stable sunspot?

\- What controls the formation of the penumbra?

\- How do sunspots maintain their structure for long periods?

\- What physical mechanisms dominate sunspot decay?

\- How does magnetoconvection operate in the strongest magnetic fields?

\- How does the solar dynamo determine future cycle strength?

\- Why do solar cycles vary in amplitude?

\- Can active-region evolution improve major-flare prediction?

\- How accurately can future solar cycles be predicted?

\- How do sunspots on other stars compare physically with solar sunspots?



\## Summary



Sunspots are temporary dark regions on the Sun's photosphere associated with intense magnetic fields.



They appear dark because they are cooler than the surrounding photosphere, not because they are cold or empty.



A mature sunspot commonly consists of:



\- A dark central umbra

\- A lighter filamentary penumbra



Strong magnetic fields suppress and reorganize convection, reducing the amount of heat reaching the visible surface.



Sunspots frequently occur in groups within solar active regions.



Their magnetic structures can range from simple to extremely complex.



Magnetically complex active regions are more likely to produce powerful:



\- Solar flares

\- Coronal Mass Ejections



Sunspot numbers rise and fall through an approximately 11-year solar cycle.



The Sun's full magnetic polarity pattern repeats over approximately 22 years.



Sunspot latitudes also change systematically during a cycle, producing the characteristic butterfly diagram.



Sunspot observations helped establish that:



\- The Sun rotates

\- The Sun exhibits differential rotation

\- Solar activity varies cyclically

\- Sunspots contain strong magnetic fields



Modern observations use:



\- Magnetograms

\- Spectroscopy

\- Spectropolarimetry

\- Helioseismology

\- Space telescopes

\- High-resolution ground-based solar telescopes



Sunspots are fundamental to understanding solar magnetism, the solar dynamo, space weather, stellar activity, and the magnetic environments surrounding planets.
