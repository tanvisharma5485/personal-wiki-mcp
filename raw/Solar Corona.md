\# Solar Corona



\## Overview



The solar corona is the outermost layer of the Sun's atmosphere.



It extends millions of kilometers into space and gradually transitions into the solar wind.



The corona is important because it is the region where many major solar and heliospheric processes occur, including:



\- Solar wind acceleration

\- Coronal Mass Ejections

\- Magnetic reconnection

\- Coronal loops

\- Solar flares

\- Coronal holes

\- Plasma heating

\- Space-weather generation



The corona is extremely hot, with typical temperatures of roughly:



1 to several million kelvins



and in some active regions it can become even hotter.



\## Location in the Solar Atmosphere



The main layers of the Sun's atmosphere are:



Photosphere



↓



Chromosphere



↓



Transition Region



↓



Corona



The corona lies above the transition region and extends outward into interplanetary space.



\## Photosphere



The photosphere is the visible surface of the Sun.



Its temperature is roughly:



5,800 K



It is much cooler than the corona.



\## Chromosphere



The chromosphere lies above the photosphere.



It has a highly structured and dynamic plasma environment.



Its temperature generally rises with altitude.



\## Transition Region



The transition region separates the cooler chromosphere from the extremely hot corona.



Across a relatively thin region, temperature can increase from:



Tens of thousands of kelvins



to:



Hundreds of thousands or millions of kelvins



\## Corona



The corona is composed of:



\- Ionized plasma

\- Electrons

\- Protons

\- Heavier ions

\- Magnetic fields



It is extremely hot but very low in density compared with the photosphere.



\## Why the Corona Is Difficult to See



Under normal conditions, the bright photosphere overwhelms the faint corona.



The corona is much less luminous than the visible surface of the Sun.



It can be observed clearly during:



\- Total solar eclipses

\- Coronagraph observations



\## Corona During a Total Solar Eclipse



During a total solar eclipse, the Moon blocks the bright photosphere.



The faint corona becomes visible as a glowing structure surrounding the Sun.



It can appear:



\- Streamer-like

\- Feathered

\- Radial

\- Asymmetric



depending on solar activity.



\## Coronagraph



A coronagraph is an instrument that blocks the bright solar disk.



This allows scientists to observe the corona without waiting for a natural eclipse.



Coronagraphs are especially important for detecting:



\- Coronal Mass Ejections

\- Coronal streamers

\- Large-scale coronal structures



\## Discovery Through Eclipses



For centuries, total solar eclipses provided the main opportunity to study the corona.



Modern space-based instruments now observe it continuously.



\## Temperature of the Corona



The solar corona is remarkably hot.



Typical coronal temperatures are around:



1,000,000 K



Active regions may reach:



Several million kelvins



Solar flare plasma can reach:



Tens of millions of kelvins



\## Coronal Heating Problem



One of the major unsolved questions in solar physics is:



Why is the corona much hotter than the photosphere below it?



The photosphere is about:



5,800 K



while the corona reaches:



Millions of kelvins



This is known as the:



Coronal heating problem



\## Why This Is Surprising



Normally, one might expect temperature to decrease with distance from a heat source.



In the Sun's atmosphere, however, temperature rises dramatically above the photosphere.



Therefore, energy must be transported and dissipated in the outer atmosphere.



\## Major Coronal Heating Ideas



Two major classes of mechanisms are commonly discussed:



\- Wave heating

\- Magnetic reconnection and nanoflare heating



These mechanisms may both contribute.



\## Wave Heating



The solar atmosphere contains magnetohydrodynamic waves.



These waves can carry energy upward.



Examples include:



\- Alfvén waves

\- Magnetosonic waves



If their energy is dissipated in the corona, they can contribute to heating.



\## Alfvén Waves



Alfvén waves are plasma waves that propagate along magnetic field lines.



They involve coupled disturbances in:



\- Magnetic fields

\- Plasma motion



They can transport substantial energy from lower solar layers into the corona.



\## Wave Dissipation



Wave energy can be converted into heat through processes such as:



\- Turbulence

\- Resonant interactions

\- Phase mixing

\- Nonlinear effects



\## Nanoflare Heating



Nanoflares are very small magnetic energy-release events.



They were proposed as a possible mechanism for coronal heating.



If enormous numbers of tiny reconnection events occur throughout the corona, their combined energy could contribute significantly to maintaining coronal temperatures.



\## Magnetic Reconnection



Magnetic reconnection occurs when magnetic field structures change their connectivity.



This can rapidly convert magnetic energy into:



\- Heat

\- Plasma motion

\- Particle acceleration



It is fundamental to many coronal processes.



\## Coronal Magnetic Field



The corona is dominated strongly by magnetic fields.



Because the plasma density is low, magnetic forces can control large-scale plasma structures.



Coronal magnetic fields create structures such as:



\- Loops

\- Arcades

\- Streamers

\- Flux ropes

\- Open-field regions



\## Coronal Loops



Coronal loops are arch-shaped plasma structures following magnetic field lines.



They are especially prominent above active regions.



The two ends of a loop are anchored in the lower solar atmosphere.



\## Loop Footpoints



The bases of coronal loops are called:



Footpoints



They are often rooted in regions of strong photospheric magnetic field.



\## Active-Region Loops



Active regions contain especially bright and hot coronal loops.



These loops are associated with:



\- Sunspots

\- Strong magnetic fields

\- Solar flares



\## Quiet-Sun Corona



Not all of the corona belongs to active regions.



The quiet corona covers areas with lower average magnetic activity.



It still contains:



\- Loops

\- Magnetic fields

\- Waves

\- Heating processes



\## Coronal Holes



Coronal holes are large regions of the corona with:



\- Lower plasma density

\- Lower emission in extreme ultraviolet and X-rays

\- Open magnetic field lines



They appear dark in many EUV and X-ray images.



\## Open Magnetic Field Lines



Open magnetic field lines extend outward into the heliosphere instead of closing back onto the Sun nearby.



They provide pathways for solar-wind plasma to escape.



\## Coronal Holes and Solar Wind



Coronal holes are major sources of:



Fast solar wind



High-speed solar-wind streams can originate from these regions.



\## Fast Solar Wind



Fast solar wind can reach speeds around:



700–800 km/s



though exact values vary.



It is often associated with large coronal holes.



\## Slow Solar Wind



Slow solar wind commonly travels around:



300–500 km/s



Its exact origin is more complex.



It is often associated with regions near:



\- Streamer belts

\- Closed magnetic structures

\- Boundaries between open and closed fields



\## Solar Wind Origin



The corona is the source region of the solar wind.



Because the coronal plasma is extremely hot, it expands outward continuously.



This creates the:



Solar wind



\## Parker Solar Wind Model



Eugene Parker developed a theoretical model explaining how the hot corona expands outward into space.



The model predicts that the solar wind accelerates from subsonic to supersonic speeds.



This was a major development in heliophysics.



\## Solar Wind Acceleration



The solar wind does not simply drift away slowly.



It accelerates outward through the corona.



Heating, waves, magnetic fields, and pressure gradients all contribute to this process.



\## Coronal Streamers



Coronal streamers are large bright structures extending outward from the Sun.



They are associated with:



\- Closed magnetic fields

\- Dense plasma

\- Large-scale magnetic structures



They are especially visible during total solar eclipses.



\## Helmet Streamers



Large coronal streamers with cusp-like shapes are called:



Helmet streamers



They often sit above regions of closed magnetic fields.



\## Streamer Belt



A broad region of coronal streamers surrounds the Sun near the boundary between large-scale magnetic polarities.



It is associated with the:



Heliospheric current sheet



\## Heliospheric Current Sheet



The heliospheric current sheet is a large surface in the heliosphere where the polarity of the Sun's magnetic field changes.



Its structure is linked to the large-scale coronal magnetic field.



\## Coronal Plumes



Coronal plumes are elongated structures visible especially inside coronal holes.



They are associated with magnetic-field concentrations and may contribute to solar-wind outflow.



\## Interplume Regions



Regions between plumes are called:



Interplume regions



They may also play an important role in producing fast solar wind.



\## Coronal Bright Points



Coronal bright points are small, localized bright regions seen in EUV and X-ray observations.



They are associated with small magnetic loops and localized energy release.



\## Coronal Jets



Coronal jets are narrow, collimated eruptions of plasma.



They are often associated with:



\- Magnetic reconnection

\- Coronal holes

\- Small active regions



Jets can contribute plasma and energy to the solar wind.



\## Solar Spicules



Spicules are narrow jets of plasma extending upward from the chromosphere.



They may help transport:



\- Mass

\- Energy



into the corona.



Their role in coronal heating remains an active research topic.



\## Prominences



Solar prominences are relatively cool, dense plasma structures suspended in the corona by magnetic fields.



They appear bright when seen beyond the solar limb.



\## Filaments



When the same structures are viewed against the bright solar disk, they are called:



Filaments



\## Prominence Temperature



Prominence plasma is much cooler than surrounding coronal plasma.



Despite being located inside the corona, prominences can remain at temperatures around:



Thousands to tens of thousands of kelvins



because magnetic fields isolate and support the material.



\## Coronal Cavities



Coronal cavities are darker, low-density regions sometimes seen surrounding prominences.



They may contain magnetic flux ropes.



Some cavities erupt as part of CMEs.



\## Flux Ropes



A magnetic flux rope is a twisted bundle of magnetic field lines.



Flux ropes can exist in the corona and may be involved in:



\- Prominences

\- Solar eruptions

\- CMEs



\## Solar Flares in the Corona



Solar flares involve rapid release of magnetic energy in the solar atmosphere.



A large portion of this activity occurs in coronal magnetic structures.



Flares can heat plasma to:



Tens of millions of kelvins



\## Flare Loops



After a solar flare, hot plasma can fill newly reconnected magnetic loops.



These structures are called:



Post-flare loops



\## Current Sheets



Solar eruptions can create narrow regions of concentrated electric current called:



Current sheets



Magnetic reconnection often occurs within these structures.



\## Coronal Mass Ejections



A Coronal Mass Ejection is a large eruption of:



\- Magnetized plasma

\- Coronal magnetic field



into interplanetary space.



CMEs originate in the solar corona.



\## CME Connection to the Corona



Before eruption, CME-related magnetic structures may be stored in the corona.



During eruption:



\- Magnetic structures rise

\- Reconnection occurs

\- Plasma is accelerated outward



\## Coronal Dimming



After a CME eruption, parts of the corona can become temporarily darker in extreme-ultraviolet images.



This is called:



Coronal dimming



It often indicates that plasma has been removed from the corona.



\## EUV Waves



Large solar eruptions can produce broad wave-like disturbances across the corona.



These are often called:



EUV waves



They can propagate across large portions of the solar disk.



\## Coronal Shock Waves



Very fast eruptions can generate shock waves in the corona.



These shocks can accelerate energetic particles.



\## Type II Radio Bursts



Shock waves traveling through the corona can produce:



Type II radio bursts



These are important indicators of CME-driven shocks.



\## Plasma in the Corona



The corona consists mainly of:



\- Electrons

\- Protons

\- Helium nuclei

\- Trace heavier ions



All exist in an ionized plasma state.



\## Ionization



At coronal temperatures, atoms are highly ionized.



Many electrons have been stripped from their atoms.



This produces spectral lines from highly charged ions.



\## Coronal Spectral Lines



Highly ionized elements emit characteristic radiation.



Important elements include:



\- Iron

\- Oxygen

\- Silicon

\- Magnesium

\- Calcium



These emission lines help scientists determine:



\- Temperature

\- Density

\- Plasma motion

\- Composition



\## Highly Ionized Iron



Iron ions are especially useful for diagnosing coronal plasma.



Different iron ionization states form at different temperatures.



This makes them powerful temperature indicators.



\## Coronal Green Line



A famous coronal emission line occurs near:



530.3 nanometers



It is produced by highly ionized iron.



Historically, this line was once attributed to a hypothetical element called:



Coronium



before scientists understood the extreme ionization conditions in the corona.



\## Coronium



Coronium was a proposed unknown element once thought to produce unusual coronal spectral lines.



Later research showed that these lines are produced by highly ionized known elements, especially iron.



\## Spectroscopy of the Corona



Spectroscopy is used to measure:



\- Temperature

\- Density

\- Chemical composition

\- Plasma velocity

\- Ionization state



\## Doppler Shift



Spectral lines shift when plasma moves.



Blueshift:



Motion toward the observer.



Redshift:



Motion away from the observer.



These shifts help measure coronal flows.



\## Coronal Density



The corona has extremely low density compared with the photosphere.



Density decreases rapidly with distance from the Sun.



Near the lower corona, particle densities can still be high enough for frequent interactions.



Farther out, the plasma becomes increasingly tenuous.



\## Coronal Temperature Measurement



Scientists estimate coronal temperature using:



\- Spectral-line ratios

\- Ionization states

\- X-ray emission

\- EUV emission



\## Coronal Electron Temperature



Electron temperatures are commonly around:



1–2 million K



in many coronal regions.



Values vary substantially between:



\- Coronal holes

\- Quiet Sun

\- Active regions

\- Flares



\## Ion Temperature



Ions in the corona can have temperatures different from electrons.



In some regions, heavy ions can have very high effective temperatures.



This is important for understanding solar-wind heating.



\## Collisionless Plasma



As coronal density decreases with height, collisions between particles become less frequent.



The outer corona and solar wind behave increasingly like:



Collisionless plasma



This makes electromagnetic interactions especially important.



\## Plasma Beta



Plasma beta is the ratio of:



Plasma pressure



to:



Magnetic pressure



In many parts of the low corona, plasma beta is low.



This means magnetic forces dominate plasma motion.



\## Low-Beta Corona



In low-beta conditions:



Magnetic pressure > Plasma pressure



Therefore, plasma tends to follow magnetic field structures.



This explains the loop-like appearance of many coronal features.



\## Magnetohydrodynamics



The large-scale behavior of the corona is often described using:



Magnetohydrodynamics



or:



MHD



MHD combines:



\- Fluid dynamics

\- Electromagnetism



to describe magnetized plasma.



\## Magnetic Reconnection in the Corona



Reconnection can occur when magnetic field lines are forced into configurations with strong gradients.



It can release energy rapidly.



It is central to:



\- Solar flares

\- Jets

\- CMEs

\- Small-scale heating events



\## Magnetic Energy Storage



Coronal magnetic fields can store substantial free energy.



This energy can build up through:



\- Photospheric motions

\- Magnetic flux emergence

\- Magnetic shear

\- Twisting



\## Photospheric Footpoint Motion



Because coronal magnetic fields are anchored in the dense lower atmosphere, motion of their footpoints can:



\- Twist loops

\- Shear fields

\- Inject magnetic energy



into the corona.



\## Magnetic Braiding



Photospheric convection can shuffle magnetic footpoints.



This may braid magnetic field lines in the corona.



Magnetic braiding is one proposed pathway for transferring energy into coronal heating.



\## Parker Nanoflare Concept



Eugene Parker proposed that continual small-scale reconnection events caused by magnetic braiding could produce nanoflares.



These tiny events may contribute to coronal heating.



\## Coronal Turbulence



The corona contains turbulent plasma motions.



Turbulence can transfer energy from large scales to smaller scales where it is converted into heat.



\## Turbulent Cascade



In a turbulent cascade:



Large-scale energy



↓



Intermediate scales



↓



Small scales



↓



Dissipation and heating



This process may contribute substantially to coronal and solar-wind heating.



\## Alfvénic Turbulence



Interactions between Alfvén waves traveling along magnetic fields can generate turbulence.



This may help heat the corona and accelerate the solar wind.



\## Coronal Seismology



Waves and oscillations in coronal loops can be used to infer physical properties of the corona.



This technique is called:



Coronal seismology



It can provide estimates of:



\- Magnetic-field strength

\- Density

\- Temperature

\- Loop structure



\## Loop Oscillations



Coronal loops can oscillate after disturbances such as:



\- Solar flares

\- CMEs



The oscillations can reveal properties of the coronal magnetic field.



\## Kink Oscillations



A coronal loop can sway sideways.



This is called a:



Kink oscillation



It is useful in coronal seismology.



\## Slow Magnetoacoustic Waves



Coronal loops can support compressive waves known as:



Slow magnetoacoustic waves



These may appear as periodic intensity disturbances.



\## Coronal Heating Through Waves



If waves lose energy while propagating, that energy can become heat.



Scientists study damping of coronal waves to estimate how much heating they provide.



\## Heating Requirements



To maintain million-kelvin temperatures, the corona must receive continual energy input.



Different regions require different heating rates.



Active regions generally need more energy than coronal holes.



\## Energy Source



Ultimately, the energy powering coronal heating comes from processes originating in the Sun's interior and lower atmosphere.



These include:



\- Convection

\- Magnetic-field generation

\- Photospheric motions



\## Solar Magnetic Field



The Sun's magnetic field connects the photosphere, corona, solar wind, and heliosphere.



The corona is a critical region where magnetic energy is redistributed.



\## Open and Closed Magnetic Fields



Coronal magnetic fields can be divided broadly into:



Closed field:



Both ends connect back to the Sun.



Open field:



Extends outward into the heliosphere.



\## Closed-Field Regions



Closed magnetic loops dominate many:



\- Active regions

\- Streamer regions

\- Quiet-Sun structures



Plasma can become trapped along these loops.



\## Open-Field Regions



Open magnetic fields are strongly associated with:



\- Coronal holes

\- Solar-wind escape



\## Source Surface Models



Scientists use models to estimate the coronal magnetic field.



One common approximation is the:



Potential Field Source Surface model



or:



PFSS



\## PFSS Model



The PFSS model assumes the coronal magnetic field is approximately current-free below an artificial outer boundary called the:



Source surface



At that boundary, field lines are forced to become radial.



It provides a simplified model of global coronal magnetic structure.



\## Coronal Magnetic Field Measurement



Direct measurement of magnetic fields in the corona is difficult because the corona is faint.



Scientists therefore use:



\- Photospheric magnetograms

\- Spectropolarimetry

\- Coronal emission

\- Radio observations

\- Magnetic extrapolation models



\## Magnetograms



Magnetograms map magnetic fields on the photosphere.



They provide boundary conditions for models of the coronal magnetic field.



\## Coronal Magnetic Extrapolation



Scientists extrapolate magnetic fields upward from photospheric measurements.



Common approaches include:



\- Potential field models

\- Nonlinear force-free field models



\## Force-Free Magnetic Fields



In low-beta coronal regions, magnetic forces can dominate.



Some models assume electric currents flow mainly along magnetic field lines.



These are called:



Force-free magnetic fields



\## Nonlinear Force-Free Field Model



NLFFF models attempt to reconstruct complex current-carrying coronal magnetic fields.



They are often used to study:



\- Active regions

\- Free magnetic energy

\- Flux ropes

\- Flare potential



\## Coronal Energy Budget



The energy entering the corona must support:



\- Coronal heating

\- Solar-wind acceleration

\- Radiation

\- Particle acceleration

\- Eruptions



Understanding this energy distribution is a central goal of solar physics.



\## Corona and Solar Wind Connection



The corona gradually transitions into the solar wind.



There is no sharp outer boundary where the corona suddenly ends.



Instead, the hot plasma expands continuously outward.



\## Sonic Point



As solar wind accelerates, it transitions from:



Subsonic



to:



Supersonic



The location where flow speed equals the sound speed is called the:



Sonic point



\## Alfvén Critical Surface



Farther out, the solar wind eventually exceeds the Alfvén speed.



The boundary where the flow speed equals the Alfvén speed is called the:



Alfvén critical surface



Beyond this region, disturbances cannot travel upstream against the solar wind as easily.



\## Parker Solar Probe and the Alfvén Surface



Parker Solar Probe has traveled through regions near and inside the Alfvén critical surface.



These measurements provide direct information about:



\- Coronal plasma

\- Magnetic fields

\- Solar-wind acceleration



\## Solar Probe Measurements



Near-Sun spacecraft measure:



\- Magnetic fields

\- Plasma velocity

\- Density

\- Temperature

\- Energetic particles

\- Waves



These direct measurements help connect remote observations of the corona to the solar wind.



\## Parker Solar Probe



NASA's Parker Solar Probe was launched in:



2018



Its mission is to investigate:



\- Coronal heating

\- Solar-wind acceleration

\- Energetic particles

\- Near-Sun magnetic fields



\## Solar Orbiter



Solar Orbiter studies the Sun using both:



\- Remote-sensing instruments

\- In-situ instruments



It observes:



\- Corona

\- Solar magnetic fields

\- Solar wind

\- Solar eruptions



\## Solar Dynamics Observatory



The Solar Dynamics Observatory, or:



SDO



provides continuous high-resolution imaging of the Sun.



Its observations include extreme-ultraviolet images of the corona.



\## Atmospheric Imaging Assembly



The Atmospheric Imaging Assembly, or:



AIA



aboard SDO observes the Sun in several ultraviolet and EUV wavelengths.



Different channels correspond approximately to plasma at different temperatures.



\## SOHO



The Solar and Heliospheric Observatory, or:



SOHO



has studied the Sun since the 1990s.



It has observed:



\- Corona

\- Solar wind

\- CMEs

\- Solar interior



\## LASCO



LASCO is a set of coronagraph instruments aboard SOHO.



It has detected thousands of CMEs.



\## Hinode



Hinode is a solar spacecraft that studies:



\- Solar magnetic fields

\- Corona

\- Photosphere

\- Chromosphere



It provides high-resolution observations relevant to coronal heating.



\## TRACE



The Transition Region and Coronal Explorer, or:



TRACE



was a mission that produced detailed ultraviolet images of coronal loops and transition-region structures.



\## Yohkoh



Yohkoh was a solar mission focused strongly on X-ray observations of the Sun.



It helped study:



\- Solar flares

\- Hot coronal plasma



\## Skylab



Skylab carried important solar telescopes.



Its observations in the 1970s helped establish the importance of:



\- Coronal holes

\- X-ray structure of the corona



\## Coronal Holes and Skylab



Skylab observations revealed large dark regions in solar X-ray images.



These were identified as coronal holes and linked to high-speed solar wind.



\## X-Ray Corona



Because coronal plasma is extremely hot, it emits strongly in:



\- Extreme ultraviolet

\- X-rays



This allows hot coronal structures to be observed from space.



\## Why Space Observations Are Needed



Earth's atmosphere absorbs much of:



\- X-ray radiation

\- Extreme-ultraviolet radiation



Therefore, spacecraft are required for continuous observation at these wavelengths.



\## Radio Observations



The corona can also be studied using radio waves.



Radio observations reveal:



\- Plasma density

\- Shock waves

\- Energetic particles

\- Magnetic structures



\## Plasma Frequency



Radio emission and propagation in plasma depend on electron density.



The plasma frequency provides a way to estimate density in the corona.



\## Radio Bursts



Solar activity can produce radio bursts that originate in the corona.



Examples include:



\- Type II bursts

\- Type III bursts

\- Type IV bursts



\## Type III Bursts



Type III radio bursts are produced by energetic electrons traveling rapidly along open magnetic field lines.



They reveal particle escape through the corona.



\## Type IV Bursts



Type IV radio emission can come from energetic electrons trapped in large magnetic structures.



It can be associated with:



\- Flares

\- CMEs



\## White-Light Corona



The visible-light corona contains several components.



Traditionally they include:



\- K-corona

\- F-corona

\- E-corona



\## K-Corona



The K-corona is produced by sunlight scattered by free electrons in the coronal plasma.



It is highly polarized.



Its structure closely traces electron density and magnetic features.



\## F-Corona



The F-corona is sunlight scattered by interplanetary dust.



It becomes increasingly important farther from the Sun.



\## E-Corona



The E-corona consists of emission lines produced by highly ionized atoms in the coronal plasma.



\## Thomson Scattering



The K-corona is observed through:



Thomson scattering



where sunlight is scattered by free electrons.



This process is also important in CME observations.



\## Coronal Polarization



Polarization measurements help determine:



\- Coronal electron density

\- Geometry

\- Magnetic-field properties



\## Coronal Abundance



The corona contains the same basic elements as the Sun but measured elemental abundances can differ from photospheric values.



One important phenomenon is the:



FIP effect



\## First Ionization Potential Effect



The First Ionization Potential, or FIP, effect describes systematic differences in the abundance of certain elements between the photosphere and corona.



Elements with low first ionization potential can become enhanced in some coronal plasma.



\## FIP Bias



The degree of elemental fractionation is often called:



FIP bias



It varies between:



\- Active regions

\- Coronal holes

\- Solar wind streams



\## Coronal Composition and Solar Wind



Elemental and ionic composition measured in the solar wind provides clues about where the plasma originated in the corona.



\## Charge States



Ion charge states become largely fixed as coronal plasma expands outward.



These charge states can reveal the temperature conditions near the Sun.



This is called:



Freeze-in



\## Freeze-In Temperature



As plasma density decreases, collisions become too rare to maintain ionization equilibrium.



Ionization states effectively become frozen.



Their measured distribution in the solar wind can therefore preserve information about coronal temperature.



\## Coronal Mass Loss



The Sun continuously loses mass through the solar wind.



The corona is the source of this escaping material.



The mass-loss rate is small compared with the Sun's total mass.



\## Solar Mass-Loss Rate



The Sun loses roughly:



10^9 kilograms per second



through the solar wind.



Despite this large number, it is negligible compared with the Sun's total mass on human timescales.



\## Corona and Planetary Environments



The corona indirectly affects every planet through the solar wind and solar eruptions.



Its influence includes:



\- Magnetospheric compression

\- Atmospheric escape

\- Auroras

\- Radiation conditions

\- Space weather



\## Corona and Mercury



Mercury lies close to the Sun.



Its magnetosphere experiences strong solar-wind pressure originating from the corona.



\## Corona and Venus



Venus lacks a strong intrinsic global magnetic field.



Solar-wind plasma interacts directly with its upper atmosphere and induced magnetosphere.



\## Corona and Earth



The corona influences Earth through:



\- Solar wind

\- CMEs

\- Energetic particles

\- Magnetic fields



These processes can affect:



\- Magnetosphere

\- Ionosphere

\- Satellites

\- Power grids



\## Corona and Mars



Solar activity from the corona can affect atmospheric escape at Mars.



Strong solar events increase:



\- Ionization

\- Heating

\- Particle interactions



\## Corona and Giant Planets



Solar-wind disturbances originating in the corona eventually reach:



\- Jupiter

\- Saturn

\- Uranus

\- Neptune



Their influence weakens and evolves with distance but remains detectable.



\## Corona and Comets



Comet ion tails interact with the solar wind originating in the corona.



Coronal eruptions can cause:



\- Tail distortion

\- Tail disconnection events



\## Corona and the Heliosphere



The heliosphere is created by the expansion of solar plasma and magnetic fields into interstellar space.



The corona is therefore the inner source region of the heliosphere.



\## Corona and Space Weather



Many major space-weather disturbances originate in the corona.



These include:



\- CMEs

\- Solar flares

\- Energetic particle acceleration

\- High-speed solar-wind streams



\## Corona and Solar Flares



Flare energy release occurs mainly in magnetically stressed solar atmospheric structures.



The corona plays a central role because it stores large amounts of magnetic free energy.



\## Corona and CMEs



CMEs represent some of the largest-scale reorganizations of the solar corona.



A CME can remove large quantities of plasma and magnetic flux from the Sun.



\## Corona and Energetic Particles



Particles can be accelerated in the corona by:



\- Magnetic reconnection

\- CME-driven shocks

\- Turbulence



These particles may escape into interplanetary space.



\## Solar Energetic Particle Events



Some solar eruptions produce high-energy:



\- Protons

\- Electrons

\- Heavy ions



These particles can pose radiation hazards to spacecraft and astronauts.



\## Coronal Magnetic Topology



Magnetic topology describes how field lines are connected.



Important structures include:



\- Null points

\- Separatrices

\- Separators

\- Quasi-separatrix layers



These regions can be favorable sites for magnetic reconnection.



\## Magnetic Null Point



A magnetic null is a location where the magnetic field strength approaches zero.



Complex reconnection can occur near these regions.



\## Separatrix



A separatrix is a boundary separating magnetic field regions with different connectivity.



\## Quasi-Separatrix Layer



A quasi-separatrix layer is a region where magnetic-field connectivity changes rapidly but continuously.



It is important in modern flare and reconnection models.



\## Coronal Magnetic Arcades



An arcade is a series of neighboring coronal loops.



Arcades can appear:



\- Above polarity inversion lines

\- After solar flares

\- Around filaments



\## Post-Flare Arcade



After major eruptions, newly reconnected magnetic loops can form a large:



Post-flare arcade



\## Sigmoids



Some active regions show S-shaped or reverse-S-shaped structures in X-ray or EUV images.



These are called:



Sigmoids



They can indicate twisted or sheared magnetic fields and are sometimes associated with eruptions.



\## Coronal Flux Rope



A flux rope can appear as:



\- Twisted field structure

\- Cavity

\- Hot-channel feature



It can become unstable and erupt as a CME.



\## Solar Eruption Sequence



A simplified sequence is:



Magnetic energy accumulates in the corona



↓



Magnetic field becomes unstable



↓



Flux rope or filament begins to rise



↓



Current sheet forms



↓



Magnetic reconnection occurs



↓



Solar flare develops



↓



CME accelerates outward



↓



Post-flare loops form



↓



Corona gradually restructures



\## Coronal Recovery



After a major eruption, the corona can take hours or longer to return toward a more stable configuration.



Magnetic structures continue evolving after the visible flare fades.



\## Coronal Rain



Hot plasma in coronal loops can cool and condense.



Dense material may then fall toward the lower atmosphere along magnetic field lines.



This phenomenon is called:



Coronal rain



\## Thermal Instability



Coronal rain can result from:



Thermal instability



where radiative cooling causes plasma to condense rapidly.



\## Coronal Condensations



Dense cool material can temporarily form inside otherwise hot coronal loops.



These structures demonstrate the highly dynamic nature of coronal plasma.



\## Loop Heating



Coronal loops can be heated:



\- Uniformly

\- Near their footpoints

\- Intermittently



Determining the exact heating distribution is an active research problem.



\## Loop Cooling



After heating, loops cool through:



\- Thermal conduction

\- Radiation

\- Mass flows



\## Thermal Conduction



Heat moves efficiently along magnetic field lines in hot coronal plasma.



Thermal conduction is especially important at high temperatures.



\## Radiative Cooling



Coronal plasma emits radiation and loses thermal energy.



Radiative cooling becomes especially important at higher densities and lower coronal temperatures.



\## Emission Measure



Emission measure is related to:



Electron density squared integrated along the line of sight.



It is used to estimate how much emitting plasma is present.



\## Differential Emission Measure



Differential Emission Measure, or:



DEM



describes the amount of plasma present across different temperatures.



It is widely used in coronal temperature analysis.



\## Coronal Diagnostics



Scientists diagnose coronal conditions using:



\- Spectral-line ratios

\- Imaging

\- Polarization

\- Radio emission

\- Seismology

\- In-situ measurements



\## Density-Sensitive Spectral Lines



Ratios of certain spectral lines change with electron density.



These provide estimates of coronal plasma density.



\## Temperature-Sensitive Lines



Different ions form most strongly at different temperatures.



Comparing their emission helps determine coronal temperature distribution.



\## Non-Equilibrium Ionization



Rapidly heated or cooling plasma may not immediately reach ionization equilibrium.



This is called:



Non-equilibrium ionization



It can affect interpretations of coronal spectra.



\## Coronal Heating Timescales



Heating can occur over:



\- Seconds

\- Minutes

\- Longer intervals



The corona likely experiences both steady and impulsive heating.



\## Impulsive Heating



Impulsive heating occurs through short energy-release events.



Examples may include:



\- Nanoflares

\- Microflares

\- Reconnection events



\## Steady Heating



Some coronal structures may receive energy at rates that appear relatively continuous over observational timescales.



\## Coronal Microflares



Microflares are small flare-like events.



They occur frequently and may contribute to coronal energy input.



\## Brightenings



Small transient brightenings are common throughout the corona.



They may reflect localized:



\- Heating

\- Reconnection

\- Plasma flows



\## Quiet-Sun Magnetic Network



Even regions without large sunspots contain small magnetic structures.



These generate many small coronal loops and transient events.



\## Magnetic Carpet



The constantly changing pattern of small-scale magnetic fields across the solar surface is sometimes called the:



Magnetic carpet



Continual emergence, movement, and cancellation may inject energy into the corona.



\## Flux Cancellation



Opposite magnetic polarities can converge and disappear from photospheric observations.



This is called:



Magnetic flux cancellation



It can be associated with reconnection and coronal heating.



\## Flux Emergence



New magnetic field continually emerges through the photosphere.



This creates new loops and can trigger coronal activity.



\## Corona Across the Solar Cycle



The appearance of the corona changes substantially over the approximately 11-year solar cycle.



\## Corona at Solar Minimum



Near solar minimum:



\- Large polar coronal holes are common

\- Streamers are concentrated nearer the equatorial region

\- Overall structure is relatively ordered



\## Corona at Solar Maximum



Near solar maximum:



\- Magnetic structure becomes more complex

\- Streamers appear at many latitudes

\- Active regions are numerous

\- CMEs occur more frequently



\## Eclipse Corona and Solar Cycle



Historical eclipse photographs clearly show that coronal shape changes with the solar cycle.



Solar minimum corona:



Often more equatorially extended.



Solar maximum corona:



Often more irregular and distributed around the Sun.



\## Solar Magnetic Reversal



Near solar maximum, the Sun's global magnetic field reverses polarity.



This strongly reorganizes coronal structure.



\## Coronal Holes and Solar Cycle



Large polar coronal holes dominate much of solar minimum.



They shrink and reorganize as solar maximum approaches.



\## Corona on Other Stars



Stars with hot outer atmospheres can also possess:



Stellar coronae



These coronae can reach temperatures of millions of kelvins.



\## Stellar Corona



A stellar corona is the hot outer atmosphere of a star.



Magnetically active stars can produce strong:



\- X-ray emission

\- Stellar flares

\- Stellar winds



\## X-Ray Astronomy and Stellar Coronae



Many stars emit X-rays because of hot coronal plasma.



X-ray observations provide important measurements of stellar magnetic activity.



\## Red Dwarf Coronae



Active red dwarfs can possess very hot and energetic coronae.



Their high-energy emission can strongly affect nearby planets.



\## Young Stars



Young stars generally rotate faster and often show stronger magnetic activity.



Their coronae can be more luminous in X-rays than the present-day Sun.



\## Stellar Corona and Exoplanets



A planet orbiting an active star can be exposed to:



\- X-rays

\- EUV radiation

\- Stellar wind

\- Flares

\- Possible stellar CMEs



These can influence atmospheric evolution.



\## Atmospheric Escape



Strong XUV radiation from a stellar corona can heat planetary upper atmospheres.



This can contribute to:



Atmospheric escape



especially for planets orbiting close to their stars.



\## XUV Radiation



XUV broadly refers to:



\- X-ray

\- Extreme-ultraviolet



radiation.



The solar corona is a major source of XUV emission.



\## XUV and Early Planetary Atmospheres



The young Sun likely emitted more high-energy radiation than today.



This may have influenced atmospheric evolution on:



\- Earth

\- Venus

\- Mars



\## Corona and Habitability



Stellar coronal activity is relevant to planetary habitability because intense XUV emission can alter:



\- Atmospheric retention

\- Atmospheric chemistry

\- Surface radiation indirectly



\## Corona and the Young Sun



The young Sun likely had:



\- Faster rotation

\- Stronger magnetic activity

\- More intense X-ray emission

\- Stronger stellar wind



Its corona therefore played an important role in early Solar System evolution.



\## Coronal Observation Challenges



The corona is difficult to study because it is:



\- Faint

\- Hot

\- Low density

\- Highly dynamic

\- Magnetically complex



Many important quantities cannot be measured directly.



\## Line-of-Sight Integration



Coronal images combine emission from plasma along the observer's entire line of sight.



This can make three-dimensional interpretation difficult.



\## Three-Dimensional Corona



The corona is inherently three-dimensional.



Different structures overlap in two-dimensional telescope images.



Multiple viewing angles help reconstruct its geometry.



\## Tomography



Solar rotational tomography uses observations over time to reconstruct three-dimensional coronal structure.



\## Stereoscopic Observation



Spacecraft at different locations can observe the same coronal feature from multiple angles.



This allows stereoscopic reconstruction.



\## Corona and AI



Machine-learning techniques are increasingly used to analyze:



\- EUV images

\- Coronal-hole boundaries

\- CME detection

\- Magnetic fields

\- Solar-wind sources

\- Flare prediction



\## Coronal Hole Detection With AI



Automated systems can identify coronal holes using combinations of:



\- EUV intensity

\- Magnetic-field information

\- Image segmentation



\## CME Detection Algorithms



Automated image-processing and machine-learning systems can detect expanding CMEs in coronagraph data.



\## Coronal Forecasting



Improved models aim to predict:



\- Solar-wind speed

\- CME propagation

\- Magnetic connectivity

\- Space-weather conditions



\## Common Misconceptions



\### The Corona Is Cooler Because It Is Farther From the Sun's Interior



Incorrect.



The corona is much hotter than the photosphere.



Its high temperature is maintained by energy transferred through magnetic and wave processes.



\### The Corona Is the Sun's Surface



Incorrect.



The photosphere is the visible surface.



The corona is the outermost atmosphere.



\### The Corona Can Only Be Seen During an Eclipse



Incorrect.



Coronagraphs and space telescopes observe it continuously.



\### The Corona Is Empty Space



Incorrect.



It contains low-density plasma and magnetic fields.



\### The Corona Is a Solid Layer



Incorrect.



It is a highly dynamic magnetized plasma.



\### Coronal Holes Are Actual Holes in the Sun



Incorrect.



They are low-density regions containing open magnetic fields.



\### The Corona Is Uniform



Incorrect.



It contains:



\- Loops

\- Holes

\- Streamers

\- Jets

\- Prominences

\- Active regions



\### The Solar Wind Begins Far From the Sun



Incorrect.



The solar wind originates in and accelerates through the corona.



\### Every Coronal Loop Produces a Flare



Incorrect.



Most loops do not produce major eruptions.



\### Coronal Heating Is Fully Solved



Incorrect.



Wave heating, reconnection, turbulence, and nanoflares are all actively studied.



\## Important Terminology



\### Solar Corona



Outermost layer of the Sun's atmosphere.



\### Coronal Loop



Magnetically confined arch of hot plasma.



\### Coronal Hole



Low-density region of open magnetic field that can produce high-speed solar wind.



\### Coronal Streamer



Large bright structure associated with closed coronal magnetic fields.



\### Helmet Streamer



Large cusp-shaped coronal streamer.



\### Coronal Heating Problem



Question of why the corona reaches temperatures of millions of kelvins.



\### Nanoflare



Very small magnetic energy-release event proposed as a contributor to coronal heating.



\### Alfvén Wave



Magnetohydrodynamic wave traveling along magnetic fields.



\### Magnetic Reconnection



Change in magnetic-field connectivity that converts magnetic energy into other forms.



\### Coronal Mass Ejection



Large eruption of magnetized coronal plasma.



\### Coronal Dimming



Temporary EUV darkening caused by plasma evacuation during some eruptions.



\### Coronal Seismology



Study of coronal plasma properties using waves and oscillations.



\### Magnetic Flux Rope



Twisted bundle of magnetic field lines.



\### Solar Wind



Continuous outward flow of plasma from the Sun.



\### K-Corona



Visible corona produced by sunlight scattered by free electrons.



\### F-Corona



Component of the outer visible corona produced mainly by sunlight scattered by interplanetary dust.



\### E-Corona



Coronal emission-line component produced by highly ionized atoms.



\### FIP Effect



Elemental abundance fractionation associated with first ionization potential.



\### Plasma Beta



Ratio of plasma pressure to magnetic pressure.



\### Alfvén Critical Surface



Region where solar-wind flow speed equals the Alfvén speed.

\## Summary



The solar corona is the Sun's outermost atmospheric layer.



It lies above the:



\- Photosphere

\- Chromosphere

\- Transition region



The corona reaches temperatures of:



Millions of kelvins



even though the photosphere below it is only about:



5,800 K



Explaining this extreme temperature difference is known as the:



Coronal heating problem



The corona is a low-density magnetized plasma dominated by structures such as:



\- Coronal loops

\- Coronal holes

\- Streamers

\- Flux ropes

\- Prominences

\- Jets



Coronal holes contain open magnetic fields and are major sources of fast solar wind.



Closed magnetic-field regions form loops and streamers.



Magnetic reconnection plays an important role in:



\- Solar flares

\- CMEs

\- Jets

\- Coronal heating



The solar wind originates in the corona and accelerates outward to create the heliosphere.



Important missions studying the corona include:



\- Parker Solar Probe

\- Solar Orbiter

\- Solar Dynamics Observatory

\- SOHO

\- Hinode



Scientists observe the corona using:



\- Extreme-ultraviolet imaging

\- X-rays

\- Spectroscopy

\- Radio astronomy

\- Coronagraphs

\- In-situ spacecraft



The corona is fundamental to understanding solar magnetic activity, space weather, plasma physics, planetary environments, and stellar activity.
