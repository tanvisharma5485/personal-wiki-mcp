\# Charon



\## Overview



Charon is the largest moon of \[\[Pluto]] and one of the most remarkable satellite systems in the outer Solar System. It is unusually large compared with the body it orbits: Charon's diameter is a little more than half that of Pluto. Because of this unusually high moon-to-primary size ratio, Pluto and Charon are sometimes informally described as a double dwarf-planet system, although Charon is officially classified as a natural satellite.



Charon is an icy, rocky world with a diameter of about 1,212 kilometers. Its surface contains extensive water ice, impact craters, enormous tectonic fractures, mountains, plains, and a distinctive reddish polar region.



The Pluto-Charon system is also dynamically unusual. Pluto and Charon are mutually tidally locked, so each continuously presents approximately the same hemisphere toward the other. Their common center of mass, or barycenter, lies outside Pluto itself.



Charon is scientifically important for understanding \[\[Natural Satellites]], giant impacts, tidal evolution, \[\[Planetary Tectonics]], icy-world geology, \[\[Planetary Differentiation]], and the evolution of \[\[Trans-Neptunian Objects]].



\## Discovery and Physical Characteristics



Charon was discovered in 1978 by astronomer James Christy while examining photographic observations of Pluto. He noticed that Pluto appeared periodically elongated. Further observations showed that the changing appearance was caused by a previously unknown satellite.



The moon was later named Charon. In Greek mythology, Charon is the ferryman who transports souls across the river separating the world of the living from the underworld, making the name appropriate for a companion of Pluto.



Charon has a diameter of approximately 1,212 kilometers, compared with Pluto's roughly 2,377 kilometers. This makes Charon exceptionally large relative to its primary.



Its mass is approximately one-eighth that of Pluto. Its density is around 1.7 grams per cubic centimeter, indicating that it contains substantial amounts of both rock and water ice.



Unlike tiny irregular moons such as \[\[Phobos]] and \[\[Deimos]], Charon is massive enough for its gravity to pull it into an approximately spherical shape.



Its surface gravity is much weaker than Earth's but strong enough to control geological processes and retain substantial surface material.



\## Orbit and the Pluto-Charon Binary System



Charon orbits Pluto at a distance of roughly 19,600 kilometers from Pluto's center and completes one orbit in about 6.4 Earth days.



Pluto itself rotates once in approximately the same period.



This is because the two bodies have reached mutual synchronous rotation through billions of years of \[\[Tides|tidal evolution]].



Charon always presents approximately the same hemisphere toward Pluto, while Pluto also presents approximately the same hemisphere toward Charon.



This differs from the Earth-Moon system. Earth's \[\[Moon]] is tidally locked to Earth, but Earth is not tidally locked to the Moon.



The Pluto-Charon system has therefore reached a more advanced tidal equilibrium.



Because Charon is relatively massive, Pluto does not simply sit at the center while Charon moves around it. Instead, both bodies orbit their common center of mass.



This center is called the barycenter.



The Pluto-Charon barycenter lies outside Pluto's physical surface. This unusual geometry is one reason the pair is sometimes informally described as a binary or double dwarf-planet system.



However, the International Astronomical Union currently classifies Pluto as a dwarf planet and Charon as its moon.



\## Formation of Charon



The leading explanation for Charon's origin involves a giant impact early in the history of the outer Solar System.



A large object may have collided with the young Pluto or a Pluto-like precursor. The collision could have thrown material into orbit around the surviving body.



Some of this debris subsequently accumulated to form Charon.



This broad mechanism resembles the leading explanation for the formation of Earth's Moon, although the physical conditions and compositions were different.



The Pluto-Charon impact may have involved two partially differentiated icy-rocky bodies. Depending on the impact geometry, material from their outer ice-rich layers could have been placed into orbit while much of the denser rock remained in Pluto.



The system's large angular momentum and Charon's unusually high mass relative to Pluto support an impact-related origin.



The four much smaller moons—Styx, Nix, Kerberos, and Hydra—may also have formed from debris associated with the same ancient event.



Understanding this formation process provides information about collisions among large bodies during \[\[Planet Formation]] in the primordial \[\[Kuiper Belt]].



\## Surface Composition and Environment



Charon's surface is dominated by water ice. Spectroscopic observations show strong water-ice absorption features, making it compositionally different from Pluto's surface, which contains large amounts of nitrogen, methane, and carbon monoxide ice.



Ammonia-bearing compounds have also been detected in some regions of Charon. Ammonia is scientifically interesting because it can lower the freezing temperature of water and may therefore have influenced past internal or cryovolcanic processes.



Charon has no substantial atmosphere. Its gravity is too weak and its accessible volatile inventory too limited to maintain a Pluto-like global atmosphere.



The surface is extremely cold, with temperatures only tens of kelvins above absolute zero.



At these temperatures, water ice behaves mechanically as geological bedrock.



Charon's surface has been exposed for billions of years to ultraviolet radiation, solar wind, Galactic cosmic rays, and micrometeorite impacts. These \[\[Space Environment|space-weathering]] processes gradually modify exposed material.



Despite its cold environment, Charon is not a geologically featureless frozen body. Observations by \[\[New Horizons]] revealed evidence of extensive tectonic and possibly cryovolcanic evolution.



\## Mordor Macula and the Red North Pole



One of Charon's most distinctive features is a large reddish region around its north pole, informally known as Mordor Macula.



The coloration is thought to be connected to material originating from Pluto.



Methane can escape from Pluto's atmosphere and travel through space. Some of these molecules reach Charon and become trapped on its extremely cold polar surface, particularly during the long polar winter.



Solar ultraviolet radiation and energetic particles then chemically transform the methane into heavier, more complex organic-rich compounds.



These materials can develop a reddish appearance and are often broadly described as tholin-like substances.



This process provides an important example of material transfer between two planetary bodies.



Charon's polar coloration may therefore partly consist of chemically processed material originally supplied by Pluto.



Seasonal cycles are important because the Pluto-Charon system experiences extremely long seasons during its roughly 248-year orbit around the Sun.



\## Tectonics and the Great Equatorial Fracture System



One of the most surprising discoveries from the New Horizons flyby was the scale of Charon's \[\[Planetary Tectonics]].



A vast system of fractures, scarps, valleys, and canyons stretches across much of the moon.



Some canyons are several kilometers deep, and the tectonic system extends for more than a thousand kilometers.



This enormous fracture belt suggests that Charon's crust experienced global extension.



One possible explanation involves the freezing of an ancient subsurface ocean.



Liquid water occupies a different volume than the ice phases produced during freezing. As an internal ocean gradually froze, expansion could place the outer ice shell under tension.



The crust would then fracture, producing large extensional faults and canyons.



This mechanism provides indirect evidence that Charon may once have contained substantial internal liquid water.



\## Northern and Southern Terrains



Charon's surface can broadly be divided into different geological terrains.



The northern regions contain more rugged and heavily cratered landscapes, suggesting relatively old surfaces.



South of the major tectonic belt lies a smoother region known as Vulcan Planitia.



The contrast between these terrains indicates that Charon experienced substantial geological evolution rather than remaining unchanged since formation.



The smoother southern plains may have been resurfaced after the formation of much of the older terrain.



\## Vulcan Planitia and Possible Cryovolcanism



Vulcan Planitia is a broad, relatively smooth plain in Charon's southern hemisphere.



Its surface contains fewer large craters than some northern regions and displays features consistent with extensive resurfacing.



One major hypothesis proposes that Vulcan Planitia formed through \[\[Cryovolcanism]].



Instead of molten silicate rock, cryovolcanism involves water-rich or other volatile-rich material moving from the interior toward the surface.



If Charon once possessed a subsurface ocean, water mixed with ammonia or other compounds may have erupted through fractures.



This material could spread across the surface and freeze, creating broad plains.



Such a process is sometimes called cryolava flooding.



The interpretation remains under investigation, but Vulcan Planitia is one of the strongest candidates for large-scale ancient cryovolcanic resurfacing on Charon.



\## Mountains, Moats and Impact Craters



Charon contains mountains rising several kilometers above surrounding terrain.



One particularly unusual feature observed by New Horizons is a mountain located inside a depression or moat.



This morphology is uncommon and its exact formation mechanism remains uncertain.



The surface also contains numerous impact craters. Crater density varies between terrains, helping scientists estimate relative surface ages.



Older surfaces generally accumulate more craters, while geological resurfacing can erase or bury previous impacts.



Charon's crater distribution also provides information about the population of small objects in the \[\[Kuiper Belt]]. Because small distant bodies are difficult to observe directly, impact craters on Pluto and Charon act as an indirect record of their abundance.



New Horizons observations found fewer very small craters in some regions than expected under certain earlier models. This suggests that the Kuiper Belt may contain fewer extremely small objects than would result from a simple long-term collisional cascade.



This evidence contributes to modern theories in which planetesimals can form relatively rapidly at substantial sizes.



\## Interior and Thermal Evolution



Charon probably contains a mixture of rock and water ice.



Its density indicates a significant rocky component, while the surface is dominated by ice.



Early in its history, Charon may have contained enough heat for internal differentiation. Heat sources could include energy from its formation, radioactive decay within rocky material, tidal interactions with Pluto, and the giant impact associated with its origin.



If internal temperatures became high enough, water ice could melt and separate from denser rock. This would produce a differentiated structure containing a rocky interior and water-rich outer layers.



A subsurface ocean may have existed between the rocky interior and outer ice shell.



As Charon cooled, this ocean could gradually freeze.



Freezing and associated volume changes may have generated the global extensional stresses responsible for the enormous tectonic fracture system.



Ammonia-bearing compounds could have acted as antifreeze, lowering water's freezing temperature and allowing liquid to survive longer.



Whether any liquid remains inside Charon today is unknown.



Because Charon is much smaller than major ocean-world moons such as Europa or Ganymede, maintaining a present-day ocean is more difficult, but its ancient geological record strongly suggests significant internal evolution.



\## Tidal Evolution



The modern Pluto-Charon system is the result of billions of years of tidal interaction.



Immediately after Charon formed, it probably orbited much closer to Pluto, while Pluto rotated significantly faster than it does today.



Tidal forces transferred angular momentum between Pluto's rotation and Charon's orbit.



Charon gradually migrated outward while Pluto's rotation slowed.



Eventually, the rotation periods of both bodies became equal to Charon's orbital period.



The result is the current mutually synchronous configuration.



This process also generated heat inside the bodies. Early tidal heating may have contributed to Charon's internal evolution and possibly helped maintain liquid water.



The present configuration is dynamically stable, with the same hemispheres of Pluto and Charon approximately facing each other continuously.



The system provides an important natural example of how \[\[Tides]] can dramatically transform both planetary rotation and satellite orbits.



\## Pluto's Smaller Moons



In addition to Charon, Pluto has four known small moons:



Styx, Nix, Kerberos, and Hydra.



These moons orbit outside Charon.



They are much smaller and have irregular shapes.



Their orbits are close to a chain of orbital period relationships associated with Charon's motion, making the system dynamically interesting.



The smaller moons rotate in complex ways rather than simply maintaining one face toward Pluto.



Their unusual rotational states are influenced by the changing gravitational field of the Pluto-Charon binary.



The existence of several small outer moons supports scenarios in which substantial debris remained after the giant impact that formed Charon.



\## Exploration by New Horizons



Before 2015, Charon appeared only as a small unresolved or barely resolved object even in the best telescopic observations.



NASA's \[\[New Horizons]] mission completely transformed knowledge of the moon.



The spacecraft launched in 2006 and flew through the Pluto system in July 2015.



Its cameras revealed Charon as a complex geological world containing enormous tectonic fractures, mountains, plains, craters, and the reddish north polar region.



Spectrometers mapped water ice, ammonia-bearing compounds, and other surface materials.



New Horizons also improved measurements of Charon's dimensions, surface properties, and geological history.



The flyby demonstrated that relatively small icy bodies far from the Sun can experience extensive geological evolution.



Because New Horizons passed through the system at high speed, it could observe only one encounter rather than enter orbit. Much of Charon's surface was imaged, but some regions remain poorly characterized.



A future Pluto-system orbiter could study seasonal changes, map Charon at much higher resolution, measure its gravity field, and investigate its interior.



\## Charon Compared With Other Icy Moons



Charon shares several characteristics with other icy satellites but occupies a very different environment.



\[\[Europa]] has a global subsurface ocean maintained largely by tidal heating from Jupiter and orbital resonance with neighboring moons. Charon no longer experiences comparable continuous tidal heating.



\[\[Enceladus]] possesses active south-polar plumes that directly release subsurface material into space. No present-day plume activity has been detected on Charon.



\[\[Ganymede]] is much larger and has an internally generated magnetic field. Charon is smaller and has no known intrinsic global magnetic field.



\[\[Triton]] has a nitrogen-rich surface, tenuous atmosphere, and active or recently active volatile geology. Charon's surface is dominated much more strongly by water ice and lacks a significant atmosphere.



Charon's importance comes from showing that even a relatively small icy body can experience differentiation, tectonic expansion, possible ocean freezing, and extensive resurfacing without the strong present-day tidal heating found in some giant-planet satellite systems.



\## Scientific Importance



Charon provides a rare opportunity to study a satellite almost comparable in scale to its primary.



The Pluto-Charon mass ratio and external barycenter make the system an extreme example of binary-like orbital dynamics.



Its likely giant-impact origin provides information about collisions among large icy bodies during \[\[Solar System Formation]].



Its mutually synchronous orbit demonstrates the long-term consequences of tidal angular-momentum exchange.



Its giant tectonic fractures may record the freezing of an ancient internal ocean, while Vulcan Planitia may preserve evidence of large-scale cryovolcanic resurfacing.



The reddish north pole reveals chemical and physical exchange between Pluto and Charon, demonstrating that planetary bodies can modify one another's surfaces even without direct contact.



Finally, Charon's impact craters provide an indirect record of the otherwise difficult-to-detect population of small trans-Neptunian objects.



\## Misconceptions and Open Questions



Charon is not simply a small asteroid captured by Pluto. Its unusually large mass relative to Pluto and the properties of the system strongly favor formation through a major ancient collision.



Charon is not officially classified as a dwarf planet. Although the Pluto-Charon barycenter lies outside Pluto and the pair is sometimes informally called a binary dwarf planet, Charon remains formally classified as Pluto's moon.



The reddish north pole does not mean Charon's entire surface has Pluto's composition. Most of Charon is dominated by water ice, while the polar material is thought to include radiation-processed compounds partly derived from methane escaping Pluto.



Charon is not known to have a present global ocean. Geological evidence is compatible with a past subsurface ocean, but whether any liquid survives today remains uncertain.



The surface is also not geologically uniform. Different crater densities, tectonic terrains, and smooth plains show that multiple geological processes operated over time.



Major open questions include the exact nature of Charon's interior, whether any subsurface liquid remains, how long its ancient ocean survived, and precisely how Vulcan Planitia formed.



Scientists also continue investigating the details of the giant impact that produced the Pluto-Charon system, how the smaller moons formed, and how quickly tidal evolution moved Charon to its present orbit.



A future orbiter or lander could determine Charon's gravity field, internal structure, detailed composition, heat flow, geological chronology, and possible remaining internal activity.



\## Summary



Charon is the largest moon of \[\[Pluto]] and has a diameter of approximately 1,212 kilometers, slightly more than half Pluto's diameter.



Its unusually large size relative to Pluto creates a binary-like system in which both bodies orbit a barycenter located outside Pluto.



Pluto and Charon are mutually tidally locked and complete their rotations and mutual orbit in about 6.4 Earth days.



Charon's surface is dominated by water ice and contains a reddish north polar region thought to form partly from methane escaping Pluto and being chemically processed by radiation.



The moon has enormous tectonic fractures, mountains, impact craters, and the smooth plains of Vulcan Planitia. These features suggest extensive ancient geological activity and possible \[\[Cryovolcanism]].



One leading model proposes that Charon once possessed a subsurface ocean. As the interior cooled and water froze, expansion may have fractured the crust on a global scale.



Charon probably formed through a giant impact involving the young Pluto and another large icy body. Debris from this event may also have produced Pluto's smaller moons.



The \[\[New Horizons]] flyby in 2015 revealed this complex geology and transformed Charon from a distant point of light into one of the best-studied large worlds of the \[\[Kuiper Belt]].



Charon therefore provides important evidence about giant impacts, tidal evolution, icy-world geology, internal oceans, \[\[Planetary Tectonics]], and the formation of satellite systems in the outer Solar System.
