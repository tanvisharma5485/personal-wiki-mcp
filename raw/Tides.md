\# Tides



\## Overview



Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level caused mainly by the gravitational interaction between Earth, the \[\[Moon]], and the \[\[Sun]]. The Moon is the dominant influence because it is much closer to Earth, even though the Sun is far more massive.



Tides are not simply caused by the Moon pulling water upward at one location. They result from differences in gravitational force across Earth together with the motion of the Earth-Moon system around their common center of mass.



Tidal effects are important not only for Earth's oceans but also for the long-term evolution of planetary rotations, satellite orbits, internal heating, and orbital dynamics throughout the Solar System.



\## Gravitational Cause of Tides



The Moon's gravitational pull is slightly stronger on the side of Earth facing the Moon and slightly weaker on the side facing away from it.



This difference in gravitational attraction across Earth creates what is called a:



Tidal force



The near side experiences a stronger lunar pull, while the far side experiences a weaker pull relative to Earth's center.



This differential force helps produce two broad tidal bulges on opposite sides of Earth.



\## Why There Are Two Tidal Bulges



A common misconception is that the Moon produces only one bulge on the side facing it.



In reality, the Earth-Moon system rotates around a shared center of mass called the:



Barycenter



Because different parts of Earth experience different lunar gravitational accelerations, one bulge forms roughly toward the Moon and another roughly on the opposite side.



The actual ocean response is much more complex because continents, coastlines, ocean depth, Earth's rotation, and basin geometry strongly modify the idealized pattern.



\## Earth-Moon Barycenter



Earth and the Moon both orbit their common center of mass.



The barycenter lies inside Earth because Earth is much more massive than the Moon, but it does not lie exactly at Earth's center.



This shared orbital motion is important for understanding the geometry of tidal forces.



\## Solar Tides



The Sun also produces tides on Earth.



Because the Sun is extremely massive but very distant, its tidal effect is weaker than the Moon's tidal effect.



Solar tides modify lunar tides depending on the relative positions of the Sun, Earth, and Moon.



\## Spring Tides



Spring tides occur when the gravitational effects of the Sun and Moon reinforce each other.



This happens around:



New Moon



and:



Full Moon



During spring tides, the difference between high tide and low tide is generally larger than average.



The term "spring tide" does not refer to the season of spring. It comes from the idea of the water "springing" or rising more strongly.



\## Neap Tides



Neap tides occur when the Sun and Moon are approximately at right angles as seen from Earth.



This happens around:



First quarter Moon



and:



Third quarter Moon



Their tidal effects partially oppose each other, producing a smaller tidal range.



\## Tidal Range



Tidal range is the difference in water level between:



High tide



and:



Low tide



The range varies greatly from place to place.



Some coastlines experience only small tidal changes, while others experience changes of many meters.



\## High Tide



High tide is the period when sea level reaches a local maximum during the tidal cycle.



\## Low Tide



Low tide is the period when sea level reaches a local minimum.



\## Semidiurnal Tides



Many coastlines experience:



Two high tides and two low tides each lunar day.



This pattern is called:



Semidiurnal tide



The two high tides are often not exactly equal because the Moon's orbit and Earth's geometry are complex.



\## Diurnal Tides



Some locations experience approximately:



One high tide and one low tide each lunar day.



This is called:



Diurnal tide



\## Mixed Tides



Many regions experience two high tides and two low tides of significantly different heights.



This is called:



Mixed tide



or:



Mixed semidiurnal tide



\## Lunar Day



A lunar day is the time required for a location on Earth to rotate back to approximately the same position relative to the Moon.



It lasts about:



24 hours 50 minutes



This is longer than a normal solar day because the Moon moves eastward in its orbit while Earth rotates.



\## Why Tides Occur Later Each Day



Because the lunar day is about 50 minutes longer than the 24-hour solar day, corresponding tidal conditions often occur roughly:



50 minutes later each day



However, local geography can cause substantial deviations.



\## Tidal Period



For a typical semidiurnal tide, the time between successive high tides is approximately:



12 hours 25 minutes



Again, real local tides can vary from this idealized value.



\## Role of Earth's Rotation



Earth's rotation carries coastlines through the large-scale tidal pattern.



However, the actual oceans do not behave as simple stationary bulges directly beneath and opposite the Moon.



Ocean basins respond dynamically to tidal forcing.



\## Continents and Ocean Basins



Continents block the free movement of ocean water.



The size and shape of ocean basins strongly influence tidal behavior.



This is why local tides can differ dramatically from the simple two-bulge model.



\## Shallow Water Effects



As tidal waves move into shallow coastal water, they can:



Slow down



Increase in height



Change shape



Interact strongly with the coastline



This can produce unusually large tides in some regions.



\## Resonance



Ocean basins can have natural oscillation periods.



If the tidal forcing is close to one of these natural periods, resonance can amplify tides.



This is one reason certain bays experience exceptionally large tidal ranges.



\## Bay of Fundy



The Bay of Fundy in Canada is famous for having some of the world's largest tidal ranges.



Its shape and natural resonance strongly amplify tidal motion.



\## Amphidromic Systems



In large ocean basins, tidal waves often rotate around points called:



Amphidromic points



At an ideal amphidromic point, tidal amplitude is very small.



Around the point, the time of high tide changes progressively.



\## Cotidal Lines



Lines connecting locations experiencing high tide at approximately the same time are called:



Cotidal lines



They help map how tidal waves propagate around ocean basins.



\## Coriolis Effect



Earth's rotation causes moving ocean water to be deflected by the:



Coriolis effect



This contributes to the rotating tidal patterns observed in many ocean basins.



\## Tidal Currents



Tides do not only change water level.



They also generate horizontal water motion called:



Tidal currents



These currents can be very strong in:



Straits



Channels



Estuaries



Narrow coastal passages



\## Flood Current



A tidal current moving generally toward the coast or upstream is called:



Flood current



\## Ebb Current



A tidal current moving generally away from the coast or downstream is called:



Ebb current



\## Slack Water



Between flood and ebb currents, the water may temporarily become relatively calm.



This period is called:



Slack water



It does not necessarily occur at exactly the same time as high or low tide.



\## Tidal Bores



A tidal bore is a wave that travels upstream when a strong incoming tide enters a shallow, narrowing river or estuary.



Tidal bores occur only where local geography and tidal range are favorable.



They can travel significant distances inland.



\## Estuaries



Tides strongly affect estuaries where freshwater rivers meet seawater.



They influence:



Salinity



Sediment transport



Nutrient movement



Wetlands



Marine ecosystems



Navigation



\## Intertidal Zone



The intertidal zone is the coastal region exposed during low tide and submerged during high tide.



It is one of the most environmentally variable marine habitats.



Organisms living there must tolerate changes in:



Water coverage



Temperature



Salinity



Wave exposure



Oxygen availability



\## Tidal Ecosystems



Tides influence many ecosystems, including:



Mangroves



Salt marshes



Mudflats



Rocky shores



Estuaries



Coral environments



Tidal cycles control feeding, reproduction, nutrient exchange, and habitat availability for many organisms.



\## Perigean Tides



The Moon's orbit around Earth is elliptical.



When the Moon is near its closest point to Earth, called:



Perigee



its tidal effect is somewhat stronger.



If perigee occurs near a new or full Moon, especially large spring tides can occur.



These are sometimes called:



Perigean spring tides



\## Apogean Tides



When the Moon is near:



Apogee



its farthest point from Earth, lunar tidal forcing is slightly weaker.



\## Supermoon and Tides



A full or new Moon occurring near perigee is sometimes popularly called a:



Supermoon



It can contribute to somewhat higher-than-average tides, especially if combined with favorable weather conditions.



It does not automatically cause catastrophic flooding.



\## Atmospheric Pressure and Tides



Local sea level is also influenced by atmospheric pressure.



Low atmospheric pressure allows sea level to rise slightly, while high pressure can suppress it.



This effect is separate from astronomical tides.



\## Wind and Tides



Strong winds can push water toward or away from coastlines.



Therefore observed sea level can differ significantly from predicted astronomical tides.



\## Storm Surge



Storm surge is an abnormal rise in sea level caused mainly by:



Strong winds



Low atmospheric pressure



Storm circulation



It is not itself an astronomical tide.



However, a storm surge arriving near high tide can produce much more severe coastal flooding.



\## Astronomical Tide Versus Storm Surge



Astronomical tide is predictable from the gravitational motions of the Earth, Moon, and Sun.



Storm surge is caused mainly by weather.



Observed coastal water level can be approximated as a combination of:



Astronomical tide



plus:



Meteorological effects



\## Tide Prediction



Tides can be predicted very accurately at many locations because the motions of Earth, Moon, and Sun are highly predictable.



However, real-time sea level also depends on:



Weather



Waves



Atmospheric pressure



River flow



Ocean currents



\## Harmonic Analysis



Tide prediction often uses:



Harmonic analysis



The observed tide is represented as the combination of many periodic components called:



Tidal constituents



\## Tidal Constituents



Different tidal constituents correspond to different astronomical cycles.



Important constituents arise from:



Lunar motion



Solar motion



Earth's rotation



Orbital eccentricity



Orbital inclination



\## M2 Constituent



The largest tidal constituent in many locations is:



M2



It is the principal lunar semidiurnal constituent.



Its period is approximately:



12.42 hours



\## S2 Constituent



S2 is the principal solar semidiurnal tidal constituent.



Its period is exactly:



12 solar hours



The interaction between M2 and S2 helps produce the spring-neap cycle.



\## Spring-Neap Cycle



Because lunar and solar semidiurnal tides have slightly different periods, their relative phase changes over time.



This produces a spring-neap cycle of approximately:



14.8 days



\## K1 and O1



Important diurnal constituents include:



K1



and:



O1



These contribute strongly to one-cycle-per-day tidal variations.



\## Lunar Declination



The Moon moves north and south of Earth's equatorial plane during its orbit.



This is called:



Lunar declination



It contributes to unequal high tides and diurnal tidal patterns.



\## Declination Effects



When lunar declination is large, one daily tidal bulge can affect a location more strongly than the other.



This can increase:



Diurnal inequality



\## Diurnal Inequality



Diurnal inequality refers to differences between successive high tides or successive low tides.



It is especially noticeable in mixed-tide regions.



\## Tidal Datum



A tidal datum is a reference sea level derived from long-term tidal observations.



Examples are used for:



Navigation charts



Coastal engineering



Land elevation



Flood planning



\## Mean Sea Level



Mean sea level is an average sea-surface height measured over a sufficiently long interval.



It differs from instantaneous tidal level.



\## Mean Higher High Water



Mean Higher High Water is the average height of the higher of the two daily high tides at locations with mixed or semidiurnal tides.



It is commonly used in coastal mapping and regulation.



\## Tides and Navigation



Tide predictions are essential for:



Ships



Harbors



Ports



Fishing vessels



Coastal construction



A vessel may require high tide to safely pass through shallow water.



\## Tides and Sediment Transport



Tidal currents move large amounts of:



Sand



Mud



Silt



Organic material



This can reshape coastlines and estuaries over time.



\## Tidal Flats



Tidal flats are broad regions of sediment alternately exposed and submerged by tides.



They provide important habitats for:



Birds



Shellfish



Fish



Microorganisms



\## Tidal Energy



Moving tidal water contains energy that can be used to generate electricity.



Two major approaches are:



Tidal range systems



and:



Tidal stream systems



\## Tidal Barrage



A tidal barrage is a dam-like structure built across an estuary or bay.



Water moving through turbines during rising or falling tides generates electricity.



\## Tidal Stream Turbine



Tidal stream systems use underwater turbines placed in strong tidal currents.



They work somewhat like underwater wind turbines.



\## Advantages of Tidal Energy



Tidal energy is highly predictable because astronomical tidal cycles can be calculated far in advance.



It can provide renewable electricity in suitable locations.



\## Challenges of Tidal Energy



Challenges include:



High construction costs



Limited suitable sites



Marine ecosystem impacts



Sediment changes



Maintenance in seawater



Effects on navigation



\## Tidal Friction



Ocean tides interact with:



Seafloor



Coastlines



Shallow water



This dissipates energy as heat.



The process is called:



Tidal friction



\## Earth Rotation and Tidal Friction



Because Earth rotates faster than the Moon orbits Earth, Earth's tidal bulges are carried slightly ahead of the Earth-Moon line.



The Moon gravitationally pulls on these displaced bulges.



This creates a torque that gradually:



Slows Earth's rotation



and:



Transfers angular momentum to the Moon's orbit



\## Lengthening of Earth's Day



Tidal friction causes Earth's rotation to slow gradually.



As a result, the length of Earth's day increases over geological time.



The rate is not perfectly constant because ocean basin geometry and other factors change.



\## Lunar Recession



The Moon is gradually moving farther from Earth.



Laser ranging measurements show that its average distance increases by approximately:



3.8 centimeters per year



at present.



\## Conservation of Angular Momentum



The slowing of Earth's rotation and outward movement of the Moon are related through:



Conservation of angular momentum



Angular momentum is transferred from Earth's rotation to the Moon's orbit.



\## Lunar Laser Ranging



Apollo astronauts and Soviet robotic missions placed retroreflectors on the lunar surface.



Scientists fire laser pulses from Earth toward these reflectors.



The round-trip travel time allows extremely precise measurement of the Earth-Moon distance.



\## Long-Term Earth-Moon Evolution



In the distant past, the Moon was closer to Earth.



Tidal forces were therefore stronger.



Earth also rotated faster, meaning days were shorter.



Over billions of years, tidal friction has changed both:



Earth's rotation



and:



Moon's orbital distance



\## Ancient Day Length



Geological records indicate that Earth once had shorter days.



Some ancient sedimentary and biological records preserve tidal and daily cycles that help reconstruct past rotation rates.



\## Tidal Rhythmites



Sedimentary layers formed by repeated tidal currents are called:



Tidal rhythmites



Some ancient rhythmites preserve information about:



Past tides



Earth's rotation rate



Earth-Moon orbital evolution



\## Tidal Locking



Tidal forces can gradually change the rotation of astronomical bodies.



One important result is:



Tidal locking



The Moon is tidally locked to Earth.



\## Why the Moon Shows One Side



The Moon rotates once on its axis in approximately the same time that it completes one orbit around Earth.



This is called:



Synchronous rotation



Therefore approximately the same lunar hemisphere faces Earth at all times.



\## Tidal Locking Process



Early in lunar history, the Moon probably rotated faster.



Earth's tidal forces created internal deformation and friction inside the Moon.



This dissipated rotational energy.



Over time, the Moon's rotation slowed until it became synchronized with its orbit.



\## Near Side and Far Side



The hemisphere generally facing Earth is called:



Near side



The hemisphere generally facing away is called:



Far side



The far side is not permanently dark.



Both sides experience sunlight.



\## Libration



Although the Moon is tidally locked, observers on Earth can see slightly more than half of its surface over time.



This apparent rocking motion is called:



Libration



It results from the Moon's:



Orbital eccentricity



Orbital inclination



Observer position on Earth



\## Tidal Heating



Tidal forces can deform the interiors of moons and planets.



Repeated deformation causes internal friction and generates heat.



This is called:



Tidal heating



\## Io



Jupiter's moon \[\[Io]] is the most dramatic example of tidal heating in the Solar System.



Io's slightly eccentric orbit causes Jupiter's enormous gravity to repeatedly flex its interior.



This generates enough heat to power intense volcanic activity.



\## Europa



\[\[Europa]] also experiences tidal heating.



This may help maintain a global subsurface ocean beneath its icy crust.



\## Enceladus



\[\[Enceladus]] experiences tidal heating from Saturn.



Internal heat helps maintain liquid water beneath its icy surface and powers its famous south-polar plumes.



\## Tidal Resonance



Orbital resonances can maintain orbital eccentricity and therefore sustain tidal heating.



For example, Io, Europa, and Ganymede participate in the:



Laplace resonance



\## Laplace Resonance



For approximately every one orbit completed by Ganymede:



Europa completes about two



and:



Io completes about four



This gravitational relationship helps maintain orbital eccentricities and ongoing tidal heating.



\## Tidal Heating and Habitability



Tidal heating can provide energy even far from a star.



This is important for icy moons such as:



Europa



Enceladus



Potential liquid-water environments can therefore exist beyond the traditional stellar habitable zone.



\## Excessive Tidal Heating



Too much tidal heating can make an environment extremely hostile.



Io demonstrates this, with intense volcanism and rapid geological resurfacing.



Thus tidal heating can either support or reduce habitability depending on its strength.



\## Tides on the Moon



Earth also raises tides on the Moon.



Because the Moon is mostly solid, these are:



Solid-body tides



rather than large ocean tides.



The Moon's shape flexes slightly as gravitational forces vary.



\## Solid Earth Tides



The Moon and Sun also deform solid Earth.



Earth's crust rises and falls slightly due to gravitational tides.



These are called:



Earth tides



or:



Solid Earth tides



The deformation can reach several tens of centimeters in some components.



\## Atmospheric Tides



Earth's atmosphere also experiences periodic variations called:



Atmospheric tides



These are driven by a combination of:



Solar heating



Gravity



Atmospheric dynamics



They are distinct from ordinary ocean tides.



\## Solar Atmospheric Tides



Daily heating by the Sun creates strong atmospheric pressure oscillations, especially in the tropics.



These are examples of thermally driven atmospheric tides.



\## Tides on Other Planets



Tidal forces operate throughout the Solar System.



They influence:



Planetary rotation



Moon orbits



Ring systems



Interior heating



Orbital evolution



\## Mercury



The Sun raises strong tides on \[\[Mercury]].



Mercury is not synchronously locked. Instead, it is trapped in a:



3:2 spin-orbit resonance



It rotates three times for every two orbits around the Sun.



\## Venus



Solar tidal effects influence \[\[Venus]], but its unusual slow retrograde rotation involves a more complicated evolutionary history including atmospheric and gravitational torques.



\## Mars



The moons \[\[Phobos]] and \[\[Deimos]] experience tidal interactions with Mars.



Phobos orbits faster than Mars rotates beneath it.



As a result, tidal interaction causes Phobos to slowly spiral inward.



\## Phobos



\[\[Phobos]] is gradually approaching Mars.



In the distant future, it may either:



Break apart



or:



Impact Mars



depending on how its orbit and structure evolve.



\## Deimos



\[\[Deimos]] orbits beyond Mars's synchronous orbital distance.



It is therefore slowly moving outward rather than inward.



\## Jupiter



Tidal interactions are especially strong in the Jovian moon system.



They influence the orbits and interiors of:



Io



Europa



Ganymede



and other moons.



\## Saturn



Saturn's moons also experience strong tidal evolution.



\[\[Enceladus]] is heated significantly by tidal flexing.



Tidal interactions also contribute to the orbital migration of several Saturnian moons.



\## Titan



\[\[Titan]] raises tides on Saturn and also experiences Saturn's tidal forces.



Its orbital evolution provides information about how tidal dissipation occurs inside Saturn.



\## Rings and Tides



Planetary tidal forces determine whether material can remain gravitationally bound into a moon close to a planet.



This is related to the:



Roche limit



\## Roche Limit



Inside the Roche limit, tidal forces from a planet can exceed the self-gravity holding a weak satellite together.



Material may then break apart or be unable to accrete into a large moon.



This concept is important for understanding:



Planetary rings



Moon disruption



Close satellite orbits



\## Rigid and Fluid Roche Limits



The exact Roche limit depends on the properties of the orbiting body.



A fluid body generally disrupts farther away than a strong rigid body.



\## Tidal Disruption



If an object passes sufficiently close to a massive planet or star, differential gravity can tear it apart.



This is called:



Tidal disruption



\## Shoemaker-Levy 9



Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 was broken into fragments by Jupiter's tidal forces before the fragments collided with Jupiter in 1994.



This provided a dramatic example of tidal disruption.

\## Exoplanets

\## Tidally Locked Exoplanets

\## Red Dwarf Planets

\## Climate of Tidally Locked Planets

\## Tidal Heating in Exoplanets

\## Tides and Plate Tectonics



Tidal stresses act on Earth's crust, but they are much smaller than the forces driving plate tectonics.



Tides do not drive Earth's plate tectonic system.



\## Tides and Earthquakes



Tidal forces slightly change stresses in Earth's crust.



Research has examined whether they can influence the exact timing of some earthquakes.



However, tides do not provide a reliable method for predicting earthquakes and are not the primary cause of tectonic earthquakes.



\## Tides and Volcanoes



Tidal stresses may slightly modulate some volcanic or hydrothermal systems under particular conditions.



They are not the fundamental energy source for Earth's volcanism.

\## Common Misconceptions



\### Tides Are Caused Only by the Moon



Incorrect. The Moon has the largest tidal influence on Earth, but the Sun also contributes significantly.



\### There Is Only One High Tide Facing the Moon



Incorrect. The idealized tidal pattern contains two broad bulges, although real oceans respond much more complexly.



\### High Tide Always Occurs When the Moon Is Directly Overhead



Incorrect. Local ocean dynamics can shift high tide by many hours relative to the Moon's position.



\### Spring Tides Happen Only in Spring



Incorrect. Spring tides occur near every new and full Moon throughout the year.



\### A Full Moon Always Causes Coastal Flooding



Incorrect. Full Moon spring tides can raise tidal range, but flooding depends strongly on local geography, weather, winds, and sea level.



\### Tides Cause Tsunamis



Incorrect. Tsunamis are usually caused by earthquakes, landslides, volcanic events, or impacts. Tides and tsunamis are different phenomena.



\### The Moon Is Not Moving Away From Earth



Incorrect. Lunar laser ranging shows the Moon is currently receding by about 3.8 centimeters per year.



\### Tidal Locking Means the Moon Does Not Rotate



Incorrect. The Moon rotates once during each orbit. This synchronous rotation is why nearly the same hemisphere faces Earth.



\### Tidal Heating Requires Liquid Water



Incorrect. Tidal heating comes from internal deformation and friction and can occur in solid rock or ice.



\## Important Terminology



\*\*Tidal Force:\*\* Difference in gravitational acceleration across an extended object.



\*\*Tidal Range:\*\* Difference between local high and low tide.



\*\*Spring Tide:\*\* Enhanced tidal range occurring near new and full Moon.



\*\*Neap Tide:\*\* Reduced tidal range occurring near first and third quarter Moon.



\*\*Semidiurnal Tide:\*\* Two high and two low tides per lunar day.



\*\*Diurnal Tide:\*\* Approximately one high and one low tide per lunar day.



\*\*Mixed Tide:\*\* Two unequal high and low tides during a lunar day.



\*\*Tidal Current:\*\* Horizontal movement of water associated with tides.



\*\*Tidal Friction:\*\* Energy loss caused by tidal motion and deformation.



\*\*Tidal Locking:\*\* Rotational evolution leading to a stable relationship between rotation and orbital motion.



\*\*Tidal Heating:\*\* Internal heat generated by repeated gravitational deformation.



\*\*Roche Limit:\*\* Distance within which tidal forces can disrupt a gravitationally bound satellite under appropriate conditions.



\*\*Barycenter:\*\* Common center of mass around which two bodies orbit.



\## Simplified Ocean Tide Process



The Moon's gravity varies across Earth. Differential gravitational forces produce tidal forcing. Earth's rotation and ocean dynamics then cause water to move through complex basin-scale tidal patterns. Continents, ocean depth, resonance, and the Coriolis effect determine the actual timing and height of local tides.



\## Simplified Spring and Neap Cycle



At new Moon, the Sun, Moon, and Earth are approximately aligned, so solar and lunar tidal effects reinforce each other and produce spring tides.



Around first quarter, the Sun and Moon are approximately at right angles, so their tidal effects partially cancel and produce neap tides.



At full Moon they align again and another spring tide occurs. Around third quarter another neap tide occurs.



This cycle repeats approximately every two weeks.



\## Simplified Earth-Moon Tidal Evolution



Earth rotates faster than the Moon orbits. Tidal friction shifts Earth's tidal bulges slightly ahead of the Moon. The Moon pulls backward on those bulges, slowing Earth's rotation. At the same time, angular momentum is transferred into the Moon's orbit, causing the Moon to gradually move farther away.



\## Scientific Importance



Tides connect \[\[Orbital Dynamics]], planetary interiors, oceanography, geology, and habitability.



On Earth they influence coastal environments and slowly alter the Earth-Moon system. Elsewhere, tides can generate enormous internal heat, maintain subsurface oceans, synchronize planetary rotation, circularize orbits, and even destroy objects that pass too close to larger bodies.



\## Open Questions



Scientists continue studying how tidal dissipation varies inside Earth, giant planets, moons, and exoplanets. The detailed interiors of Jupiter and Saturn strongly influence the migration rates of their moons, while measurements of lunar recession provide information about the past evolution of Earth's oceans and rotation.



Other important questions include how tidal heating influences the long-term habitability of Europa and Enceladus, how common tidally locked habitable exoplanets may be, how efficiently exoplanet atmospheres redistribute heat, and how orbital resonances maintain tidal heating over billions of years.



\## Summary



Tides are produced by differential gravitational forces, primarily from the Moon and secondarily from the Sun. Earth's oceans respond to these forces in complex ways controlled by rotation, continents, basin geometry, water depth, and resonance.



The interaction of solar and lunar tides produces spring and neap tides. Local regions can experience semidiurnal, diurnal, or mixed tidal patterns.



Tidal friction gradually slows Earth's rotation and transfers angular momentum to the Moon's orbit, causing the Moon to recede from Earth by roughly 3.8 centimeters per year at present. The same general physics causes tidal locking, orbital circularization, and tidal heating throughout planetary systems.



Tidal heating is especially important for \[\[Io]], \[\[Europa]], and \[\[Enceladus]], where gravitational flexing drives geological activity and can help maintain subsurface liquid water.



Tides therefore play a major role not only in Earth's oceans but also in the long-term geological and orbital evolution of planets, moons, and exoplanets.
