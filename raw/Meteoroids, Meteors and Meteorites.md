\# Meteoroids, Meteors and Meteorites



\## Overview



Meteoroids, meteors, and meteorites describe different stages or contexts of small natural solid objects associated with space and their interaction with planetary atmospheres and surfaces.



The terms are related but are not interchangeable.



A meteoroid is a natural solid object traveling through space that is generally smaller than an asteroid.



A meteor is the visible phenomenon produced when a meteoroid enters an atmosphere at high speed and heats the surrounding gas and itself.



A meteorite is material from a meteoroid that survives atmospheric passage and reaches the surface of a planet or moon.



The basic sequence for an object entering Earth's atmosphere is:



Meteoroid in space → atmospheric entry → meteor phenomenon → surviving material becomes a meteorite



If the object completely disintegrates or vaporizes before reaching the ground, no recoverable meteorite may remain.



\## Basic Definitions



\### Meteoroid



A meteoroid is a relatively small natural solid body moving through interplanetary space.



Meteoroids can consist of:



\- Rock

\- Metal

\- Mixtures of rock and metal

\- Fragile cometary material



They may originate from:



\- Asteroids

\- Comets

\- The Moon

\- Mars

\- Collisions between Solar System bodies



\### Meteor



A meteor is the luminous phenomenon associated with a meteoroid entering an atmosphere at high velocity.



The bright streak is commonly called a:



Shooting star



or:



Falling star



A meteor is not actually a star.



\### Meteorite



A meteorite is natural extraterrestrial material that survives passage through an atmosphere and reaches the surface.



Meteorites provide physical samples of material from other parts of the Solar System.



\## Meteoroid Versus Meteor Versus Meteorite



\### Meteoroid



Location:



Space



Meaning:



Small natural solid body traveling through space.



\### Meteor



Location:



Atmosphere



Meaning:



Visible atmospheric phenomenon produced during high-speed entry.



\### Meteorite



Location:



Surface



Meaning:



Extraterrestrial material that survives atmospheric entry and reaches the ground.



\## Simple Example



Suppose a small rocky fragment is traveling through interplanetary space.



While it remains in space, it is a:



Meteoroid



When it enters Earth's atmosphere and produces a bright streak, the visible phenomenon is a:



Meteor



If part of the object survives and lands on Earth, that surviving material is a:



Meteorite



\## Origin of Meteoroids



Meteoroids originate from several sources.



Important sources include:



\- Asteroid collisions

\- Cometary debris

\- Planetary impacts

\- Lunar impacts

\- Martian impacts

\- Fragmentation of larger bodies



\## Asteroid-Derived Meteoroids



Collisions between asteroids can break larger bodies into smaller fragments.



Some fragments eventually enter Earth-crossing orbits.



If one reaches Earth's atmosphere, it may produce a meteor and potentially a meteorite.



Many meteorites recovered on Earth originated from asteroids.



\## Comet-Derived Meteoroids



Comets release dust and small particles as they approach the Sun.



Solar heating causes volatile ices to sublimate.



Escaping gas carries dust and particles away from the nucleus.



These particles can spread along the comet's orbit.



When Earth passes through such a debris stream, a meteor shower can occur.



\## Lunar Meteoroids and Meteorites



Large impacts on the Moon can eject lunar rocks into space.



Some fragments escape the Moon's gravity.



A small fraction eventually reach Earth.



Meteorites confirmed to have originated from the Moon are called:



Lunar meteorites



\## Martian Meteorites



Large impacts on Mars can eject rocks at velocities high enough to escape Martian gravity.



Some of these fragments eventually reach Earth.



These are called:



Martian meteorites



Scientists identify them using:



\- Mineral composition

\- Isotopic composition

\- Trapped gases

\- Geological characteristics



Some Martian meteorites contain trapped gases resembling the Martian atmosphere measured by spacecraft.



\## Interplanetary Dust



The Solar System also contains extremely small particles called interplanetary dust.



Sources include:



\- Comets

\- Asteroid collisions

\- Collisional debris



Some of this material enters Earth's atmosphere continuously.



\## Size



There is no perfectly simple boundary separating all terms used for small Solar System bodies.



Meteoroids are generally smaller than objects normally described as asteroids.



They can range from tiny grains to objects approximately meter-scale in size.



Terminology may vary depending on scientific context.



\## Micrometeoroids



Very small meteoroids are often called micrometeoroids.



They can pose risks to:



\- Spacecraft

\- Satellites

\- Space stations

\- Astronauts



Despite their tiny size, their extremely high relative velocities allow them to carry significant kinetic energy.



\## Micrometeorites



Tiny extraterrestrial particles that survive atmospheric entry and reach Earth's surface can be called micrometeorites.



Large quantities of extraterrestrial dust reach Earth over time.



\## Atmospheric Entry



When a meteoroid enters Earth's atmosphere, it can be traveling at extremely high velocity.



Earth-entry velocities are typically at least approximately:



11 km/s



and can exceed:



70 km/s



depending on the object's orbit and encounter geometry.



\## Why Meteors Glow



A common misconception is that meteors glow simply because of friction with air.



The actual process is more complex.



At extremely high entry speeds, the meteoroid strongly compresses the air in front of it.



The gas becomes extremely hot.



Atmospheric molecules become excited and ionized.



Material from the meteoroid is also heated, vaporized, and removed.



The combination produces the luminous meteor phenomenon.



\## Atmospheric Compression



The atmosphere cannot move out of the way quickly enough when an object travels at many kilometers per second.



Air in front of the meteoroid becomes compressed.



Compression dramatically increases temperature.



This contributes to:



\- Heating

\- Ionization

\- Ablation

\- Light production



\## Ablation



Ablation is the removal of material from a meteoroid during atmospheric entry.



Material may be removed through:



\- Melting

\- Vaporization

\- Mechanical erosion

\- Fragmentation



A meteoroid can lose a large fraction or all of its original mass through ablation.



\## Ionization



High-energy collisions during atmospheric entry can ionize atmospheric gases and vaporized meteoroid material.



Ionization contributes to the glowing trail.



Some meteor trails can also produce detectable radar echoes.



\## Meteor Altitude



Many visible meteors begin glowing at altitudes around:



70–120 km



although the exact altitude varies depending on:



\- Entry speed

\- Size

\- Composition

\- Entry angle

\- Atmospheric density



Small particles often disappear high in the atmosphere.



Larger bodies can penetrate much deeper.



\## Meteor Color



Meteors can appear in different colors.



Color can depend on:



\- Chemical composition

\- Velocity

\- Atmospheric gases

\- Temperature

\- Human visual perception

\- Camera response



Possible colors include:



\- White

\- Yellow

\- Orange

\- Red

\- Green

\- Blue



\## Chemical Elements and Meteor Colors



Certain atomic emissions can contribute characteristic wavelengths.



Elements associated with meteor spectra may include:



\- Sodium

\- Magnesium

\- Iron

\- Calcium



Atmospheric oxygen and nitrogen also contribute to meteor emission.



Color alone is not always a reliable way to determine composition.



\## Shooting Star



A shooting star is simply a common name for a meteor.



It is not a star.



Stars are enormous astronomical objects located vastly farther away.



\## Falling Star



Falling star is another informal term for a meteor.



Stars do not fall through Earth's atmosphere.



\## Fireball



A fireball is an unusually bright meteor.



Fireballs can be bright enough to attract widespread attention.



Some can be visible during daylight.



\## Bolide



The term bolide is commonly used for an exceptionally bright meteor, often one that explodes or fragments dramatically in the atmosphere.



Definitions vary between organizations and scientific contexts.



A bolide may produce:



\- Intense light

\- Fragmentation

\- Sonic booms

\- Atmospheric shock waves



\## Superbolide



An exceptionally energetic and luminous bolide may be described as a superbolide.



Large atmospheric impact events such as the 2013 Chelyabinsk event are often described using this term.



\## Meteor Fragmentation



Meteoroids can break apart during atmospheric entry.



Fragmentation can occur when aerodynamic forces exceed the mechanical strength of the object.



Fragmentation increases surface area and can dramatically increase energy deposition in the atmosphere.



\## Dynamic Pressure



Atmospheric pressure on an incoming object increases as it penetrates deeper into the atmosphere.



Weak objects may fragment at high altitude.



Stronger objects may penetrate deeper before breaking apart.



\## Airburst



An airburst occurs when a meteoroid or asteroid releases a large amount of energy in the atmosphere rather than reaching the ground intact.



The energy can generate:



\- Shock waves

\- Heat

\- Bright flashes

\- Fragmentation



Large airbursts can cause serious surface damage even without creating an impact crater.



\## Chelyabinsk Event



On February 15, 2013, an asteroid-sized object entered Earth's atmosphere over Chelyabinsk, Russia.



The object was approximately around 20 meters in diameter.



It entered at high speed and exploded in the atmosphere.



The resulting shock wave damaged buildings and shattered windows.



Many people were injured, primarily by broken glass.



The Chelyabinsk event demonstrated that relatively small near-Earth objects can cause significant local damage.



\## Tunguska Event



On June 30, 1908, a powerful atmospheric explosion occurred over the Tunguska region of Siberia.



The event flattened a vast area of forest.



The most widely accepted explanation is an atmospheric explosion caused by a small asteroid or cometary body.



No large conventional impact crater was produced.



\## Meteorite Survival



Not every meteoroid becomes a meteorite.



Most small meteoroids completely ablate in the atmosphere.



Larger or stronger objects have a greater chance of producing surviving fragments.



Survival depends on:



\- Initial size

\- Composition

\- Strength

\- Entry speed

\- Entry angle

\- Fragmentation

\- Atmospheric trajectory



\## Terminal Velocity



After a surviving meteorite loses most of its cosmic velocity, atmospheric drag can slow it substantially.



It then falls toward Earth at approximately terminal velocity rather than continuing at its original interplanetary speed.



This stage is sometimes called dark flight because the object is no longer producing a bright meteor.



\## Dark Flight



Dark flight is the final portion of a meteorite's atmospheric descent after luminous flight has ended.



During dark flight:



\- Velocity is much lower

\- Winds can alter the trajectory

\- The meteorite is usually no longer glowing visibly



Predicting dark flight helps scientists locate meteorite fall zones.



\## Fusion Crust



Meteorites often develop a thin, dark outer layer called a fusion crust.



It forms when the exterior melts during atmospheric entry and rapidly cools.



Fusion crust is generally thin because atmospheric heating occurs primarily at the surface.



\## Are Meteorites Molten Inside?



Usually not.



Atmospheric entry is extremely rapid.



The outer surface may melt or ablate, while the interior can remain relatively cool.



Meteorites are therefore not necessarily glowing-hot molten rocks when they reach the ground.



\## Regmaglypts



Some meteorites display thumbprint-like depressions called regmaglypts.



They can form during atmospheric ablation.



They are particularly noticeable on some iron meteorites.



\## Meteorite Classification



Meteorites are commonly divided into three broad groups:



\- Stony meteorites

\- Iron meteorites

\- Stony-iron meteorites



\## Stony Meteorites



Stony meteorites are composed primarily of silicate minerals.



They are the most commonly recovered meteorite type.



Major categories include:



\- Chondrites

\- Achondrites



\## Chondrites



Chondrites are primitive stony meteorites.



Many contain small spherical structures called:



Chondrules



Chondrites are extremely important because many have undergone relatively little geological alteration since the early Solar System.



They preserve material dating from approximately 4.56 billion years ago.



\## Chondrules



Chondrules are small, rounded mineral grains found in many chondritic meteorites.



They formed from molten or partially molten droplets in the early Solar System.



Their exact formation mechanisms remain an important research topic.



\## Carbonaceous Chondrites



Carbonaceous chondrites are primitive meteorites containing carbon-rich material.



Some contain:



\- Organic molecules

\- Hydrated minerals

\- Water-bearing compounds



They are scientifically important for studying:



\- Early Solar System chemistry

\- Water distribution

\- Organic chemistry

\- Prebiotic materials



\## Amino Acids in Meteorites



Some carbonaceous meteorites contain amino acids and other organic compounds.



This demonstrates that complex organic chemistry can occur naturally in space.



It does not prove that life itself arrived on meteorites.



\## Murchison Meteorite



The Murchison meteorite fell in Australia in 1969.



It is a carbonaceous chondrite famous for containing a diverse collection of organic compounds, including amino acids.



It is extensively studied in research on prebiotic chemistry.



\## Achondrites



Achondrites are stony meteorites that do not contain chondrules in the same primitive form as chondrites.



Many originated from differentiated parent bodies that experienced:



\- Melting

\- Crystallization

\- Volcanism

\- Geological processing



Some achondrites originate from:



\- Asteroids

\- Moon

\- Mars



\## Differentiation



Planetary differentiation occurs when a sufficiently large body becomes internally separated into layers.



Dense materials sink toward the center.



Lighter silicate materials remain closer to the surface.



This can produce:



\- Metallic core

\- Silicate mantle

\- Crust



Meteorites can preserve fragments from these different layers.



\## Iron Meteorites



Iron meteorites consist primarily of:



\- Iron

\- Nickel



Many are thought to represent fragments of metallic cores of differentiated asteroid parent bodies.



They provide direct evidence about core-forming processes in early planetesimals.



\## Nickel in Meteorites



Iron meteorites often contain significant nickel.



The iron-nickel composition helps distinguish many meteorites from ordinary terrestrial rocks.



However, chemical and structural analysis is usually required for reliable identification.



\## Widmanstätten Pattern



Some iron meteorites display distinctive interlocking crystalline structures called Widmanstätten patterns.



These patterns form through extremely slow cooling of iron-nickel material inside asteroid parent bodies.



Such slow cooling cannot be reproduced easily in ordinary terrestrial environments.



\## Stony-Iron Meteorites



Stony-iron meteorites contain substantial amounts of both:



\- Silicate minerals

\- Iron-nickel metal



Major types include:



\- Pallasites

\- Mesosiderites



\## Pallasites



Pallasites contain crystals of olivine embedded in iron-nickel metal.



They are among the most visually distinctive meteorites.



Their exact formation environments remain an area of research.



\## Mesosiderites



Mesosiderites are mixtures of silicate rock and metal.



They probably formed through violent collisions and mixing within or between differentiated asteroid bodies.



\## Primitive Meteorites



Primitive meteorites preserve relatively unprocessed material from the early Solar System.



Chondrites are particularly important examples.



They provide information about:



\- Solar System age

\- Initial composition

\- Early chemistry

\- Formation of solids

\- Planet-building material



\## Meteorites and the Age of the Solar System



Radiometric dating of meteorites provides some of the best estimates for the age of the Solar System.



The oldest Solar System solids date to approximately:



4.567 billion years ago



These measurements help establish the timeline of Solar System formation.



\## Calcium-Aluminum-Rich Inclusions



Some primitive meteorites contain calcium-aluminum-rich inclusions, commonly abbreviated:



CAIs



CAIs are among the oldest known solid materials formed in the Solar System.



Their ages provide an important reference point for the beginning of Solar System history.



\## Isotopes



Meteorites contain isotopes that allow scientists to study:



\- Radioactive decay

\- Formation ages

\- Thermal histories

\- Cosmic-ray exposure

\- Parent-body evolution



Important isotope systems include:



\- Uranium-lead

\- Rubidium-strontium

\- Samarium-neodymium

\- Aluminum-magnesium



\## Cosmic-Ray Exposure Age



While a meteoroid travels through space, it is exposed to cosmic rays.



These energetic particles produce characteristic isotopes.



Scientists can measure these isotopes to estimate how long the meteoroid traveled through space after being separated from its parent body.



\## Parent Body



A parent body is the larger asteroid, comet, planet, or moon from which a meteoroid or meteorite originated.



Determining parent bodies helps scientists reconstruct Solar System history.



\## Asteroid Families



Collisions can break asteroids apart and create groups of fragments with related orbits.



These groups are called asteroid families.



Some meteorites may originate from such families.



\## Meteorites From Vesta



Several meteorite groups are strongly associated with asteroid Vesta.



These include:



\- Howardites

\- Eucrites

\- Diogenites



Collectively they are called HED meteorites.



Spectral observations and spacecraft measurements support the connection between HED meteorites and Vesta.



\## HED Meteorites



HED stands for:



Howardite



Eucrite



Diogenite



These meteorites provide samples of a differentiated asteroid.



\## Lunar Meteorites



Lunar meteorites are rocks blasted from the Moon by impacts and later delivered naturally to Earth.



They complement samples collected directly by Apollo and Luna missions.



\## Martian Meteorites



Martian meteorites provide physical samples of Mars without requiring sample-return missions.



They allow laboratory analysis of:



\- Martian rocks

\- Mineralogy

\- Isotopes

\- Water-related alteration

\- Volcanic history

\- Atmospheric gases



\## SNC Meteorites



Many Martian meteorites historically fall into groups called:



\- Shergottites

\- Nakhlites

\- Chassignites



Together they are often called SNC meteorites.



Additional Martian meteorite types are also known.



\## ALH 84001



ALH 84001 is a famous Martian meteorite discovered in Antarctica.



In the 1990s, researchers reported structures and chemical features that were proposed as possible evidence of ancient Martian life.



The interpretation became highly controversial.



The meteorite is not accepted as definitive evidence of extraterrestrial life.



\## Meteorite Fall



A meteorite fall is an event in which a meteor is observed and surviving meteorites are subsequently recovered.



Because the trajectory is known, falls provide valuable scientific information.



\## Meteorite Find



A meteorite find is a meteorite discovered without anyone having observed its fall.



Many meteorites found in:



\- Antarctica

\- Deserts



belong to this category.



\## Why Antarctica Is Important



Antarctica is one of the world's best locations for meteorite recovery.



Reasons include:



\- Dark meteorites contrast with ice

\- Cold, dry conditions preserve specimens

\- Ice movement can concentrate meteorites in certain regions

\- Limited terrestrial rock exposure makes meteorites easier to recognize



\## Deserts



Hot deserts are also excellent locations for meteorite searches.



Meteorites are easier to detect against relatively uniform surfaces.



Dry environments can also reduce some forms of weathering.



\## Meteorite Weathering



After reaching Earth, meteorites begin interacting with:



\- Oxygen

\- Water

\- Soil

\- Microorganisms



Iron-bearing meteorites can rust.



Weathering can gradually alter their original extraterrestrial composition.



\## Contamination



Meteorites can become contaminated by terrestrial material.



Scientists therefore use careful collection and storage procedures for scientifically valuable specimens.



\## Meteor Showers



A meteor shower occurs when Earth passes through a stream of debris in space.



Most major meteor showers are associated with debris released by comets.



Some are associated with asteroids or objects with comet-like histories.



\## Meteor Shower Radiant



During a meteor shower, meteors appear to originate from a common point in the sky.



This apparent point is called the:



Radiant



The effect is caused by perspective.



The particles actually travel along roughly parallel trajectories.



\## Naming Meteor Showers



Meteor showers are usually named after the constellation containing their radiant.



Examples:



Perseids → Perseus



Leonids → Leo



Geminids → Gemini



Orionids → Orion



\## Perseid Meteor Shower



The Perseids are one of the best-known annual meteor showers.



Parent body:



Comet 109P/Swift-Tuttle



The shower typically peaks in August.



\## Leonid Meteor Shower



The Leonids are associated with:



Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle



The Leonids can occasionally produce exceptionally intense meteor storms.



\## Geminid Meteor Shower



The Geminids are unusual because their parent body is:



3200 Phaethon



Phaethon is an asteroid-like object with unusual characteristics.



The Geminids are among the strongest annual meteor showers.



\## Quadrantids



The Quadrantids are another major annual meteor shower.



Their likely parent body is associated with:



2003 EH1



The shower has a relatively sharp peak.



\## Orionids



The Orionids are associated with debris from:



Halley's Comet



Earth encounters Halley-related debris during more than one annual meteor shower.



\## Eta Aquariids



The Eta Aquariids are also associated with Halley's Comet.



They are particularly favorable for observers in the Southern Hemisphere.



\## Sporadic Meteors



Not every meteor belongs to a meteor shower.



Meteors that are not clearly associated with a known shower are called sporadic meteors.



They occur throughout the year.



\## Meteor Storm



A meteor storm is an exceptionally intense meteor shower.



Meteor rates can become dramatically higher than during ordinary showers.



Meteor storms occur when Earth passes through particularly dense regions of a debris stream.



\## Zenithal Hourly Rate



Meteor shower activity is often described using the Zenithal Hourly Rate, or ZHR.



ZHR estimates how many meteors an observer might see per hour under ideal conditions with the radiant overhead and a dark sky.



Actual observed rates are often lower.



\## Meteor Observation



Meteors can be studied using:



\- Human visual observation

\- Photography

\- Video cameras

\- Spectroscopy

\- Radar

\- Infrasound

\- Satellites



Each technique provides different information.



\## Meteor Radar



Ionized meteor trails can reflect radio waves.



Radar systems can therefore detect meteors even when:



\- It is daytime

\- Clouds are present

\- Meteors are too faint for visual observation



\## Meteor Spectroscopy



Meteor spectra reveal emission from:



\- Meteoroid material

\- Atmospheric gases



Spectroscopy can help estimate chemical composition.



\## Meteorites Versus Earth Rocks



Meteorites can resemble ordinary terrestrial rocks.



Possible identifying characteristics include:



\- Fusion crust

\- High density

\- Magnetic response

\- Iron-nickel metal

\- Chondrules

\- Regmaglypts



However, none of these alone guarantees that a rock is a meteorite.



Laboratory analysis is the most reliable method.



\## Meteorites and Magnetism



Many meteorites contain iron-nickel metal and respond to magnets.



However:



Not all meteorites are strongly magnetic.



Many terrestrial rocks are also magnetic.



Magnetism alone cannot confirm a meteorite.



\## Meteorites and Holes



Meteorites generally do not contain large numbers of gas bubbles like many volcanic rocks.



A rock filled with obvious vesicles is often terrestrial slag or volcanic material rather than a meteorite.



There are exceptions and identification requires expert analysis.



\## Meteorite Impact



If an incoming object is sufficiently large, it may retain enough energy to strike the surface at high velocity.



The collision can create an impact crater.



\## Impact Crater Formation



A hypervelocity impact releases enormous kinetic energy.



The basic stages include:



\- Contact and compression

\- Excavation

\- Modification



The event can:



\- Compress rock

\- Melt rock

\- Vaporize material

\- Eject debris

\- Create shock metamorphism



\## Meteor Crater



Meteor Crater in Arizona is a famous impact crater.



It formed approximately 50,000 years ago.



The impactor was an iron-rich asteroid or meteoroid tens of meters across.



The crater is approximately:



1.2 km in diameter



\## Impact Energy



Kinetic energy is:



E = 1/2 mv²



Because velocity is squared, high-speed cosmic objects carry enormous kinetic energy even when their mass is relatively modest.



\## Why Small Objects Can Be Dangerous



A small asteroid traveling at tens of kilometers per second can release energy equivalent to a large explosion.



Therefore, impact hazard depends on both:



\- Mass

\- Velocity



\## Atmospheric Protection



Earth's atmosphere provides significant protection against small incoming objects.



Most small meteoroids:



\- Ablate

\- Fragment

\- Vaporize



before reaching the surface.



Larger objects can penetrate much farther.



\## Size and Damage



Very small particles:



Usually burn up high in the atmosphere.



Meter-scale objects:



May produce bright fireballs and sometimes meteorites.



Objects tens of meters across:



Can produce damaging airbursts.



Objects hundreds of meters across:



Can cause severe regional destruction.



Kilometer-scale objects:



Can have global consequences.



Exact effects depend on:



\- Composition

\- Velocity

\- Entry angle

\- Impact location



\## Chelyabinsk Versus Tunguska



Chelyabinsk:



\- 2013

\- Object around 20 meters

\- Powerful airburst

\- Window damage and injuries



Tunguska:



\- 1908

\- Larger object

\- Powerful atmospheric explosion

\- Flattened large areas of forest



Both demonstrate the importance of atmospheric impact events.



\## Chicxulub Impact



The Chicxulub impact occurred approximately:



66 million years ago



The impactor was roughly around:



10 km in diameter



The event contributed to the end-Cretaceous mass extinction, including the extinction of non-avian dinosaurs.



The impactor was asteroid-scale rather than what would normally be described as a small meteoroid.



\## Meteorites and Mass Extinction



Most meteorites are harmless.



However, very large asteroid or comet impacts can cause global environmental effects.



Potential consequences include:



\- Dust in atmosphere

\- Reduced sunlight

\- Wildfires

\- Tsunamis

\- Climate disruption

\- Ecosystem collapse



\## Meteorites and Planetary Defense



Scientists monitor near-Earth objects to identify potential future impact hazards.



Planetary-defense work includes:



\- Detection

\- Orbit determination

\- Impact probability calculations

\- Physical characterization

\- Deflection research



\## Near-Earth Objects



Near-Earth objects, or NEOs, are asteroids and comets whose orbits bring them relatively close to Earth's orbit.



Most NEOs pose no immediate danger.



Continuous observation improves impact-risk predictions.



\## DART Mission



NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test, or DART, demonstrated asteroid deflection using a kinetic impactor.



In 2022, DART intentionally struck Dimorphos, the moonlet of asteroid Didymos.



The impact changed Dimorphos's orbital period.



This demonstrated that kinetic impact can alter the orbit of a small asteroid.



\## Meteorites as Scientific Samples



Meteorites are extraordinarily valuable because they provide physical samples from space without requiring spacecraft sample-return missions.



They allow laboratory measurements impossible with remote telescopes.



\## What Meteorites Tell Us



Meteorites provide information about:



\- Age of the Solar System

\- Formation of planets

\- Asteroid interiors

\- Early Solar System chemistry

\- Water distribution

\- Organic molecules

\- Planetary differentiation

\- Collisions

\- Magnetic fields

\- Thermal histories



\## Meteorites and Earth's Water



Some meteorites contain water-bearing minerals.



Carbonaceous asteroids are considered important possible contributors to Earth's water inventory.



Isotope measurements help scientists compare potential sources.



\## Meteorites and Origin of Life



Meteorites can contain organic compounds relevant to prebiotic chemistry.



They may have delivered:



\- Organic molecules

\- Carbon

\- Water

\- Other volatile compounds



to early Earth.



However, meteorites containing organic molecules do not demonstrate that life itself originated in space.



\## Meteorites on Other Planets



Meteorites can also land on:



\- Moon

\- Mars

\- Mercury

\- Other solid bodies



Mars rovers have identified meteorites on the Martian surface.



Because Mars has a thinner atmosphere than Earth, atmospheric entry conditions differ.



\## Meteors on Other Planets



Meteor phenomena can occur in any atmosphere if incoming particles interact with atmospheric gases strongly enough.



Meteors have been studied or inferred in planetary atmospheres beyond Earth.



\## Moon and Meteors



The Moon has essentially no substantial atmosphere.



Therefore, incoming meteoroids generally strike its surface directly rather than producing Earth-like atmospheric meteors.



These impacts contribute to the formation and modification of lunar regolith.



\## Micrometeoroid Bombardment



Airless bodies are continuously bombarded by tiny meteoroids.



Over geological time, this contributes to:



\- Surface erosion

\- Regolith formation

\- Space weathering

\- Impact gardening



\## Impact Gardening



Impact gardening is the repeated mixing and overturning of surface material caused by impacts.



It is particularly important on airless bodies such as the Moon.



\## Meteoroids and Spacecraft



Micrometeoroids can damage spacecraft because their impact velocities are extremely high.



Spacecraft may use protective structures such as:



Whipple shields



to reduce damage.



\## Whipple Shield



A Whipple shield uses multiple layers separated by space.



An incoming small high-speed particle strikes the outer layer and fragments.



The resulting debris spreads before reaching the inner wall.



This reduces concentrated impact damage.



\## International Space Station



The International Space Station uses protective shielding against:



\- Micrometeoroids

\- Orbital debris



Both can travel at dangerous relative velocities.



\## Meteoroids Versus Space Debris



Meteoroids are natural objects.



Orbital debris is human-made material in space.



Examples of orbital debris include:



\- Defunct satellites

\- Rocket fragments

\- Collision fragments



Both can threaten spacecraft.



\## Meteorite Value



Meteorites can have scientific and commercial value.



Scientific value depends on:



\- Rarity

\- Classification

\- Preservation

\- Provenance

\- Research importance



A rare meteorite may be scientifically valuable even if it is small.



\## Meteorite Collection



Meteorites should be documented carefully.



Important information includes:



\- Exact location

\- Date

\- Context

\- Photographs

\- Orientation

\- Nearby fragments



Poor handling can contaminate scientifically valuable material.



\## Meteorite Recovery After a Fireball



Scientists can combine observations from multiple cameras to reconstruct a meteor trajectory.



They can estimate:



\- Atmospheric path

\- Velocity

\- Fragmentation

\- Original orbit

\- Potential landing area



This allows organized meteorite searches.



\## Meteorite Strewn Field



When a meteoroid fragments and multiple pieces reach the ground, fragments may be distributed over an elongated area.



This is called a:



Strewn field



Larger fragments may travel differently from smaller fragments during dark flight.



\## Meteorite Naming



Meteorites are generally named according to the location where they were found or fell.



Formal naming follows established scientific conventions.



\## Famous Meteorites



Important examples include:



\- Murchison

\- Allende

\- Hoba

\- ALH 84001

\- Orgueil

\- Sikhote-Alin



Each is scientifically significant for different reasons.



\## Hoba Meteorite



The Hoba meteorite in Namibia is the largest known intact meteorite on Earth.



It is an iron meteorite.



Its mass is approximately:



60 tonnes



It remains at the site where it was discovered.



\## Allende Meteorite



The Allende meteorite fell in Mexico in 1969.



It is a carbonaceous chondrite.



It contains calcium-aluminum-rich inclusions.



These inclusions are among the oldest known Solar System solids.



\## Sikhote-Alin Meteorite



The Sikhote-Alin meteorite fall occurred in Russia in 1947.



A large iron meteoroid fragmented in the atmosphere.



Thousands of fragments reached the ground.



The event produced numerous small impact craters.



\## Meteorites and Earth's Geology



Meteorites can sometimes be difficult to recognize after long periods on Earth because weathering changes their appearance.



Ancient meteorites can also become incorporated into geological deposits.



\## Fossil Meteorites



Meteorites preserved within ancient sedimentary rocks are sometimes called fossil meteorites.



They can provide information about ancient asteroid collisions and Solar System events.



\## Meteorites and Asteroid Mining



Meteorite compositions help scientists understand resources present in asteroids.



Potential asteroid materials include:



\- Iron

\- Nickel

\- Water

\- Platinum-group elements



However, asteroid mining remains technologically and economically challenging.



\## Common Misconceptions



\### A Meteor Is a Rock



Not exactly.



A meteoroid is the object in space.



A meteor is the luminous atmospheric phenomenon.



A meteorite is surviving material reaching the ground.



\### Meteors Are Stars



Incorrect.



Stars are enormous distant astronomical objects.



A shooting star is a meteor.



\### Every Meteor Produces a Meteorite



Incorrect.



Most small meteoroids completely ablate in the atmosphere.



\### Meteorites Reach the Ground as Molten Balls



Usually incorrect.



Their surfaces experience intense heating, but atmospheric entry is brief.



The interior may remain relatively cool.



\### Meteorites Are Radioactive and Dangerous



Most meteorites are not dangerously radioactive.



They contain naturally occurring isotopes like ordinary rocks.



\### All Meteorites Are Magnetic



Incorrect.



Many contain magnetic iron-nickel material, but not all are strongly magnetic.



\### Any Magnetic Rock Is a Meteorite



Incorrect.



Many terrestrial rocks and industrial materials are magnetic.



\### Meteorites Always Make Craters



Incorrect.



Small meteorites usually land after being slowed dramatically by the atmosphere and do not create large impact craters.



\### Meteors Burn Because of Simple Friction



This is an oversimplification.



Atmospheric compression, shock heating, ablation, excitation, and ionization all contribute.



\### Meteor Showers Are Dangerous Rains of Rocks



Ordinary meteor showers are produced mostly by tiny particles that burn up high in the atmosphere.



They do not normally represent a ground-impact hazard.



\### The Asteroid Belt Is the Only Source of Meteorites



Incorrect.



Meteorites can originate from several bodies, including:



\- Asteroids

\- Moon

\- Mars



\### Comets and Meteoroids Are the Same



Incorrect.



Comets are larger volatile-rich bodies.



They can release particles that become meteoroids.



\## Important Terminology



\### Meteoroid



A small natural solid object traveling through space.



\### Meteor



The luminous atmospheric phenomenon associated with meteoroid entry.



\### Meteorite



Extraterrestrial material that survives atmospheric passage and reaches a surface.



\### Fireball



An unusually bright meteor.



\### Bolide



An exceptionally bright and often explosively fragmenting meteor.



\### Ablation



Loss of material during atmospheric entry.



\### Fusion Crust



Thin melted and rapidly cooled surface layer on many meteorites.



\### Chondrite



Primitive stony meteorite containing chondrules.



\### Achondrite



Stony meteorite lacking primitive chondrules and commonly associated with differentiated parent bodies.



\### Chondrule



Small rounded mineral object found in many primitive meteorites.



\### CAI



Calcium-aluminum-rich inclusion.



Among the oldest known Solar System solids.



\### Parent Body



Larger astronomical body from which a meteorite originated.



\### Strewn Field



Area over which meteorite fragments from one fall are distributed.



\### Dark Flight



Non-luminous final descent of meteorite fragments.



\### Radiant



Apparent point in the sky from which meteor-shower meteors seem to originate.



\## Open Scientific Questions



Scientists continue investigating:



\- Exactly how chondrules formed

\- How CAIs formed and were transported

\- How quickly planetesimals formed

\- Which asteroids produce particular meteorite groups

\- How much water meteorites delivered to early Earth

\- How important extraterrestrial organics were for prebiotic chemistry

\- How asteroid collisions evolved through Solar System history

\- How often dangerous airbursts occur

\- How many small near-Earth objects remain undiscovered

\- How accurately atmospheric fragmentation can be predicted

\- How different meteorite parent bodies formed and differentiated

\- How primitive meteorites preserve early Solar System magnetic fields

\- How efficiently meteorites move from the asteroid belt to Earth

\- How Martian meteorites are launched without being completely destroyed

\- How meteorite delivery rates changed throughout Earth's history



\## Meteoroids Versus Asteroids



Asteroids are generally larger natural rocky or metallic bodies orbiting the Sun.



Meteoroids are generally smaller.



The boundary is partly conventional rather than representing a fundamental physical distinction.



\## Meteoroids Versus Comets



Meteoroids are small solid particles or fragments.



Comets are larger bodies containing volatile material that can become active near the Sun.



Comets can release enormous numbers of meteoroids.



\## Meteors Versus Meteorites



Meteor:



Atmospheric light phenomenon.



Meteorite:



Physical extraterrestrial material recovered from a surface.



\## Meteorites Versus Asteroids



Meteorites are samples that have reached a planetary surface.



Asteroids are larger bodies orbiting the Sun.



Many meteorites are fragments of asteroids.



\## Why Meteorites Matter



Meteorites are among the most important natural samples available to planetary scientists.



Unlike most planets and asteroids, which can usually be studied only remotely or through expensive spacecraft missions, meteorites can be analyzed directly in laboratories.



They allow extremely precise measurements of:



\- Age

\- Isotopes

\- Minerals

\- Organic compounds

\- Magnetic properties

\- Chemical composition



Meteorites therefore provide direct evidence about the earliest stages of Solar System history.



\## Summary



Meteoroids, meteors, and meteorites represent closely related but distinct concepts.



A meteoroid is a small natural solid object traveling through space.



When it enters an atmosphere at high velocity, interactions with atmospheric gas produce a luminous meteor.



If part of the object survives atmospheric passage and reaches the surface, it becomes a meteorite.



Meteoroids originate primarily from asteroids and comets, although meteorites from the Moon and Mars are also found on Earth.



Meteorites are classified broadly into stony, iron, and stony-iron groups.



Primitive chondrites preserve some of the oldest material in the Solar System and provide critical evidence about its formation approximately 4.6 billion years ago.



Meteor showers occur when Earth passes through streams of debris, commonly left behind by comets.



Larger incoming objects can produce fireballs, airbursts, impact craters, and potentially hazardous events.



Meteorites allow scientists to directly study asteroid interiors, planetary differentiation, Solar System chronology, water, organic chemistry, collisions, and the building materials from which planets formed.
