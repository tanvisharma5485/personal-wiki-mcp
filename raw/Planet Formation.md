\# Planet Formation



\## Overview



Planet formation is the process by which planets develop from material surrounding a young star. The modern scientific picture begins with the collapse of a molecular cloud, the formation of a protostar, and the creation of a rotating disk of gas and dust around it.



This disk is called a:



Protoplanetary disk



Within the disk, tiny dust grains collide, stick together, grow into larger bodies, and eventually form planetesimals, planetary embryos, and planets.



Planet formation is closely connected with \[\[Solar System Formation]], \[\[Planetary Migration]], \[\[Planetary Differentiation]], \[\[Orbital Dynamics]], and the study of \[\[Exoplanets]].



\## Birth of a Star and Disk



Stars form inside cold molecular clouds composed mainly of hydrogen and helium, with smaller quantities of heavier elements and dust.



A region of the cloud can become gravitationally unstable and begin collapsing.



As the cloud contracts, conservation of angular momentum causes it to rotate more rapidly.



Most material falls toward the center and forms a protostar, while some material settles into a flattened rotating disk around it.



This disk becomes the environment in which planets can form.



\## Protoplanetary Disk



A protoplanetary disk contains gas, dust, ice, and chemical compounds orbiting a young star.



The disk is not uniform.



Temperature, density, pressure, and composition vary strongly with distance from the star.



The inner disk is usually hotter, while the outer disk is colder.



These temperature differences strongly influence what materials can condense into solids.



\## Disk Lifetime



Protoplanetary disks do not survive indefinitely.



Gas disks around young stars generally disperse over timescales of a few million years, although exact lifetimes vary.



This creates an important time constraint.



Gas giant planets must acquire most of their hydrogen and helium before the disk gas disappears.



\## Dust Grains



Planet formation begins with microscopic solid particles.



These grains may contain:



Silicates, metals, carbon-rich compounds, and ices depending on temperature and location in the disk.



Dust grains collide due to relative motion caused by turbulence, settling, and orbital effects.



At sufficiently low collision speeds, grains can stick together.



\## Dust Coagulation



The process by which small grains collide and grow into larger aggregates is called:



Dust coagulation



The first stages can produce porous structures made of many tiny particles.



These aggregates gradually increase in size.



\## From Dust to Pebbles



Dust grains can grow into millimeter- to centimeter-scale objects often described as:



Pebbles



Pebble-sized material is extremely important in modern planet formation models because planets and planetesimals may grow efficiently by collecting large quantities of drifting pebbles.



\## Radial Drift



Gas in a protoplanetary disk does not orbit at exactly the same speed as solid particles.



Gas pressure causes the gas to move slightly more slowly than the local Keplerian orbital speed.



Solid particles therefore experience a headwind.



This removes orbital energy and causes many particles to drift inward toward the star.



This is called:



Radial drift



\## Radial Drift Barrier



Meter- and smaller-scale particles can move inward rapidly.



If growth is too slow, large quantities of solids may spiral into the star before they can form planets.



This challenge is called the:



Radial drift barrier



Understanding how solids overcome this barrier is an important problem in planet formation research.



\## Fragmentation Barrier



Collisions do not always result in sticking.



If two aggregates collide too quickly, they may:



Bounce



Fragment



Erode one another



This creates another difficulty known as the:



Fragmentation barrier



\## Planetesimal Formation



A major transition occurs when small solids form much larger objects called:



Planetesimals



Planetesimals are gravitationally bound solid bodies that can range from roughly kilometer scale to hundreds of kilometers in size.



Once they are large enough, gravity becomes increasingly important in their growth.



\## Streaming Instability



One important model for planetesimal formation is:



Streaming instability



In this process, interactions between gas and concentrated solid particles cause pebbles to collect into dense clumps.



If a clump becomes dense enough, its self-gravity can cause it to collapse.



This may rapidly form large planetesimals.



\## Why Streaming Instability Matters



Streaming instability can potentially bypass some of the difficulties involved in growing continuously from centimeter-sized particles to kilometer-sized objects.



Instead of every intermediate size forming through slow pairwise collisions, dense collections of pebbles can collapse gravitationally.



\## Pressure Bumps



Local high-pressure regions in a disk can slow or stop inward particle drift.



These locations are called:



Pressure bumps



They can concentrate solids and may create favorable sites for planetesimal formation.



\## Rings and Gaps in Disks



Modern telescopes have observed many protoplanetary disks containing:



Rings



Gaps



Spirals



Asymmetries



Some of these structures may be produced by forming planets.



Others can result from disk physics, chemistry, ice lines, or pressure variations.



Therefore, not every gap is direct evidence of a planet.



\## ALMA Observations



The Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array, or:



ALMA



has produced detailed images of protoplanetary disks.



These observations show that disk substructure can appear very early in stellar evolution.



This has strongly influenced modern theories of how quickly planet formation may begin.



\## Gravitational Growth



Once planetesimals exist, gravity increases the probability of collisions.



A larger body can attract nearby smaller bodies, increasing its effective collision area.



This effect is called:



Gravitational focusing



\## Runaway Growth



During early gravitational growth, slightly larger planetesimals can grow faster than smaller ones because their stronger gravity allows them to capture more material.



This process is called:



Runaway growth



A small number of bodies can therefore become much larger than the surrounding population.



\## Oligarchic Growth



As the largest bodies grow, they begin to dominate and dynamically stir nearby planetesimals.



Growth enters a stage called:



Oligarchic growth



Several planetary embryos then grow within separate feeding zones.



\## Planetary Embryos



Planetary embryos are bodies larger than typical planetesimals but smaller than fully formed planets.



In the inner Solar System, some embryos may have reached sizes comparable to:



The Moon



or:



Mars



before the final stage of terrestrial planet formation.



\## Feeding Zone



A planet's feeding zone is the region of the disk from which it acquires much of its material.



As planets grow and interact gravitationally, feeding zones can overlap.



This causes mixing of material from different parts of the disk.



\## Accretion



The overall process of gaining material through collisions and gravitational capture is called:



Accretion



Planet formation involves multiple forms of accretion, including:



Dust accretion



Planetesimal accretion



Pebble accretion



Gas accretion



\## Pebble Accretion



Pebble accretion occurs when a growing planet captures small solid particles that are partially coupled to the surrounding gas.



Gas drag can help slow the particles enough for them to be captured.



This can make planetary growth much faster than planetesimal collisions alone.



\## Pebble Isolation Mass



As a growing planet becomes sufficiently massive, it can disturb the gas disk and create a pressure structure that prevents additional pebbles from drifting inward.



The approximate mass where this occurs is called:



Pebble isolation mass



This can strongly influence the later growth of planetary cores.



\## Terrestrial Planet Formation



Rocky planets generally form in the hotter inner regions of planetary systems where temperatures are too high for large quantities of volatile ices to condense.



The available solids there are dominated by:



Silicate rock



Metal



Refractory compounds



\## Terrestrial Planet Growth



A simplified terrestrial planet formation sequence is:



Dust grains grow



↓



Planetesimals form



↓



Planetary embryos develop



↓



Embryos gravitationally interact



↓



Giant impacts occur



↓



A small number of rocky planets remain



This final stage can continue for tens to hundreds of millions of years after the initial disk forms.



\## Giant Impacts



During late-stage rocky planet formation, planetary embryos can collide at high velocities.



These are called:



Giant impacts



They can:



Merge bodies



Strip atmospheres



Remove mantle material



Create debris disks



Change planetary rotation



Create moons



\## Moon Formation



The leading model for the formation of Earth's \[\[Moon]] involves a giant impact between the early Earth and another large planetary body.



This illustrates how violent collisions can be a normal part of planetary formation.



\## Core Formation During Accretion



As rocky planets grow, impact energy and radioactive heating can melt substantial portions of their interiors.



Dense metals such as iron can sink toward the center.



Lighter silicate rock remains above.



This process leads to \[\[Planetary Differentiation]].



\## Giant Planet Formation



Gas giants such as \[\[Jupiter]] and \[\[Saturn]] contain enormous quantities of hydrogen and helium.



Two major classes of models are used to explain giant planet formation:



Core accretion



and:



Disk instability



Core accretion is widely used in many models, while disk instability may operate under certain disk conditions.



\## Core Accretion Model



In the core accretion model, a solid planetary core grows first.



The core forms from:



Planetesimals



Pebbles



Other solid material



Once it becomes sufficiently massive, it begins capturing a substantial atmosphere from the surrounding disk.



\## Critical Core Mass



A core reaching approximately several to perhaps around ten Earth masses, depending strongly on disk conditions and atmosphere physics, may become capable of rapid gas accretion.



There is no single universal critical mass.



It depends on factors such as:



Temperature



Opacity



Accretion rate



Disk properties



\## Gas Envelope



A growing giant planet develops an atmosphere or:



Gas envelope



around its solid core.



Initially, this envelope may grow relatively slowly because it must cool and contract before more gas can be accumulated.



\## Runaway Gas Accretion



Once the envelope becomes sufficiently massive, gas accretion can accelerate dramatically.



This phase is called:



Runaway gas accretion



A planet can then acquire large quantities of hydrogen and helium before the disk disappears.



\## Jupiter and Saturn



Jupiter and Saturn are believed to have acquired large gas envelopes while the solar nebula still contained abundant hydrogen and helium.



Their formation therefore had to progress rapidly enough to occur before gas-disk dispersal.



\## Ice Giants



\[\[Uranus]] and \[\[Neptune]] contain much smaller fractions of hydrogen and helium than Jupiter and Saturn.



They are commonly classified as:



Ice giants



Their formation remains challenging to explain completely because growth at their present large distances from the Sun would have been relatively slow in simple models.



This is one reason planetary migration is important in theories of Solar System evolution.



\## Disk Instability



Another proposed mechanism for giant planet formation is:



Disk instability



A sufficiently massive and cold protoplanetary disk can become gravitationally unstable.



Parts of the disk may fragment into dense clumps.



Some clumps could contract to form giant planets.



\## Gravitational Instability Criterion



Whether a disk becomes gravitationally unstable depends on factors including:



Surface density



Temperature



Rotation



Cooling rate



A commonly used measure is the:



Toomre Q parameter



Low enough Q values can indicate gravitational instability.



\## Core Accretion Versus Disk Instability



Core accretion builds a giant planet around a solid core and may operate over millions of years.



Disk instability can potentially produce massive gas clumps much more rapidly.



The relative importance of the two processes may differ among planetary systems.



\## Snow Line



The:



Snow line



or:



Ice line



is the approximate distance from a young star beyond which temperatures are low enough for certain volatile compounds, especially water, to condense into solid ice.



The exact location changes as the disk evolves.



\## Why the Snow Line Matters



Beyond the water snow line, there is more solid material available because water ice can join rock and metal.



This may accelerate planetary core growth.



The snow line therefore has major implications for the formation of giant planets.



\## Other Ice Lines



Different molecules condense at different temperatures.



Protoplanetary disks can therefore contain additional condensation fronts associated with substances such as:



Carbon dioxide



Carbon monoxide



Ammonia



These chemical transitions can influence planetary composition.



\## Planet Composition



A planet's composition depends partly on where and when it forms.



Hot inner regions favor refractory rocky material.



Colder outer regions allow larger amounts of:



Water ice



Carbon-bearing ices



Other volatile compounds



However, later migration and mixing can substantially change this simple picture.



\## Water Delivery to Rocky Planets



Earth formed largely inside the region where temperatures were relatively high.



Some of Earth's water may therefore have been delivered or mixed in from material originating farther from the Sun.



Possible contributors include:



Water-rich asteroids



Planetesimals from outer regions



Material mixed through planetary scattering



Comets may also have contributed, but their exact contribution remains under study.



\## Protoplanetary Disk Chemistry



Disk chemistry strongly influences the material available to forming planets.



Important processes include:



Condensation



Sublimation



Chemical reactions



Ultraviolet irradiation



Dust-surface chemistry



Gas transport



A planet's atmosphere and interior chemistry can therefore preserve clues about its formation environment.



\## Carbon-to-Oxygen Ratio



The abundance ratio of carbon to oxygen can vary across a disk.



Scientists study whether atmospheric elemental ratios of giant planets can provide information about where they formed.



However, interpreting these ratios is complicated by migration, accretion history, and chemical evolution.



\## Metallicity



In astronomy, metallicity refers to the abundance of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium.



Stars with higher metallicity tend statistically to have a higher occurrence rate of giant planets.



This strongly supports the importance of solid material in giant planet formation.



\## Low-Mass Planet Formation



Smaller planets appear to form around stars covering a wider range of metallicities.



This suggests that forming Earth-sized or somewhat larger planets may require less heavy-element material than forming giant planets.



\## Planetary Migration



Newly formed planets do not necessarily remain where they originated.



Interactions with the gas disk can change their orbits.



This process is called:



\[\[Planetary Migration]]



Migration is essential for understanding many observed planetary systems.



\## Type I Migration



Lower-mass planets create gravitational disturbances in the disk but generally do not open deep gaps.



Torques from the surrounding gas can cause:



Type I migration



This can move planets inward or, under some conditions, outward.



\## Gap Opening



A sufficiently massive planet can strongly disturb the disk and create a gap around its orbit.



Whether a gap forms depends on:



Planet mass



Disk viscosity



Disk scale height



\## Type II Migration



Historically, migration of gap-opening giant planets has been described as:



Type II migration



In modern theory, the relationship between planet motion and disk gas evolution is more complex than the simplest classical picture.



\## Hot Jupiters



Hot Jupiters are giant planets orbiting extremely close to their stars.



They probably did not form in their present hot environments through a simple local version of giant planet formation.



Migration or later dynamical processes likely moved many of them inward.



\## Resonant Chains



Migrating planets can become trapped in:



Orbital resonances



Multiple planets may form a resonant chain.



Some systems preserve these resonances, while others later become dynamically unstable.



\## Disk Dispersal



Eventually the protoplanetary disk loses its gas.



Important processes include:



Accretion onto the star



Photoevaporation



Disk winds



Planet formation



When the gas disappears, gas-driven migration and gas accretion decline dramatically.



\## Photoevaporation



High-energy radiation from the young star can heat disk gas until some of it escapes.



This process is called:



Photoevaporation



It helps clear protoplanetary disks.



\## Disk Winds



Magnetic processes may also drive gas away from the disk through:



Disk winds



These may contribute strongly to disk evolution and dispersal.



\## Transition Disks



Some young stars possess disks with large inner cavities or reduced dust emission.



These are called:



Transition disks



Possible causes include:



Planet formation



Photoevaporation



Dust evolution



Multiple mechanisms may operate simultaneously.



\## Late-Stage Dynamical Evolution



Planet formation does not end immediately when the gas disk disappears.



Planetary embryos and planets can continue interacting gravitationally.



Their orbits may become unstable.



This can lead to:



Collisions



Scattering



Ejections



Orbital rearrangement



\## Planet-Planet Scattering



When planets pass close to one another, strong gravitational interactions can radically change their orbits.



This is called:



Planet-planet scattering



A planet may be:



Moved inward



Moved outward



Placed on an eccentric orbit



Ejected from the system



\## Eccentric Exoplanets



Many giant exoplanets have highly eccentric orbits.



Dynamical interactions such as planet-planet scattering may explain some of these configurations.



\## Rogue Planets



A planet ejected from its planetary system can travel through interstellar space without orbiting a star.



Such objects are called:



Rogue planets



or:



Free-floating planets



\## Orbital Instability in the Solar System



The early Solar System may have undergone significant orbital rearrangement after the planets formed.



Models involving giant planet migration are used to explain features such as:



Small-body populations



Orbital resonances



Distribution of asteroids



Outer Solar System structure



\## Nice Model



The:



Nice model



is a family of models in which the giant planets began in a more compact orbital arrangement and later migrated.



Gravitational interactions with a disk of planetesimals altered their orbits.



Different versions of the model produce different histories.



\## Grand Tack Hypothesis



The:



Grand Tack hypothesis



proposes that Jupiter first migrated inward and later reversed direction after interacting with Saturn.



This process could have strongly influenced the distribution of material in the inner Solar System.



It is one possible model rather than a proven exact history.



\## Asteroid Belt



The \[\[Asteroid Belt]] contains material left from early Solar System evolution.



Jupiter's gravity and other dynamical processes prevented this region from forming into a single large planet.



Its current mass is only a small fraction of Earth's mass.



\## Small Bodies as Leftovers



Asteroids, comets, and many trans-Neptunian objects are remnants of the planet formation process.



They preserve relatively primitive material.



Studying them provides information about the chemical and physical conditions of the early Solar System.



\## Meteorites



\[\[Meteoroids, Meteors and Meteorites|Meteorites]] provide direct laboratory samples of early Solar System solids.



Some contain materials older than the planets themselves.



\## Calcium-Aluminum-Rich Inclusions



Some primitive meteorites contain:



Calcium-Aluminum-Rich Inclusions



or:



CAIs



They are among the oldest known solids formed in the Solar System.



Their ages are approximately:



4.567 billion years



They provide an important reference point for dating Solar System formation.



\## Chondrules



Many primitive meteorites contain small rounded particles called:



Chondrules



They formed from molten or partially molten droplets early in Solar System history.



Their exact formation mechanisms remain debated.



\## Chondrites



Meteorites containing chondrules and primitive material are called:



Chondrites



Their composition can preserve information about the early solar nebula.



\## Short-Lived Radioactive Isotopes



Early Solar System materials contained radioactive isotopes such as:



Aluminum-26



Their decay generated heat inside early planetesimals.



This heating could cause:



Melting



Differentiation



Volcanic activity



\## Planetesimal Differentiation



Some early planetesimals became hot enough to separate into:



Metallic cores



Rocky mantles



Crusts



Fragments of these bodies are represented by different meteorite classes.



\## Iron Meteorites



Many iron meteorites are thought to be fragments of metallic cores of differentiated planetesimals.



They provide direct evidence that differentiation occurred very early.



\## Achondrites



Achondrites are meteorites that experienced significant melting and geological processing.



Some represent crust or mantle material from differentiated parent bodies.



\## Vesta



The asteroid \[\[Vesta]] is a differentiated protoplanet.



Meteorites linked to Vesta demonstrate that some early planetesimals developed:



Core



Mantle



Crust



\## Protoplanets



The early Solar System likely contained many more large bodies than exist today.



These:



Protoplanets



or planetary embryos



collided, merged, and were ejected until the current planetary system emerged.



\## Mars as a Possible Stranded Embryo



Some models suggest that \[\[Mars]] may represent a planetary embryo that stopped growing relatively early.



Its smaller mass compared with Earth and Venus is an important constraint on inner Solar System formation models.



\## Mercury Formation



\[\[Mercury]] has an unusually large metallic core relative to its size.



Possible explanations include:



Special composition of its formation region



High-temperature processes



Giant impacts



Selective removal of silicate material



The exact cause remains uncertain.



\## Earth Formation



\[\[Earth]] grew through accretion of planetesimals and planetary embryos.



It underwent:



Major impacts



Differentiation



Core formation



Atmosphere evolution



Moon-forming impact



Its final assembly probably lasted tens of millions of years.



\## Venus Formation



\[\[Venus]] likely formed from broadly similar inner Solar System material to Earth.



However, later atmospheric and geological evolution produced a radically different planet.



\## Jupiter's Role



The formation of \[\[Jupiter]] strongly influenced the architecture of the Solar System.



Its gravity affected:



Asteroids



Comets



Planetesimals



Other planets



Jupiter's formation time and migration history are therefore important in many Solar System models.



\## Saturn's Role



\[\[Saturn]] also participated in the dynamical evolution of the early Solar System.



Its interaction with Jupiter may have strongly influenced migration behavior.



\## Uranus and Neptune Formation



The formation of \[\[Uranus]] and \[\[Neptune]] is difficult to explain if they formed entirely at their current distances using slow planetesimal accretion.



Modern models often involve:



Pebble accretion



Migration



A more compact early planetary system



\## Kuiper Belt



The \[\[Kuiper Belt]] contains remnants from the outer regions of the planetary disk.



Its current orbital structure preserves evidence of:



Neptune migration



Resonances



Early Solar System dynamics



\## Pluto



\[\[Pluto]] is a large trans-Neptunian object that formed from outer Solar System material.



Its composition and orbit reflect the history of the Kuiper Belt and Neptune's migration.



\## Oort Cloud



The \[\[Oort Cloud]] is thought to contain icy bodies scattered outward during planet formation.



Interactions with the giant planets may have sent objects to enormous distances.



External gravitational forces then helped populate the distant cloud.



\## Binary Formation



Many small bodies, especially in the Kuiper Belt, exist as binaries.



Their existence provides clues about planetesimal formation.



Some models involving gravitational collapse of pebble clouds naturally produce binary systems.



\## Arrokoth



The trans-Neptunian object Arrokoth has a two-lobed shape.



New Horizons observations suggest it formed from a relatively gentle merger of two components.



This supports models in which some planetesimals formed in dynamically cold environments.



\## Circumplanetary Disks



Growing giant planets can develop smaller disks around themselves.



These are called:



Circumplanetary disks



Moons can form within them.



\## Regular Satellites



Many regular moons of giant planets likely formed from material in circumplanetary disks.



Examples include large moons orbiting:



Jupiter



Saturn



Uranus



\## Galilean Moons



Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto likely formed within a disk surrounding young Jupiter.



Their different compositions reflect temperature and density gradients in that environment.



\## Irregular Satellites



Many distant irregular moons were probably:



Captured



rather than formed in a simple circumplanetary disk.



Their unusual inclinations and eccentricities support this interpretation.



\## Moon Formation Mechanisms



Moons can form through several mechanisms.



These include:



Co-accretion in circumplanetary disks



Giant impacts



Gravitational capture



Fragmentation



Earth's Moon is the best-known giant-impact example.



\## Planetary Rings



Ring systems may contain material from:



Disrupted moons



Collisions



Material prevented from forming a moon inside the Roche limit



Rings therefore also connect with planetary formation and satellite evolution.



\## Multiple-Star Systems



Planet formation can also occur in binary and multiple-star systems.



The gravitational influence of additional stars can:



Truncate disks



Disturb planetary orbits



Change planet formation efficiency



Despite these challenges, many planets have been discovered in binary systems.



\## Circumbinary Planets



Some planets orbit both stars of a binary system.



These are called:



Circumbinary planets



Their existence demonstrates that planet formation can succeed even in dynamically complex environments.



\## Planets Around Red Dwarfs



Red dwarf stars commonly host small planets.



Because these stars are low mass, their disks may have different:



Masses



Temperatures



Lifetimes



Radiation environments



Yet compact multi-planet systems appear to be common.



\## TRAPPIST-1



The TRAPPIST-1 system contains seven roughly Earth-sized planets orbiting a very low-mass star.



Its compact resonant architecture provides important evidence about:



Migration



Disk evolution



Planet formation around small stars



\## Super-Earths



Super-Earths are planets more massive than Earth but less massive than planets such as Neptune, though exact classification boundaries vary.



They are common among known exoplanets.



The Solar System has no planet in this common size range.



\## Sub-Neptunes



Sub-Neptunes are generally larger than rocky super-Earths but smaller than Neptune.



Many have significant gas envelopes.



Understanding why they are common elsewhere but absent from the Solar System is an important planet formation question.



\## Radius Valley



Exoplanet observations reveal a relative shortage of planets at certain sizes between many super-Earths and sub-Neptunes.



This feature is called the:



Radius valley



It may result from atmospheric loss through:



Stellar irradiation



Core-powered mass loss



Planet formation history



\## Atmospheric Formation



Planetary atmospheres can arise from multiple sources.



Primary atmospheres consist mainly of gas captured directly from the protoplanetary disk.



Secondary atmospheres form later through:



Volcanic outgassing



Impacts



Chemical reactions



Atmospheric delivery



\## Primary Atmospheres



Gas giants retain enormous primary atmospheres composed mainly of:



Hydrogen



Helium



Some rocky planets may initially capture hydrogen-rich envelopes but later lose them.



\## Secondary Atmospheres



Terrestrial planets can develop secondary atmospheres through interior outgassing and volatile delivery.



Earth, Venus, and Mars underwent major atmospheric evolution after formation.



\## Atmospheric Loss During Formation



Young planets experience intense stellar radiation and frequent impacts.



These processes can remove or modify early atmospheres.



Therefore, planet formation and atmosphere evolution are tightly connected.



\## Impact Delivery of Volatiles



Asteroids and other small bodies can deliver:



Water



Carbon compounds



Nitrogen-bearing compounds



Other volatiles



to growing planets.



Impacts can also remove atmospheric material.



\## Magnetic Fields and Formation



Planet formation determines:



Mass



Composition



Core size



Thermal history



These factors affect whether a planet can maintain an internal dynamo and magnetic field.



Magnetic fields therefore depend indirectly on formation history.



\## Rotation



Planetary rotation rates can be altered by:



Accretion



Giant impacts



Tidal evolution



Atmospheric torques



The wide range of planetary rotation rates reflects complex histories.



\## Planetary Obliquity



Obliquity is the tilt of a planet's rotation axis.



Large impacts and gravitational interactions during formation can alter obliquity.



\[\[Uranus]], with an axial tilt near 98 degrees, may have experienced major collisions or other strong dynamical processes.



\## Planetary Eccentricity



Young planetary systems can begin with relatively circular orbits.



Later gravitational interactions may increase eccentricities.



Gas-disk interactions can also damp eccentricity.



The final orbital architecture records the balance between these processes.



\## Inclination



Planetary orbital inclinations can also be modified by:



Scattering



Resonances



Stellar companions



Disk warping



Migration



\## Formation Timescales



Different stages operate on different timescales.



Dust growth can begin very quickly.



Planetesimals may form during the first few million years.



Giant planet cores must generally form while gas remains.



Rocky planet assembly can continue for tens or even hundreds of millions of years.



Late orbital evolution can continue much longer.



\## Chronology of Solar System Formation



A simplified timeline is:



About 4.567 billion years ago:



Oldest known Solar System solids form.



Soon afterward:



Planetesimals and planetary embryos develop.



Within a few million years:



Gas giants must acquire substantial gas envelopes.



Over tens of millions of years:



Terrestrial planets continue colliding and assembling.



Later:



Planetary migration and small-body scattering reshape the Solar System.



\## Observing Planet Formation



Scientists cannot watch a single planetary system evolve for millions of years.



Instead, they observe many systems at different ages.



By comparing them, researchers reconstruct an evolutionary sequence.



\## Young Stellar Objects



Young stars are classified into evolutionary stages based partly on their surrounding material.



Early stages may contain:



Large envelopes



Dense disks



Later stages contain increasingly evolved disks.



\## Infrared Excess



Dust surrounding young stars absorbs stellar light and reradiates energy at infrared wavelengths.



This produces:



Infrared excess



It is one of the main ways protoplanetary disks are detected.



\## Disk Spectroscopy



Spectroscopy reveals disk:



Temperature



Chemical composition



Gas content



Dust mineralogy



Different wavelengths probe different disk regions.



\## Direct Imaging of Disks



Modern observatories can directly image nearby disks.



Observations reveal:



Spirals



Rings



Gaps



Dust traps



Warped structures



\## HL Tauri



ALMA observations of:



HL Tauri



revealed spectacular rings and gaps in a very young protoplanetary disk.



The image became an important example showing that disk structure can develop very early.



\## PDS 70



The star:



PDS 70



hosts a protoplanetary disk containing directly observed forming giant planets.



It provides rare evidence of planets still embedded in their birth environment.



\## Circumplanetary Material



Observations of systems such as PDS 70 provide possible evidence for material surrounding young planets.



Such material may eventually form moons.



\## James Webb Space Telescope



The \[\[James Webb Space Telescope]] can study young stars and disks in infrared wavelengths.



It can investigate:



Disk chemistry



Ices



Dust



Planet-forming regions



Young planetary atmospheres



\## Future Observations



Future telescopes aim to resolve smaller structures within planet-forming disks and detect younger, lower-mass planets.



These observations may help determine:



Where planets form



How quickly they form



How migration begins



How atmospheres are acquired



\## Computer Simulations



Planet formation is studied extensively through numerical simulations.



Models can follow:



Dust dynamics



Gas flow



Gravitational interactions



Collisions



Migration



Atmospheric growth



No single simulation can yet capture all physical scales simultaneously.



\## N-Body Simulations



N-body simulations calculate gravitational interactions among many objects.



They are widely used for studying:



Planetesimals



Planetary embryos



Giant impacts



Long-term orbital evolution



\## Hydrodynamic Simulations



Gas-disk evolution and planet-disk interaction are studied using:



Hydrodynamic simulations



These models help investigate:



Migration



Gap opening



Spiral waves



Gas accretion



\## Magnetohydrodynamics



Protoplanetary disks contain ionized material and magnetic fields.



Some disk processes are therefore modeled using:



Magnetohydrodynamics



or:



MHD



\## Turbulence



Disk turbulence can affect:



Dust collisions



Particle settling



Migration



Gas accretion



The exact sources and strength of disk turbulence remain major research questions.



\## Dead Zones



Some disk regions may be weakly ionized and less magnetically turbulent.



These regions have historically been called:



Dead zones



Their actual behavior can be complex and depends on non-ideal MHD effects.



\## Vertical Settling



Dust particles gradually settle toward the disk midplane because of stellar gravity.



Gas turbulence opposes complete settling.



A dense midplane layer can promote particle concentration.



\## Disk Midplane



The disk midplane is the central plane of the flattened disk.



It often contains high concentrations of solids and is a major site of planet formation.



\## Disk Temperature



The disk is heated by:



Stellar radiation



Viscous processes



Accretion



Temperature generally decreases outward but can contain complex local structure.



\## Migration Traps



Disk regions where competing migration torques balance may temporarily halt planetary migration.



These are sometimes called:



Planet traps



Their effectiveness depends on disk evolution.



\## Resonance Capture



Migrating planets approaching one another can become locked into orbital resonances.



Resonance capture is common in theoretical models of compact planetary systems.



\## Dynamical Instability



When resonant configurations break, planets may experience close encounters.



This can produce:



Scattering



Collisions



Ejections



The present architecture of a planetary system may therefore look very different from its original disk-era architecture.



\## Formation Versus Evolution



Planet formation gradually transitions into:



Planetary evolution



There is no exact universal boundary between the two.



Processes such as:



Atmospheric loss



Differentiation



Migration



Tidal evolution



Impacts



continue after the main accretion phase.



\## Evidence From Exoplanets



Thousands of \[\[Exoplanets]] reveal that planetary systems can have architectures very different from the Solar System.



Examples include:



Hot Jupiters



Compact systems



Super-Earths



Sub-Neptunes



Highly eccentric giants



Resonant chains



This diversity is one of the strongest constraints on planet formation theory.



\## Solar System as One Example



The Solar System should not be assumed to represent the only normal outcome of planet formation.



Its architecture is one result among many possible evolutionary pathways.



\## Common Misconceptions



\### Planets Form Directly From Gas Clouds



Not usually in the simple sense. A collapsing molecular cloud forms a star and disk first. Most planets then form within the disk.



\### Every Dust Collision Makes a Bigger Object



Incorrect. Collisions can lead to sticking, bouncing, erosion, or fragmentation.



\### Planets Form Where We See Them Today



Not necessarily. \[\[Planetary Migration]] can move planets over large distances.



\### The Asteroid Belt Is a Destroyed Planet



There is no evidence that the present asteroid belt was once a fully formed large planet that exploded. Its material represents remnants that never assembled into a major planet, although collisions among differentiated parent bodies occurred.



\### Giant Planets Are Entirely Gas



Incorrect. Giant planets contain heavier elements and likely have central regions enriched in rock, metal, and ices.



\### All Planets Form at the Same Speed



Incorrect. Formation times depend strongly on location, available solids, disk structure, planet mass, and accretion mechanism.



\### Rocky Planet Formation Ends When the Gas Disk Disappears



Incorrect. Giant impacts and orbital evolution can continue for tens of millions of years afterward.



\### Every Gap in a Protoplanetary Disk Contains a Planet



Incorrect. Planets can create gaps, but other physical and chemical processes can also produce disk structures.



\### Planet Formation Is Fully Understood



Incorrect. Many major questions remain unresolved, including planetesimal formation, giant planet core growth, migration histories, and the origin of common exoplanet architectures.



\## Important Terminology



\*\*Protoplanetary Disk:\*\* Rotating disk of gas and dust surrounding a young star.



\*\*Accretion:\*\* Growth through collection of surrounding material.



\*\*Planetesimal:\*\* Gravitationally bound solid body formed during early planet formation.



\*\*Planetary Embryo:\*\* Large growing body between planetesimal and planet scale.



\*\*Pebble Accretion:\*\* Growth through capture of small gas-coupled solids.



\*\*Streaming Instability:\*\* Concentration of solid particles that can lead to gravitational collapse into planetesimals.



\*\*Snow Line:\*\* Disk region beyond which a volatile compound can condense into solid ice.



\*\*Core Accretion:\*\* Giant planet formation model involving a solid core followed by gas capture.



\*\*Disk Instability:\*\* Formation of massive gas clumps through gravitational fragmentation of a disk.



\*\*Runaway Growth:\*\* Stage where larger planetesimals grow faster than smaller ones.



\*\*Oligarchic Growth:\*\* Stage where several dominant planetary embryos control nearby material.



\*\*Giant Impact:\*\* Collision between planetary-scale bodies during late formation.



\*\*Radial Drift:\*\* Inward movement of solids caused by aerodynamic interaction with disk gas.



\*\*Gravitational Focusing:\*\* Increase in collision probability caused by gravitational attraction.



\## Simplified Planet Formation Sequence



A molecular cloud collapses and forms a young star surrounded by a protoplanetary disk. Dust inside the disk collides and grows into larger aggregates and pebbles. Concentrations of solids produce planetesimals. Gravity allows planetesimals to grow into planetary embryos.



In the inner disk, embryos undergo additional collisions and eventually form rocky planets. In colder outer regions, larger amounts of solid material can help planetary cores form rapidly. Some cores capture enormous amounts of disk gas and become giant planets.



Meanwhile, planets interact gravitationally with the gas disk and with one another. Migration, resonances, collisions, and scattering rearrange their orbits. After the gas disk disappears, rocky planet assembly and long-term dynamical evolution can continue.



The remaining small bodies preserve material left over from this process.



\## Scientific Importance



Planet formation connects astronomy with chemistry, geology, plasma physics, orbital dynamics, and planetary science.



Understanding how planets form helps explain why Earth exists, why the Solar System has its particular architecture, how water and organic material reach rocky worlds, why giant planets differ from terrestrial planets, and why exoplanetary systems show such extraordinary diversity.



\## Open Questions



Major unanswered questions include exactly how micron-sized dust crosses the growth barriers and becomes large planetesimals, how important streaming instability is in different disks, and whether pebble accretion or planetesimal accretion dominates planetary growth.



Scientists are also investigating how quickly Jupiter-like planets form, whether disk instability contributes significantly to giant planet populations, how often planets migrate over large distances, and what caused the specific arrangement of the Solar System.



Other important questions concern why super-Earths and sub-Neptunes are so common around other stars but absent from our Solar System, how frequently planets are ejected into interstellar space, where terrestrial planets obtain their water, and how planet formation changes around low-mass stars, binary stars, and stars with different chemical compositions.



\## Summary



Planet formation begins in protoplanetary disks surrounding young stars. Tiny dust grains grow into aggregates and pebbles, while concentrations of solids eventually produce planetesimals.



Gravity allows planetesimals to grow through runaway and oligarchic growth into planetary embryos. Rocky planets form through further accretion and giant impacts, while giant planets can acquire massive hydrogen-helium envelopes if their cores become large enough before the gas disk disappears.



Modern theories also emphasize pebble accretion, streaming instability, planetary migration, disk substructure, and late dynamical instability.



The final position of a planet may be very different from its birthplace. Interactions among planets, gas, and leftover planetesimals can restructure entire planetary systems.



Asteroids, comets, meteorites, and trans-Neptunian objects preserve remnants from these early processes, while observations of protoplanetary disks and exoplanets show that planet formation can produce an enormous range of planetary systems.



Understanding planet formation is therefore fundamental to explaining the origin of the Solar System, the formation of Earth, and the diversity of planets throughout the galaxy.
