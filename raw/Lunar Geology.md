\# Lunar Geology



\## Overview



Lunar geology is the study of the Moon's:



\- Surface

\- Crust

\- Mantle

\- Interior

\- Rocks

\- Minerals

\- Impact history

\- Volcanic history

\- Tectonic features

\- Regolith

\- Geological evolution



The Moon preserves an exceptionally ancient geological record because it has:



\- No active plate tectonics

\- No substantial atmosphere

\- No liquid-water erosion

\- Very low current geological activity



As a result, many features formed billions of years ago remain visible today.



\## Importance of Lunar Geology



Studying lunar geology helps scientists understand:



\- Early Solar System history

\- Impact rates

\- Planetary differentiation

\- Volcanism on small rocky bodies

\- Crust formation

\- Interior evolution

\- Earth-Moon history



The Moon acts as a geological archive of the inner Solar System.



\## Origin of the Moon



The leading model for the Moon's formation is the:



Giant Impact Hypothesis



According to this model, the young Earth was struck by a large planetary body, often called:



Theia



Material ejected from the collision eventually formed the Moon.



\## Giant Impact Hypothesis



A simplified sequence is:



Early Earth forms



↓



Large protoplanet collides with Earth



↓



Rocky debris is ejected into orbit



↓



Debris forms a disk around Earth



↓



Material accretes



↓



Moon forms



The exact details remain an active area of research.



\## Lunar Differentiation



Soon after formation, the Moon became hot enough for much of its interior to melt.



This allowed materials to separate by density.



The process is called:



Planetary differentiation



\## Lunar Magma Ocean



A major early stage of lunar evolution was the:



Lunar Magma Ocean



The Moon may once have possessed a deep global or near-global layer of molten rock.



\## Magma Ocean Crystallization



As the magma ocean cooled:



Dense minerals tended to sink.



Lighter minerals rose toward the surface.



This process helped create the Moon's crust and mantle.



\## Plagioclase Flotation



A light mineral called:



Plagioclase feldspar



was relatively buoyant in the lunar magma ocean.



Crystals floated upward and accumulated near the surface.



This contributed to formation of the ancient lunar highland crust.



\## Anorthosite



Much of the lunar highland crust is rich in:



Anorthosite



Anorthosite is a rock composed mainly of:



Plagioclase feldspar



\## Lunar Highlands



The lunar highlands are the Moon's:



\- Bright

\- Ancient

\- Heavily cratered



terrain.



They are dominated by ancient crustal rocks.



\## Highlands Age



Much of the lunar highland crust formed more than:



4 billion years ago



This makes it some of the oldest preserved crustal material in the Solar System.



\## Lunar Maria



The darker regions visible from Earth are called:



Lunar maria



Singular:



Mare



The word means:



Seas



but lunar maria contain no liquid water.



\## What Lunar Maria Are



Lunar maria are vast plains of:



Basaltic volcanic rock



They formed when lava erupted onto the surface and filled large impact basins.



\## Basalt



Basalt is a dark volcanic rock formed from rapidly cooled lava.



Lunar mare basalts are generally richer in:



\- Iron

\- Magnesium



than the bright highland crust.



\## Why Maria Are Dark



Mare basalts have lower reflectivity than the plagioclase-rich highlands.



Therefore, they appear darker.



\## Distribution of Maria



Most major maria are located on the:



Near side of the Moon



The far side contains far less mare basalt.



\## Near Side



The near side is the lunar hemisphere that generally faces Earth.



It contains large maria such as:



\- Mare Imbrium

\- Mare Serenitatis

\- Mare Tranquillitatis

\- Mare Crisium

\- Oceanus Procellarum



\## Far Side



The far side is the hemisphere generally facing away from Earth.



It is dominated by:



\- Heavily cratered highlands

\- Thick crust

\- Relatively little mare basalt



\## Why Near and Far Sides Differ



Possible factors include differences in:



\- Crustal thickness

\- Thermal evolution

\- Impact history

\- Radioactive-element distribution



The exact origin of the asymmetry remains under investigation.



\## Lunar Crust



The Moon has a solid crust above its mantle.



Its average thickness is on the order of:



Tens of kilometers



but varies strongly with location.



\## Far-Side Crust



The far-side crust is generally thicker than the near-side crust.



This may help explain why large lava floods were more common on the near side.



\## Lunar Mantle



Below the crust lies the:



Lunar mantle



It is made mainly of silicate rock rich in minerals such as:



\- Olivine

\- Pyroxene



\## Lunar Core



The Moon has a relatively small metallic core compared with Earth.



It contains primarily:



\- Iron

\- Nickel



with lighter elements likely present.



\## Core Size



The lunar core is much smaller relative to the Moon's total radius than Earth's core is relative to Earth.



\## Interior Layers



A simplified lunar interior is:



Crust



↓



Mantle



↓



Partially molten or low-velocity region in parts of deep interior



↓



Metallic core



\## Moonquakes



The Moon experiences:



Moonquakes



They are seismic events analogous in some ways to earthquakes.



\## Types of Moonquakes



Important categories include:



\- Deep moonquakes

\- Shallow moonquakes

\- Thermal moonquakes

\- Meteorite impact events



\## Deep Moonquakes



Deep moonquakes occur hundreds of kilometers below the surface.



Many are related to:



Tidal forces from Earth



\## Shallow Moonquakes



Shallow moonquakes occur closer to the surface.



Some can be relatively strong compared with other lunar seismic events.



\## Thermal Moonquakes



Thermal moonquakes are caused by expansion and contraction of the lunar surface as temperature changes between:



\- Lunar day

\- Lunar night



\## Impact Seismic Events



Meteorite impacts also generate seismic waves through the Moon.



\## Apollo Seismometers



Apollo astronauts placed seismometers on the lunar surface.



These instruments provided key information about:



\- Moonquakes

\- Interior structure

\- Crust

\- Mantle

\- Core



\## Lunar Surface Temperature



The Moon experiences extreme temperature differences because it lacks a thick atmosphere.



Typical surface temperatures can range from:



Very hot during lunar daytime



to:



Extremely cold during lunar night



\## Lunar Day



A complete cycle from one sunrise to the next at a given lunar location lasts about:



29.5 Earth days



\## Long Heating and Cooling



Because lunar day and night each last roughly two Earth weeks, the surface experiences prolonged heating and cooling.



\## Impact Craters



Impact craters are the dominant geological landforms on the Moon.



They are created when:



\- Asteroids

\- Meteoroids

\- Comets



strike the lunar surface at high speed.



\## Why the Moon Has So Many Craters



The Moon preserves craters extremely well because it lacks:



\- Rain

\- Rivers

\- Oceans

\- Strong winds

\- Plate tectonics



These processes erase craters on Earth much more efficiently.



\## Crater Formation



A simplified crater-forming sequence is:



Impactor strikes surface



↓



Shock wave compresses rock



↓



Material is excavated



↓



Crater cavity forms



↓



Ejecta spreads outward



↓



Crater walls and floor adjust



\## Simple Craters



Smaller lunar impact craters tend to have:



\- Bowl-shaped interiors

\- Relatively smooth walls

\- Simple geometry



\## Complex Craters



Larger craters may contain:



\- Central peaks

\- Terraced walls

\- Flat floors

\- Landslides



\## Central Peaks



Central peaks form when deeply compressed rock rebounds upward after a large impact.



\## Peak-Ring Basins



Very large impact structures can contain rings of mountains around their centers.



These are called:



Peak-ring basins



\## Multi-Ring Basins



Some enormous lunar impacts created:



Multiple concentric mountain rings



These structures are called:



Multi-ring basins



\## Impact Basins



Impact basins are extremely large impact structures.



Many maria formed inside ancient impact basins.



\## South Pole-Aitken Basin



The:



South Pole-Aitken Basin



is one of the largest and oldest known impact structures in the Solar System.



It lies mostly on the lunar far side.



\## South Pole-Aitken Basin Importance



It is important because the impact may have excavated material from deep within the Moon.



Studying it can reveal information about:



\- Lower crust

\- Mantle

\- Early lunar history



\## Imbrium Basin



The Imbrium Basin is a major near-side impact basin.



It was later partly filled with basaltic lava to create:



Mare Imbrium



\## Orientale Basin



The Orientale Basin is a spectacular example of a multi-ring impact basin.



It is located near the Moon's western limb as viewed from Earth.



\## Crater Rays



Some young craters are surrounded by bright streaks called:



Rays



These consist of material ejected during the impact.



\## Tycho Crater



Tycho is a relatively young prominent crater famous for its extensive bright ray system.



\## Copernicus Crater



Copernicus is another prominent young lunar crater with:



\- Central peaks

\- Terraced walls

\- Bright ejecta



\## Ejecta



Ejecta is material thrown outward from an impact crater.



It can include:



\- Rock fragments

\- Melted material

\- Dust



\## Ejecta Blanket



Material deposited around a crater forms an:



Ejecta blanket



\## Secondary Craters



Large blocks ejected from a primary crater can strike the surface elsewhere.



These impacts form:



Secondary craters



\## Crater Chains



Secondary impacts can create lines or clusters of craters called:



Crater chains



\## Impact Melt



The enormous energy of an impact can melt lunar rock.



This creates:



Impact melt



\## Impact Melt Deposits



Impact melt may form:



\- Pools

\- Sheets

\- Flows



on crater floors and nearby terrain.



\## Impact Breccia



Impact events shatter and recombine rock fragments.



The resulting rock is called:



Breccia



\## Lunar Breccias



Many Apollo samples are breccias formed by repeated impacts.



They can contain fragments of much older rocks.



\## Regolith



The loose layer covering much of the Moon is called:



Lunar regolith



It consists of:



\- Dust

\- Rock fragments

\- Impact debris

\- Glass particles

\- Mineral grains



\## Regolith Formation



Regolith develops through billions of years of:



\- Meteorite impacts

\- Micrometeorite impacts

\- Solar-wind exposure

\- Thermal cycling



\## Gardening



Continuous small impacts overturn and mix lunar surface material.



This process is called:



Impact gardening



\## Regolith Thickness



Regolith thickness varies.



It is generally:



\- Several meters in many mare regions

\- Thicker in ancient highland regions



\## Lunar Dust



Lunar dust consists of extremely fine particles.



It is often:



\- Sharp

\- Abrasive

\- Electrostatic



because particles have not been rounded by water or wind.



\## Dust Hazards



Lunar dust can affect:



\- Space suits

\- Seals

\- Machinery

\- Optical instruments

\- Human health



\## Agglutinates



Micrometeorite impacts can partially melt lunar soil grains.



The melted material combines particles into structures called:



Agglutinates



\## Glass



Lunar regolith contains glass created by:



\- Impact melting

\- Volcanic eruptions



\## Impact Glass



High-energy impacts can melt surface material rapidly.



Rapid cooling forms:



Impact glass



\## Volcanic Glass



Some lunar volcanic eruptions produced glass beads.



These are evidence of explosive volcanic activity.



\## Orange Soil



Apollo 17 astronauts discovered orange-colored volcanic glass deposits near:



Shorty Crater



These grains formed during ancient volcanic eruptions.



\## Pyroclastic Deposits



Explosive volcanism can produce:



Pyroclastic deposits



made of volcanic fragments and glass beads.



\## Lunar Volcanism



The Moon once experienced extensive volcanic activity.



Most major volcanism occurred billions of years ago.



\## Mare Volcanism



The best-known lunar volcanism produced the basaltic maria.



A simplified sequence is:



Large impact basin forms



↓



Interior remains hot



↓



Mantle partially melts



↓



Magma rises through fractures



↓



Basaltic lava floods basin



↓



Mare plain forms



\## Timing of Mare Volcanism



Most major mare volcanism occurred roughly between:



About 4 billion and 1 billion years ago



although some volcanic deposits may be younger.



\## Flood Basalts



Many maria formed through repeated large lava flows.



These are analogous in some ways to:



Flood basalt provinces on Earth



\## Lava Flow Features



Lunar maria contain geological features such as:



\- Flow fronts

\- Lava channels

\- Rilles

\- Volcanic domes



\## Rilles



Rilles are long narrow depressions on the Moon.



They can have different origins.



Types include:



\- Sinuous rilles

\- Straight rilles

\- Arcuate rilles



\## Sinuous Rilles



Sinuous rilles are winding channels resembling dry river valleys.



However, they were generally formed by:



Lava flow



rather than liquid water.



\## Hadley Rille



Hadley Rille is a famous sinuous rille explored during:



Apollo 15



It is believed to have formed through ancient lava flow processes.



\## Straight Rilles



Straight rilles are more linear features.



Many formed through:



Tectonic extension



and faulting.



\## Arcuate Rilles



Arcuate rilles are curved structures often found near mare basin margins.



They may result from:



Crustal stresses associated with mare loading and subsidence.



\## Lava Tubes



Lava can form underground channels called:



Lava tubes



When lava drains away, empty tunnels may remain.



\## Lunar Lava Tubes



Lunar lava tubes are scientifically and practically important because they may:



\- Preserve volcanic history

\- Protect against radiation

\- Protect against micrometeorites

\- Provide stable temperatures



They have been proposed as possible locations for future lunar habitats.



\## Skylights



When part of a lava-tube roof collapses, an opening can form.



These openings are called:



Skylights



\## Lunar Pits



Several deep lunar pits may provide access to underground lava tubes or caves.



\## Volcanic Domes



Lunar domes are broad low volcanic features.



They may form from relatively viscous lava eruptions.



\## Marius Hills



The:



Marius Hills



region contains numerous volcanic domes and other volcanic features.



\## Gruithuisen Domes



The:



Gruithuisen Domes



are unusually bright volcanic domes with compositions different from typical mare basalt.



\## Silicic Volcanism



Most lunar volcanism was basaltic.



However, some regions show evidence for more silica-rich volcanism.



This indicates greater diversity in lunar magmatic processes.



\## KREEP



KREEP is a geochemical component enriched in:



\- Potassium

\- Rare Earth Elements

\- Phosphorus



The name comes from:



K = Potassium



REE = Rare Earth Elements



P = Phosphorus



\## Origin of KREEP



As the lunar magma ocean crystallized, many incompatible elements remained concentrated in the final liquid.



This material became enriched in:



\- Potassium

\- Rare earth elements

\- Phosphorus

\- Uranium

\- Thorium



\## Procellarum KREEP Terrane



Much of the Moon's KREEP-rich material is concentrated on the near side in the:



Procellarum KREEP Terrane



or:



PKT



\## Importance of KREEP



KREEP contains radioactive heat-producing elements such as:



\- Uranium

\- Thorium

\- Potassium



These may have prolonged volcanic activity in parts of the Moon.



\## Thorium Mapping



Orbital spacecraft have mapped thorium abundance using:



Gamma-ray spectroscopy



This helps identify KREEP-rich regions.



\## Lunar Mineralogy



Important lunar minerals include:



\- Plagioclase

\- Pyroxene

\- Olivine

\- Ilmenite



\## Plagioclase



Plagioclase dominates much of the ancient highland crust.



\## Pyroxene



Pyroxene is common in lunar basalts and mantle-derived materials.



\## Olivine



Olivine is common in lunar mantle rocks and some volcanic materials.



\## Ilmenite



Ilmenite is an iron-titanium oxide mineral.



Some mare basalts contain high concentrations of:



Titanium-rich ilmenite



\## Titanium-Rich Basalts



Some mare basalts contain unusually high titanium abundances.



Remote sensing can identify these compositional differences.



\## Lunar Rock Types



Important lunar rock groups include:



\- Anorthosite

\- Basalt

\- Breccia

\- Impact melt rock

\- KREEP-rich rocks



\## Ferroan Anorthosite



Ferroan anorthosite is a major ancient lunar crustal rock type.



It is rich in plagioclase.



\## Mare Basalt



Mare basalt represents volcanic lava that erupted from the lunar mantle.



\## Breccia



Breccias contain fragments of multiple rocks welded together by impact processes.



\## Sample Return



Lunar samples returned to Earth have been essential for determining:



\- Absolute ages

\- Mineralogy

\- Chemistry

\- Volcanic history

\- Impact chronology



\## Apollo Samples



Apollo astronauts returned approximately:



382 kilograms



of lunar material to Earth.



\## Luna Samples



The Soviet Luna robotic missions also returned lunar soil and rock samples.



\## Chang'e Samples



China's Chang'e missions have returned additional lunar material.



These samples expand knowledge beyond Apollo and Luna landing regions.



\## Chang'e 5



Chang'e 5 returned samples from:



Oceanus Procellarum



These samples include relatively young lunar basalt.



\## Chang'e 5 Age



Some Chang'e 5 basalts are approximately:



2 billion years old



showing that lunar volcanism continued much later than some older sample records indicated.



\## Chang'e 6



Chang'e 6 returned samples from the:



Lunar far side



including material from the South Pole-Aitken Basin region.



Far-side sample return is especially valuable because previous samples came almost entirely from the near side.



\## Radiometric Dating



Scientists determine lunar rock ages using radioactive isotopes.



Methods include systems involving:



\- Uranium-lead

\- Rubidium-strontium

\- Samarium-neodymium

\- Argon isotopes



\## Impact Crater Dating



The Moon provides a key calibration for:



Crater-counting chronology



\## Crater Counting



Older surfaces generally accumulate more impact craters.



Scientists estimate relative surface ages by counting:



\- Craters

\- Crater sizes



\## Absolute Age Calibration



Lunar samples with radiometrically measured ages can be linked to crater densities at their collection sites.



This allows crater counts to estimate ages elsewhere.



\## Importance for Other Planets



The lunar crater chronology is used to estimate surface ages on:



\- Mercury

\- Mars

\- Other rocky bodies



although impact histories differ between worlds.



\## Late Heavy Bombardment



Some interpretations of lunar samples suggested a period of unusually intense impacts around:



3.9 billion years ago



This proposed event is called:



Late Heavy Bombardment



or:



LHB



\## Debate About Late Heavy Bombardment



Whether there was a sharp impact spike or a more gradual decline in bombardment remains debated.



The lunar impact record is central to resolving this question.



\## Tectonics



The Moon does not have Earth-style plate tectonics.



However, it does possess tectonic features produced by:



\- Cooling

\- Contraction

\- Basin loading

\- Crustal stresses

\- Tidal forces



\## Faults



Lunar crust contains numerous:



Faults



formed when rocks fractured and moved.



\## Normal Faults



Extension can pull the crust apart and produce:



Normal faults



Some rilles formed this way.



\## Thrust Faults



Compression can push one section of crust over another.



This produces:



Thrust faults



\## Lobate Scarps



Lobate scarps are small cliff-like landforms interpreted as:



Thrust faults



They form as the Moon contracts.



\## Lunar Contraction



The Moon has cooled gradually over billions of years.



Cooling causes its interior to shrink slightly.



This generates compressional stress.



\## Young Lobate Scarps



Some lobate scarps appear geologically young.



This indicates that the Moon experienced tectonic activity relatively recently.



\## Moonquakes and Scarps



Shallow moonquakes may be associated with active or recently active thrust faults.



\## Wrinkle Ridges



Wrinkle ridges are long low ridges common across lunar maria.



They formed mainly through compressional deformation of basaltic plains.



\## Mare Loading



Large amounts of basalt added mass to impact basins.



This changed crustal stresses and contributed to formation of:



\- Wrinkle ridges

\- Rilles

\- Faults



\## Graben



A graben is a down-dropped block of crust bounded by normal faults.



Some straight lunar rilles are graben.



\## Horst



A raised crustal block bounded by faults is called a:



Horst



\## Tectonic Stress Sources



Lunar tectonic stresses can arise from:



\- Global cooling

\- Tidal forces

\- Impact basin formation

\- Volcanic loading

\- Local crustal movement



\## Tidal Forces



Earth's gravity produces tidal stresses in the Moon.



These stresses can contribute to:



\- Deep moonquakes

\- Fault activity



\## Lunar Mascons



Mascon means:



Mass concentration



Mascons are regions of unusually strong gravitational attraction associated especially with large impact basins.



\## Discovery of Mascons



Tracking early lunar spacecraft revealed gravitational anomalies over major maria.



These became known as:



Mascons



\## Why Mascons Form



Factors include:



\- Dense basaltic lava

\- Uplifted mantle beneath impact basins

\- Basin structure



\## Mascons and Spacecraft



Mascons can significantly perturb lunar spacecraft orbits.



Mission planners must account for the Moon's uneven gravity field.



\## GRAIL Mission



NASA's:



Gravity Recovery and Interior Laboratory



or:



GRAIL



mission mapped the Moon's gravity field in extraordinary detail.



\## GRAIL Findings



GRAIL improved understanding of:



\- Crustal thickness

\- Impact basins

\- Interior structure

\- Tectonics

\- Mascons



\## Lunar Gravity



The Moon's surface gravity is approximately:



1.62 m/s²



This is about:



One-sixth of Earth's surface gravity.



\## Low Gravity and Geology



Lower gravity affects:



\- Crater dimensions

\- Ejecta travel

\- Mountain stability

\- Volcanic eruption behavior



\## Lunar Mountains



Many lunar mountains were formed not by plate tectonics but by:



\- Giant impacts

\- Basin ring formation

\- Crustal uplift



\## Montes Apenninus



The:



Montes Apenninus



mountain range forms part of the rim of the Imbrium impact basin.



\## Lunar Valleys



Lunar valleys can form through:



\- Impact excavation

\- Tectonic faulting

\- Volcanic processes



\## Vallis Schröteri



Vallis Schröteri is the largest known sinuous rille on the Moon.



It likely formed through volcanic processes.



\## Lunar Swirls



Lunar swirls are bright, winding surface patterns.



Examples include:



Reiner Gamma



\## Swirl Formation



Lunar swirls are associated with localized:



Crustal magnetic anomalies



Their exact formation mechanism remains debated.



\## Magnetic Shielding Hypothesis



One idea is that local magnetic fields partially deflect solar wind.



This may reduce space weathering and preserve brighter surface material.



\## Lunar Magnetic Field



The Moon does not possess a strong global magnetic field today.



However, lunar rocks preserve evidence that the Moon once had a stronger internal magnetic field.



\## Ancient Lunar Dynamo



The Moon may once have generated a magnetic field through a:



Core dynamo



The strength, timing, and duration of this ancient dynamo remain active research topics.



\## Remanent Magnetism



Ancient lunar rocks can retain magnetic signatures.



This is called:



Remanent magnetization



\## Crustal Magnetic Anomalies



Localized regions of magnetized lunar crust remain today.



These create small magnetic environments.



\## Mini-Magnetospheres



Some strong crustal anomalies can partially deflect the solar wind.



These localized plasma structures are sometimes described as:



Mini-magnetospheres



\## Space Weathering



The lunar surface is continuously modified by:



\- Solar wind

\- Micrometeorites

\- Cosmic radiation



This process is called:



Space weathering



\## Effects of Space Weathering



Space weathering can make lunar soil:



\- Darker

\- Redder in spectral slope

\- Less strongly diagnostic of original minerals



\## Nanophase Iron



Space weathering can create extremely small metallic iron particles called:



Nanophase iron



These strongly influence optical properties of lunar soil.



\## Mature Regolith



Older exposed soil experiences more space weathering and is called:



Mature regolith



\## Immature Regolith



Freshly excavated material has experienced less space weathering.



It is called:



Immature regolith



\## Crater Rays and Fresh Material



Bright rays around young craters often consist of relatively fresh material excavated from below the weathered surface.



Over time, space weathering darkens these rays.



\## Lunar Water



The Moon was once considered almost completely dry.



Modern measurements show that water exists in several forms.



\## Water Ice



Water ice exists in some permanently shadowed regions near the lunar poles.



\## Permanently Shadowed Regions



Some deep polar craters receive little or no direct sunlight.



They are called:



Permanently Shadowed Regions



or:



PSRs



\## Cold Traps



Temperatures inside some PSRs are low enough to preserve volatile materials for very long periods.



These regions are called:



Cold traps



\## Polar Water Sources



Possible sources of lunar water include:



\- Comets

\- Water-rich asteroids

\- Solar-wind interactions

\- Ancient lunar interior



\## Solar-Wind Hydrogen



Solar-wind protons can interact with oxygen in lunar minerals.



These reactions may form:



\- Hydroxyl

\- Water-related molecules



\## Hydroxyl



Hydroxyl consists of:



OH



and has been detected or inferred on parts of the lunar surface.



\## Water in Volcanic Glass



Some lunar volcanic glass contains measurable water.



This shows that parts of the lunar interior were not completely dry.



\## Lunar Volatiles



Other volatile compounds may exist in polar cold traps.



Possible materials include compounds containing:



\- Carbon

\- Nitrogen

\- Sulfur

\- Hydrogen



\## Polar Geology



The lunar poles are major targets for scientific exploration because they contain:



\- Ancient terrain

\- Shadowed craters

\- Volatile deposits

\- Unusual illumination conditions



\## South Pole



The lunar south polar region is especially important for current exploration plans.



It includes terrain associated with the enormous:



South Pole-Aitken Basin



\## Lunar Resources



Potential lunar geological resources include:



\- Water ice

\- Oxygen bound in minerals

\- Metals

\- Silicates



\## Oxygen in Lunar Rocks



Lunar minerals contain large amounts of oxygen chemically bound to elements such as:



\- Silicon

\- Aluminum

\- Iron

\- Magnesium



Extracting this oxygen would require industrial processing.



\## Helium-3



Solar wind implants helium into lunar regolith, including small quantities of:



Helium-3



It has sometimes been proposed as a future fusion resource.



Practical economic extraction remains highly speculative.



\## Geological Timescale



The lunar geological timescale divides lunar history into major periods.



Important divisions include:



\- Pre-Nectarian

\- Nectarian

\- Imbrian

\- Eratosthenian

\- Copernican



\## Pre-Nectarian Period



The Pre-Nectarian covers the earliest lunar history before formation of the Nectaris Basin.



It includes:



\- Early crust formation

\- Very ancient impact events



\## Nectarian Period



The Nectarian period begins with formation of the:



Nectaris Basin



and includes major early impact events.



\## Imbrian Period



The Imbrian period includes:



\- Formation of Imbrium Basin

\- Extensive mare volcanism



It is divided into:



\- Early Imbrian

\- Late Imbrian



\## Eratosthenian Period



The Eratosthenian period represents a later stage in which impact rates and volcanic activity had declined substantially.



\## Copernican Period



The Copernican is the youngest major lunar geological period.



It is named after:



Copernicus Crater



Young bright-rayed craters are characteristic features.



\## Relative Dating



Lunar geological units can be relatively dated using:



\- Crater density

\- Superposition

\- Cross-cutting relationships

\- Ejecta relationships



\## Principle of Superposition



If one geological unit lies on top of another:



The upper unit is generally younger.



\## Cross-Cutting Relationships



A feature cutting across another geological structure is usually:



Younger than the feature it cuts.



\## Crater Degradation



Older craters become:



\- More eroded by later impacts

\- Less sharply defined

\- More filled with regolith and ejecta



\## Saturation



Extremely old lunar surfaces can accumulate so many craters that new impacts destroy older ones at approximately the same rate they create new visible craters.



This is called:



Crater saturation



\## Regolith Breccias



Regolith can become compacted and lithified by impacts.



This forms:



Regolith breccias



\## Megaregolith



Below the fine surface regolith lies a much thicker layer of heavily fractured rock produced by ancient large impacts.



This is called:



Megaregolith



\## Megaregolith Thickness



The megaregolith may extend:



Kilometers



into the lunar crust in heavily impacted regions.



\## Impact Fracturing



Repeated large impacts extensively fractured the lunar crust.



This influences:



\- Seismic-wave propagation

\- Crustal porosity

\- Thermal evolution



\## Lunar Crustal Porosity



GRAIL observations indicate significant porosity in parts of the lunar crust.



Much of this was produced by:



Impact fracturing



\## Heat Flow



Apollo missions measured heat escaping from the lunar interior.



This provided information about:



\- Radioactive heating

\- Interior temperature

\- Thermal evolution



\## Lunar Heat-Producing Elements



Important radioactive heat-producing elements include:



\- Uranium

\- Thorium

\- Potassium



They are concentrated in some KREEP-rich regions.



\## Lunar Cooling



The Moon has gradually lost internal heat over billions of years.



As it cooled:



\- Major volcanism declined

\- Interior convection weakened

\- Global contraction occurred



\## Present Geological Activity



The Moon is much less geologically active than Earth.



However, it is not completely inactive.



Present processes include:



\- Moonquakes

\- Impact cratering

\- Regolith gardening

\- Thermal fracturing

\- Possible tectonic movement



\## Recent Volcanism Debate



Some small lunar features have been proposed as evidence for geologically recent volcanic activity.



The timing and interpretation of some of these features remain debated.



\## Irregular Mare Patches

\## Ina

\## Transient Lunar Phenomena



Historical observers have occasionally reported short-lived changes in lunar appearance called:



Transient Lunar Phenomena

There is no strong evidence for widespread active volcanism today.



\## Thermal Fracturing



Large temperature cycles can gradually break lunar surface rocks apart.



This process contributes to regolith formation.



\## Micrometeorite Bombardment

\- Produce small craters

\- Melt grains

\- Modify regolith



\## Current Impact Flashes

\## No Atmospheric Erosion



The Moon lacks substantial:



\- Wind

\- Rain

\- Rivers



Therefore geological features are not weathered in the same way as on Earth.



\## No Plate Tectonics



The Moon has no present Earth-like system of moving lithospheric plates.



This is one major reason ancient crust remains preserved.



\## Earth Versus Moon Geology

\- Active plate tectonics

\- Atmosphere

\- Oceans

\- Strong erosion

\- Extensive recycling of crust



Moon:



\- No active plate tectonics

\- Negligible atmosphere

\- No surface oceans

\- Impact-dominated geology

\- Ancient surfaces preserved



\## Moon Versus Mercury

\## Moon Versus Mars

\## Moon Versus Venus



Venus has:



\- Thick atmosphere

\- Extensive volcanism

\- Strong resurfacing



The Moon preserves far older surfaces.

\## Common Misconceptions



\### The Lunar Maria Are Oceans



Incorrect.



They are basaltic lava plains.



\### The Moon Has No Geological History



Incorrect.



It experienced:



\- Differentiation

\- Magma-ocean crystallization

\- Giant impacts

\- Extensive volcanism

\- Tectonic deformation



\### Every Lunar Crater Is Volcanic



Incorrect.



Most lunar craters were produced by impacts.



\### The Moon Is Completely Geologically Dead



Incorrect.



It still experiences:



\- Moonquakes

\- Impacts

\- Thermal fracturing

\- Small-scale tectonic processes



\### The Moon Has No Water



Incorrect.



Water or hydroxyl exists in:



\- Polar cold traps

\- Volcanic materials

\- Surface minerals



\### The Far Side Is Always Dark



Incorrect.



The far side receives sunlight just like the near side.



It is called the far side because it generally faces away from Earth.



\### Dark Maria Are Younger Than Every Bright Surface



Not always in a simple sense.



Maria are generally younger than the ancient highlands they fill, but individual geological units have different ages.



\### Lunar Mountains Formed Like the Himalayas



Incorrect.



Many lunar mountain ranges are impact-basin structures rather than plate-tectonic collision ranges.



\### There Is No Tectonic Activity on the Moon



Incorrect.



The Moon has faults, scarps, rilles, and evidence of contraction.



\### The Moon Has a Strong Global Magnetic Field Today



Incorrect.



It has localized crustal magnetic anomalies but no strong present global field.



\## Important Terminology



\### Lunar Geology



Study of the Moon's rocks, surface, interior, and geological evolution.



\### Lunar Magma Ocean



Early molten layer that helped differentiate the Moon.



\### Anorthosite



Plagioclase-rich rock forming much of the lunar highland crust.



\### Lunar Highlands



Bright, ancient, heavily cratered terrain.



\### Lunar Maria



Dark basaltic volcanic plains.



\### Regolith



Loose layer of fragmented material covering the lunar surface.



\### Megaregolith



Deep fractured layer beneath the surface regolith.



\### Breccia



Rock made from fragments joined by impact processes.



\### Rille



Long narrow lunar depression formed by volcanic or tectonic processes.



\### Mascon



Large gravitational mass concentration, often associated with impact basins.



\### KREEP



Material enriched in potassium, rare earth elements, and phosphorus.



\### Lobate Scarp



Small thrust-fault cliff produced partly by lunar contraction.



\### Wrinkle Ridge



Compressional ridge commonly found in lunar maria.



\### Space Weathering



Surface alteration caused by solar wind, radiation, and micrometeorites.



\### Permanently Shadowed Region



Polar location receiving little or no direct sunlight.



\### Cold Trap



Very cold region capable of preserving volatile materials.

\## Summary



Lunar geology records more than four billion years of Solar System history.



The Moon likely formed after a giant impact involving the early Earth.



Soon afterward, a:



Lunar magma ocean



allowed the Moon to differentiate.



Light plagioclase floated upward and helped form the ancient:



Anorthositic highland crust.



Later enormous impacts created:



Impact basins



Many near-side basins were subsequently filled by basaltic lava, producing the dark:



Lunar maria



The Moon's surface is dominated by:



\- Impact craters

\- Regolith

\- Basalt plains

\- Ancient highlands

\- Faults

\- Rilles



The Moon experienced extensive volcanism but lacks present Earth-like plate tectonics.



Its gradual cooling produced:



\- Contraction

\- Thrust faults

\- Lobate scarps

\- Moonquakes



Billions of years of micrometeorite impacts and solar-wind exposure created the:



Lunar regolith



and caused:



Space weathering.



Modern exploration has also shown that the Moon contains:



\- Polar water ice

\- Surface hydroxyl

\- Water in some volcanic materials



Lunar samples from Apollo, Luna, and Chang'e missions provide direct evidence about:



\- Crust formation

\- Volcanism

\- Impact history

\- Moon formation



Because geological recycling is limited, the Moon preserves an ancient record that has largely been erased from Earth.



Understanding lunar geology is therefore central to planetary science, Solar System chronology, future lunar exploration, and studies of the origin of the Earth-Moon system.
